You can't die. They can.

Quantum Immortality

In 1957 the physicist Hugh Everett proposed a way of looking at quantum mechanics. Every time either of two things can happen, the world splits into several versions, and all of them really exist. This is called the many-worlds interpretation.

A thought experiment follows from it. Suppose you're in a situation where in one version of the world you die, and in another you survive by a miracle. From the inside you can only end up in the second: where you no longer exist, there's nothing for you to feel. You will never see your own death. That is quantum immortality.

There's no way to test it. Only the one who survived gets the proof, and he can't pass it on to anyone else. From the outside it always looks like ordinary luck.

One of Andrei Samokhin's Lives

In the main game, "The One Who Stayed," Andrei drowns in Black Backwater at seven and survives. Since then he hears the Choir: the memory of his deaths in neighboring worlds. He has one childhood, and after that his life splits into many branches.

"Three Before Dawn" is one of those branches, a story of its own. Andrei is thirty-four, he lives in Kemerovo, and he can't manage to die. Every time he is killed, he goes on living in the world where he got lucky. There the watchman switched on the pump in time, and the driver managed to brake. People only see how lucky he got. How he died, only Andrei remembers.

This Night

Before dawn three people in the neighborhood are going to die: his childhood friends Vitka and Nika, and Danya, the boy next door. The Choir has told Andrei who and when. He has four hours, the whole neighborhood, and a death he can use. Only every miraculous rescue is seen by someone, and people start talking about Andrei.
