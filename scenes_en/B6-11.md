On the third of May at four-twelve the Choir wakes me and speaks in a single voice. In fifty-four years that has never happened once. They always argued, interrupted, each told his own lies. But now all together, quietly, in one pitch, the way Serafim drew out the first note:

"Go down. Get there in time for the comet."

I lie looking at the ceiling. I remember about the comet. "The twenty-eighth of July 2061, you'll be seventy-two," Galina Ivanovna said at graduation, in her shawl, and we cracked up. Eighty-six days until the twenty-eighth of July. Five hundred kilometers by river to Kemerovo.

I kept the storm jacket and the striped shirt in the creek under the sheepskin back then, and they survived; only the canvas smells of smoke.

Stepan takes me on horseback as far as the first village; from there I'm on my own. The river here is still alive, a mountain river, clear, with rapids. Two weeks later I come out at Mezhdurechensk.

The city is empty. The mines were shut after the Wager, one after another, and people left within a year, the way water leaves a bathtub when you pull the plug. Nine-story blocks stand with open windows; birches grow on the balconies. Red dogs walk the avenue in a pack and look at me without malice, as at one of their own. By one of the panel blocks Granny Nyura, eighty, is watering her vegetable patch from a bucket. She stayed because "where would I go."

"Going down?" she asks. "Everyone's going down. For the comet. You're not the first, Grandpa."

## После A
It takes me three days to lash the raft together. Six barrels, boards from the fence, wire, rope from the fuel depot. Granny Nyura brings me a jar of pickles and sits on a bucket watching me work.

"You'll drown," she says.

"Already have," I say.

On the fourth morning I push the raft into the river. It sits deep in the water, the barrels boom, and the current takes it at once, as if it had been waiting a long time. The dogs run along the bank as far as the bridge and stand there until I disappear around the bend.

## После B
Walking along the sleepers is awkward: the step between them is shorter than you need, and stepping over two is too long. I only get the rhythm by the end of the first day. My legs hum, like in Novosibirsk when I was delivering envelopes. No, I was never there. It was someone else of mine who delivered them; I remember his legs.

I walk twenty kilometers a day, then fifteen. I sleep in empty trackmen's huts; in one I find a calendar from '46 with a girl in a swimsuit. The river is always beside me, on the right, and I talk to it.

## После C
Granny Nyura does have a grandson, thirty, who drives to Novokuznetsk in an old electric UAZ for salt and medicine. I write the note for Nika on the back of Granny Nyura's electricity bill: "Coming down. Will be there by the 27th. Meet me at Krasnaya Gorka. A."

He reads the note, then looks at me.

"You're the old man from the source, then?" he asks. "Mom used to tell about you. In '31 she stood on the ice in Myski when you were there barefoot by the ice hole."

I don't remember his mother. There were three hundred people there. I nod. He puts the note in his breast pocket and buttons it.
