Two and a half years have gone by. For the second year now I've been living in two cities. All week I study in Tomsk, and on Wednesday evenings I climb onto an upper side berth in the night train to Novosibirsk, with somebody's socks right in my face, and from there I go on to Akademgorodok. Lunyov has made an arrangement with the Institute of Physiology, and now my head is being studied for real. Every Thursday they sit me in a chair, put a cap with a hundred and twenty-eight electrodes on me and ask me to "remember something unpleasant." I remember the elevator. Something flashes on the monitor, and the lab assistants exchange looks.

The cap is put on me by a grad student with short hair and a face like a strict vice principal. She adjusts the electrode behind my ear, leans in and says quietly:

"Two hundred and fifty-two, Samokhin. Hold still."

That's when I recognize Nika.

::end

In the evening we stand on the steps of the institute. Squirrels are jumping around the birches by the steps, as cheeky as old women at a front desk. Nika smokes a thin cigarette and asks, without looking at me:

"What are they doing to you, Andrei? Honestly."

## После A
I tell her everything, from the elevator to Granddad's lab under Krasnaya Gorka. Nika hears me out to the end and doesn't interrupt once. Her cigarette burns down to the filter, and she doesn't notice.

"I knew you didn't just climb out that time for nothing," she says at last.

From that evening on we see each other more than just on Thursdays.

## После B
"Measuring," I say. "You're measuring too."

Nika nods, stubs out her cigarette on the trash can and goes back into the building. After that it's all business: Thursday, the cap, a hundred and twenty-eight electrodes, "thank you, Samokhin, you're free to go." Sometimes it seems to me she wants to ask something, but she never does.

## После C
We walk around Akademgorodok until three in the morning, from Morskoy Prospekt all the way to the Ob Sea and back. I tell her about half of it; the rest, it seems, she works out on her own.

At her dorm she says, "You haven't changed, Samokhin. You've just gotten better at lying." And kisses me on the cheek, quickly, as if she were stamping a document.

## После D
In the morning I go to Lunyov and ask him to take Nika off the project. I say it's dangerous to be near me, and I believe it myself.

A week later she's transferred to another lab. She finds out who asked, and after that she nods to me in the hallway like a neighbor on the landing.
