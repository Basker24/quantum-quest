This is my third year working as a sapper. That's what Shadrin calls it; to myself I say "I go in first." Every two or three months they call me with an address. I arrive, go in first, sit down, order tea and wait for Shadrin's people to arrive. So far nothing has ever happened. Shadrin pays anyway, as if it had.
::end

::if F_В408 == ОТКАЗ
I turned him down back then, in '16. Two weeks ago he called me himself. "If you won't go, your Vitka will," he said kindly. "He's my driver now. Drives well." Tonight I go in first for the first time. That's what Shadrin calls it: a sapper.
::end

For the third year I've been living in Altai, in the gambling zone, in an apartment above the casino where I work as head of security. Shadrin runs a third of the tables there, and around here it's hard to find a table Shadrin doesn't run. I'm no kind of head of security, but I live with Lera.

Lera is a croupier, twenty-seven, from Biysk. She has quick hands; she shuffles a deck so the cards rustle like rain on a roof. On the back of her right hand she has a white scar, small, a crescent: a neighbor's German shepherd nipped her when she was five. I kiss that scar every morning; it's our ritual, for luck. Lera laughs and says my luck is inhuman as it is.

::if F_АРХИВ
I keep Granddad's notebook with me, in the glove box. I read a line at a time while I wait. Today I got to "No. 9. 4.XII.63. 04:12. Rock. Came back. The miners call it 'a gut feeling.' Let them."
::end

Tonight it's a café on the highway near Topki. It's called Uyut, Coziness, with a plastic samovar on the roof strung with lights. The Moscow people will be here by half past eleven. Shadrin is sitting in his car in the parking lot with the headlights off; I can see the glow of a cigarette in his window, though he doesn't smoke.

The café is empty. The waitress behind the counter is leafing through a magazine. One table is laid for four, menus in leather folders, artificial carnations in a little vase. The chair I've been told to take has its back to the kitchen. The kitchen door has a round window, like on a ship, and behind the window it's dark.

::if ДАР >= 40
A chill comes from that door into the back of my head, as if the kitchen opened straight onto the street, onto thirty below.
::end

## После B
I go out to the parking lot and lean down to Shadrin's window. I say: "One in the kitchen. With a sawed-off. A kid." Shadrin nods the way you nod to a waiter who's brought the bill, and raises two fingers. Two men get out of the next car.

They don't go into the café; they go around, to the back door. I sit in my car looking at the samovar with its lights. Two pops, short, like a door slamming. The waitress runs out onto the steps without her jacket and stands there hugging herself. Five minutes later the two come back and get into their car. The Moscow people don't arrive at half past eleven. Shadrin leaves first.

In the morning the news says: "Shootout at a roadside café near Topki, two dead." No photo is shown. In the evening Lera kisses my neck.

## После C
I go around the counter and push the kitchen door. The waitress looks up from her magazine and says nothing. In the kitchen it's dark and smells of burnt onions, and by the stove stands a kid in a cook's jacket, about nineteen, with a sawed-off shotgun hanging in his hand. His front tooth is chipped, like his father's. It's Deniska, Shurup's youngest; I used to give him rides in the Bychok when he was eight.

"Deniska," I say quietly. "Go home. Through the back. Now."

He looks at me, at the shotgun, at me again. His lips are trembling. I open the back door for him. He goes out into the snow, no jacket, still holding the shotgun, and runs across the yard to the garages, sinking to his knees. I close the door, go out to the parking lot and tell Shadrin: "It fell through. They're not coming." And the Moscow people really don't come.

Shadrin looks at me from the car for a long time without blinking. "You let him go," he says, not asking. "All right. This time I'll pay for the gut feeling." In the morning Lera's scar is on her right hand, where it belongs. I kiss it longer than usual.

He sticks the envelope in my breast pocket without looking. I could give it back. The envelope rides home with me, and in the evening I count it in the kitchen, twice.

## После D
"I'm not going in," I say through the car window. "Gut feeling."

Shadrin looks at me, then at the café, at the samovar with its lights. He doesn't think long.

"I don't pay for gut feelings," he says. "But I don't argue with them either. Yours runs in the family."

He raises two fingers, and the cars leave the parking lot one after another. The Moscow people meet him somewhere else, a month later, in Novosibirsk, without me. What was in the kitchen at Uyut I'll never know. In the morning I kiss the scar on Lera's right hand, and she says I'm strange.
