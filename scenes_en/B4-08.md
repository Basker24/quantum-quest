A black car comes for me with a driver who doesn't talk. It takes me to Kuznetsky Avenue, to a glass building with a bank on the ground floor and, on the top floor, windows the whole width of the wall, with the Tom below them, gray, autumnal. The office is long, like a school corridor. At the end of the corridor an old man sits at a desk.

He gets up to meet me, and it's hard to call him old. He's nearly seventy, but his back is as straight as a soldier's on parade, and his hand is dry and strong.

"Shadrin," he says. "Leonid Arkadyevich. Sit down, Andrei Nikolayevich. Tea? Mine is good, from Krasnodar. I don't drink any other kind."

"Your grandfather, Tikhon Savelyevich Samokhin," says Shadrin, "was a state criminal. A physicist. A good one. He wanted immortality. Not his own, everyone's, like in a book. Under Krasnaya Gorka, at six hundred forty meters, he had a laboratory; I supervised it for the Committee. In January '86 he killed six people down there, and himself. I ordered the level sealed. So the water wouldn't rise any higher."

He sips his tea.

"And your father is on his list," he says. "Number one hundred seventeen. 'Do not involve. Mine.' You see, I'm honest. I didn't touch your father for thirty years. But I will touch you. You die, Andrei Nikolayevich, and you come back. I saw it in your grandfather. It didn't work for him. It works for you."

He explains what he needs. He has a holding company and open-pit coal mines, and some Moscow people want to take those mines for themselves. Hence meetings that are frightening to go to. "You'll go into them first. If they kill you there, you'll come back and tell me who fired. It's called a sapper. A living sapper. I'll pay you like no one else."

I look at the box. The tea in my glass has gone cold, with a skin on it.

> That was the first time I heard everything about Granddad at once, in one piece, from the man who buried him. For a long time in this life I had no other version.

## После A
"Money up front," I say.

Shadrin laughs, short and pleased, the way you laugh at a good joke you've heard before. He presses a button; the secretary brings an envelope, thick as a brick. He lays it on the box with Granddad's notebooks and pushes it toward me. I take the envelope. The box he takes back and puts away in a cabinet without looking.

Downstairs by the car the driver opens the back door for me. The first time in my life someone has opened a door for me. I get in and think that Granddad probably got into a car like this too, and then they buried him under Krasnaya Gorka.

## После B
"The notebook," I say. "The top one. Money later."

Shadrin looks at me for a long time over his glass, and for the first time in the whole conversation he isn't smiling. Then he unties the string, takes out the top notebook and lays it in front of me.

"It'll be hard to read," he says. "There are formulas. And the handwriting. I've been trying for thirty years."

At home I open it in the kitchen, at night. The first page is ruled into columns, like the ledger by the scales at the scrap yard: "No.," "Date," "Time," "How it ended." In the first row: "No. 1. 12.IV.61. 04:12. Water." Below it, in the "How it ended" column, in even handwriting: "Came back." I sit over that line until dawn.

## После C
"No," I say, and stand up.

Shadrin doesn't stand. He drinks his tea in small sips and looks at the Tom.

"You'll come on your own," he says to the window. "You know who you are now, Andrei Nikolayevich. And knowing is expensive. It's free at first, and then it's expensive."

I walk down the long office as down a school corridor when you've been thrown out of class, and I hear the spoon clinking in the glass behind me. Downstairs there's no car; I go home by minibus. The radio is playing in the minibus, and the news is about some open-pit mines, and I sit looking at my palm with the scar and remembering how many times the blisters on it burst for someone else's money.
