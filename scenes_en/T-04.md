The mine hasn't paid wages for seven months. In February Sonka was born, red and loud like a fire engine, and in April, instead of money, they gave Dad forty cast-iron frying pans. They stand in the front hall in a tower, and everybody trips over it except Sonka, who can't walk yet.

On TV they keep saying the word "barter." At first I thought it was the name of some minister.

In May the miners go and sit down on the railroad tracks. Near Anzhero-Sudzhensk the trains are stuck, nothing is moving on the Trans-Siberian, and outside Kemerovo they're burning fires on our branch line too. Dad comes home at night smelling of smoke and says to Mom:

"Till they pay up, we don't get off the tracks."

"And what are we going to eat?" Mom asks. "Frying pans?"

On Friday at supper he tells me for the first time, "Tomorrow you're coming with me. Let them see we've got kids." Mom says nothing and slices the bread thinner than usual.

But back on Wednesday Vitka made me promise. At the old substation behind the garages there's copper cable lying around that doesn't belong to anybody, and the scrap yard on Shakhtyorov Street pays real cash for copper. "I can't haul it without you," he said. "You promised." Vitka's mom has been out of work for three months, and now he's hungry all the time.

On Friday Nina Petrovna herself comes to our apartment, in her teacher's coat, and talks with Mom in the kitchen for a long time. I'm the only one in class who solved the problem about the two trains, and on Saturday there's a math olympiad in town for the younger grades. In the front hall, next to the frying pans, she bends down to me. "A chance like this comes once, Samokhin."

Mom doesn't ask for anything. She just says she can't haul forty frying pans to the market by herself, and looks at me.

On Saturday morning all four of them are waiting for me.

> Three months later came the default, when the ruble fell through the floor, and the frying pans turned out to be the hardest currency in the house. Mom used to joke that they were the only wages inflation didn't eat.

## После A
I'll remember that night by the fire on the embankment for the rest of my life. The men bang their helmets on the rails, steady as a clock, and somebody is roasting pork fat on a piece of wire. Dad sits me down next to him and lets me hold his helmet, heavy and warm inside.

Toward morning he talks to me like a grown-up. "Remember this, Andryukha. Asking gets you nothing. You stand, and you don't budge." A week later they pay half of what they owe, in real money. The frying pans stay in the front hall.

## После B
Mom and I stand at the Central Market from eight in the morning till dark. By lunchtime I've figured out that the old ladies buy from whoever they feel sorry for, so I start lying that these are our last ones and my dad's down the mine. The part about the mine is true, by the way.

Mom goes red but doesn't say anything. By evening we've sold nine frying pans, and Mom laughs for the first time in a month.

## После C
Vitka and I spend two days sawing through the cable with a hacksaw. On the second day some older guys from Rudnichny show up at the substation and tell us we're idiots: the real copper isn't lying in the grass, it's hanging up there on the pole, thick as your arm. Lyokha, the oldest, climbs the pole with wire cutters and tells me to hold the end of the wire down below while he cuts it.

I take hold of the wire, and the back of my neck goes cold, the way it did that time under the water. I don't know what it is. I just let go and step back, even though Lyokha is swearing at me from up top.

He cuts the wire. There's a bang so loud my ears go deaf for a second, and a white flash. Lyokha falls off the pole backwards and lies in the grass with the wire cutters smoking next to him. The wire I was holding a second ago is thrashing on the ground, spitting sparks. The ambulance takes Lyokha away with his arms black to the elbows. He'll live, but he won't be back at school until winter.

We still turn our own cable in at the scrap yard, for a hundred and forty rubles. That's more than Mom got paid for April. The older guys see us there, slap Vitka on the shoulder, and remember us both.

I give Mom seventy rubles and lie that I found it. She looks at me for a long time and doesn't ask anything.

## После D
At the olympiad in the Pioneer Palace I solve every problem except the last one, the one about the snail climbing the pole, and I come in third. Nina Petrovna buys me a chocolate-covered ice cream bar and says I've got "a head on my shoulders."

Mom hangs the certificate in the kitchen, next to the calendar. Dad looks at it and doesn't say anything, but later I see him showing it to Uncle Tolya.
