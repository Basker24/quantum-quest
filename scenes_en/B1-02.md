Night shift, the third longwall, past four in the morning. Dad's on first shift today, up top, and Mikhalych, Seryoga and I are finishing the end stall by the face, where the shearer can't reach and the coal is cut the old way, with a jackhammer. The jackhammer weighs as much as a good-sized pig and shakes so hard my teeth chatter by themselves. Mikhalych shovels coal onto the conveyor and for the third time this shift tells the joke about the mother-in-law and the parachute. For the third time I laugh, out of respect for his years. Seryoga is fiddling around twenty meters off, by the conveyor drive, singing "Vladimirsky Tsentral," a prison ballad, off-key.

The seam starts to crackle. Quietly at first, like a stove when you've thrown in dry wood, then louder. Fine grit trickles out of the face onto my helmet, and a cold draft brushes my face, though there's nowhere for a draft to come from.

"She's shooting," says Mikhalych, and stops shoveling. "Hold on a minute."

Coal shoots at the Glubokaya every week, and every week nothing happens. The shift ends at six, we haven't made quota, and the foreman has promised to cut the whole crew's bonus. Mikhalych stands with his shovel, listening to the seam with his head cocked to one side like a hen.

My lamp picks out a crack above my jackhammer. Thin, like a hair. I look at it, and it looks back at me, I think.

## После D
I lay the jackhammer down on the floor, carefully, like something asleep, and step back from the face. Then another step. My knees won't bend, and I walk like I'm on stilts.

"Mikhalych," I say. "Let's go have a smoke."

You can't smoke underground, and we both know it. Mikhalych looks at me, at the crack, at me again, throws down his shovel and says, "All right, let's go." We go behind the drive, to Seryoga, and I drag the two of them farther, toward the junction with the drift, though Seryoga swears and digs in his heels; he hasn't finished his verse.

The seam breathes out when we're forty meters away. There's almost no sound, just a shove of air in our backs and a rustling, steady and powerful, like inside a giant hourglass. The coal flows down the face like water, black and gleaming, fills the stall where I was standing, the conveyor, the drive, and stops ten meters from our boots. Then the gas comes, and the three of us run with our self-rescuers on, and I run first, because I know where.

## После E
I yank the self-rescuer out of its case on my belt, rip off the seal and shove the mouthpiece in my mouth before I have time to think how it looks. The rubber is sour, breathing through it is tight. Mikhalych looks at me like I'm crazy.

"What's got into you, Samokhin? She's only shooting."

Without a word I point at the crack, then at his self-rescuer, and tug at his sleeve. Mikhalych has forty years underground. He spits, but he puts it on, and we go to the drive, and from there, grabbing Seryoga, to the junction with the drift. Seryoga swears through his mouthpiece, and it all comes out as mooing.

The seam breathes out when we're forty meters away. First my ears pop, like in the cage, the air turns empty and thick, and the gas detector on Mikhalych's belt starts squealing, thin as a mosquito. Then behind us the coal rustles, steady and powerful, like inside a giant hourglass. We breathe through rubber and run, and I run first.

Up top, Mikhalych sits on the bench outside the bathhouse and can't get his laces untied for a long time. "Gas before coal," he says, to nobody in particular. "I read about that in a book, back in '79. Where'd you read it, Samokhin?" I say I read it in a book too.
