There are six of us in the ward. Semyonych by the window, sixty, who drove a tram along Lenin Avenue for twenty years and then drank himself into the DTs, and now tells everyone about the little green men who ride his tram without paying. Quiet Roma, who doesn't talk at all. Two old men who sleep the whole time. A student from the technical university who counts the ceiling tiles. And me.

It's the third week of the heat. The window is open; beyond the bars sparrows are chirping, though it's still dark. On the ceiling there's a crack shaped like Italy. I've been looking at it since one in the morning.

::if F_ТАБЛЕТКИ
In the daytime the pills keep them behind the door. But by four in the morning the door starts to leak, like ours in the bathroom when the upstairs neighbor forgets the tap again.
::end

At ten past four they all wake up at once.

First a grown man shouts, hoarsely, in my voice, only gone rough: "Guys! Anybody there? I'm buried! Guys, I'm here!" He shouts from the dark, from under the ground, and there's a crunch in his shout. I know who it is. It's me, the one who after graduation went the way Dad went and in July started an apprenticeship at the Glubokaya. At this very minute he's being buried down there.

Under him, more quietly, a little one is whimpering. "Thirsty. Mom, I want a drink." He's seven, and he's in the water.

And a third one coughs and coughs and says through the coughing, "Window. It's stuffy. Open the window." He smells of burnt rubber.

They're the ones asking, but I'm the one who wants it. My mouth is dry, under the sheet I'm wet with sweat, and my stomach cramps for the one under the ground. Getting up and doing what they ask feels as natural as wanting to scratch an itch.

Semyonych by the window is snoring. Roma is lying with his eyes open, looking at me.

## После D
I slide off the bed, crawl on all fours to Semyonych and shake him by the shoulder. He opens his eyes, bleary, and looks at me without any surprise.

"Semyonych," I whisper. "Tell me something. Anything. About the tram."

And he does. About the Tatra whose doors froze in winter, about Galya the conductor, about how in '88 by the Palace of Culture a drunk lay down on the rails in front of him, and he went around him on another track, even though you can't go around anything in a tram. His voice is hoarse and alive, with a whistle; he coughs and swears. I hold on to his voice with both hands. The other three shout behind it, quieter and quieter, and by five in the morning I can't hear them anymore.

In the morning Aunt Lyuba, the orderly, finds me asleep on the floor by Semyonych's bed. Semyonych is holding my hand and telling everyone a little green man came to him in the night and asked for a story.

After that I can't drink anything until lunch. Every sip I hold in my mouth first, checking for sand.
