Spring comes early in '29. By late March the Tom by Rudnichny is already gray, the ice has darkened and cracked, but the fishermen still sit near the bank on their crates, like always, until the very last.

After classes I take Sixth Grade A on a field trip to the Krasnaya Gorka museum. On the way back we walk along the embankment, twenty-six kids in pairs, and every five minutes I count heads. Timur Gainullin, the smallest in the class and the quickest, runs down the slope onto the ice on a dare, to a fishing hole someone left behind.

"Gainullin, get back here!" I yell.

He turns around and waves at me, pleased with himself. The edge of the hole breaks off under his sneaker like a wafer, and Timur goes down to the waist, to the chest, over his head, so fast I don't have time to close my mouth. Only his backpack is left lying at the edge of the hole. It's about twenty meters away. The girls are shrieking, and one of the boys is already filming on his phone.

The cold pulls the scar on my palm tight before I can think anything. My lips start counting seconds on their own, like Nika did back then on the bank. On the eighth a big bubble comes up out of the hole, and nothing else.

## После A
I run straight, across the gray, and the ice gives underfoot like a trampoline in Zhukov Park. My legs remember this river from childhood. Three meters from the hole I drop onto my side and slide to the edge on my jacket, like down an ice slide.

I yank Timur out by the collar; his backpack stays lying on the ice. He doesn't cry or cough, just stares at me with white eyes and hiccups. On the bank Sixth Grade A is bawling in chorus, and the boy with the phone never thinks to stop recording. That evening the whole school watches the video, and the vice principal calls me in to ask why twenty-six children were walking along the embankment without a second adult.

## После C
I turn the kids with their backs to the river and dial 112. My voice is even, a teacher's voice, and I don't recognize it myself. The rescuers arrive eleven minutes later.

Divers bring Timur up two hours later. Since then his mother crosses to the other side of the street when she sees me, and she's right to.

## После D
I run to the left, along the bank, over dark but unbroken ice, and come at the hole from the side, where the ice is thicker. I pull Timur out by the hand on the first try. He doesn't even have time to swallow much.

The Choir rustles in my head, pleased.

## После E
I remember ice overhead from the inside, gray, with bubbles, and how I pounded on it with my fist. I remember a dacha pond and a strange boy with my face whom I never pulled out. I have no pond and no dacha. Whose memory that is, I don't stop to figure out. My body knows what to do before I do.

I drop onto my stomach right by the bank, where dry reeds stick up out of the ice, and crawl along the edge toward the hole from the side, undoing my belt as I go. The ice crunches under me but holds, because all of me is lying on it. Timur catches the belt on the second try, buckle across his fingers. I pull him centimeter by centimeter, and he crawls out onto the ice on his belly, paddling with his arms, and immediately starts bawling, louder than anyone.

On the bank Sixth Grade A is bawling in chorus. Timur shivers in my jacket and asks why I crawled like an idiot when in the movies everybody runs. "Because in March you don't run on ice," I say. I don't tell him about the boy with my face.
