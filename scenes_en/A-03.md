The boy coughs. Half the river pours out of his mouth, and he coughs again, and then starts to breathe. I turn him on his side and hold him by the shoulder until the water stops coming.

I take off the storm jacket, canvas, soaked through, and cover his legs with it. On Saturday Grandma will take it from our balcony, hold it on her knees and say, not to me, "Tisha…?" And then she'll keep it in her trunk until she dies.

The girl with the panama hat in her fist looks at me with round eyes.

"Two hundred and fifty-two," she says. "I counted."

"Good girl," I say. "You counted it all exactly right."

The freckled boy still stands a little way off, shaking. Soon the ambulance will be here, and Mom in her work overalls. I mustn't be here. Nobody in this city knows this old man, and nobody ever will.

I get up and walk toward the willows, in a wet striped shirt, barefoot over the hot sand. The footprints I leave behind are long and dark. The bushes close behind me and are still swaying, though there's no wind. Here, in the shade, the seam is still holding, and through the leaves I can see both at once: the noon of 1996, and the night of 2061 with the comet and the fog over the dry riverbed. The four minutes while the two ends of my life touch are almost up.

I look toward the night and for a long time can't work out what's wrong there. The bluff over the riverbed is the same, and the Gorka is the same, and the comet is the same. But the streetlights up top run wrong, and beyond the bluff burn the lights of a street I don't remember. The number I know by heart answers there in a stranger's sleepy voice, and in my window other people sit at a table watching television. I stand in the bushes and can't remember where I left my boots.

The willows here are sparse, and through them you can see in every direction at once. To the right, where the seam is, a crack trembles, narrow, like the gap between doors that don't quite close. Behind me, the dry riverbed of 2061 and the comet. And ahead, beyond the willows, the noon of 1996: the roofs of Shakhtyorskaya Street, the boiler-house chimney, a yellow PAZ bus crawling toward the stop.

Twenty-five years ahead lie spread out before me, with price tags, while everyone else in this city walks around blindfolded.

I look at this noon and find our balcony on the third floor, laundry on the line. Dad lives there, thirty-four years old, and Mom, who's running here across the waste ground right now in her work overalls. They don't know anything yet, about the dust or the hospital.

::if ДАР >= 70
The Choir is louder in the willows than at the backwater. The voices aren't whispering anymore; they're standing around me in the bushes, and I can tell them apart one by one. The seam holds them while it holds itself, and I feel I could hold it a while longer.
::end

Here, in 1996, I won't have the Choir; the seam will go and take it with it. If I stay, I'll have to die once, like everyone.

The Choir is silent, waiting for me to choose.
