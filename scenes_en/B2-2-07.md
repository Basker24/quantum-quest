Since '59 nobody in Kemerovo drowns. By July there's no Tom in the city, and nowhere to drown. I still go out onto the embankment at five in the morning and walk to Pritomsky, though there's nobody there to count. The piers the boys used to dive off only after I'd passed now stand over the dry bottom, twice my height, and the "No Swimming" sign hangs in its old place. The silt under it has cracked into neat squares, like graph paper.

Water is left in the city in one place, across from Rudnichny, in the Black Backwater, and in three summers its level hasn't moved a finger's width. Sixth-graders from School No. 41 are taken to look at it as a visual aid. The physics teacher, whom I once taught myself, gave them homework: explain where the water in the backwater comes from if there's no river. The neighbors' granddaughter brought me that homework. I wrote "I don't know" for her and gave myself an F.

I'm seventy-two. Every night I dream of water, and I wake at 04:12. Mom used to tell me that Dad woke at that time all his life and went out on the balcony to smoke. In the daytime I'm drawn to the river the way you're drawn home after a long work trip. The Choir hums steady and low now, like the transformer box in the yard.

In the evenings the comet is visible in the sky. Galina Ivanovna didn't lie: on the twenty-eighth of July the comet will pass closest to the Sun.

"This is how I keep records," she says. "I came up with it myself, back when I was nine. Can I come too? I'll stand on the bank."

::if F_ДОЛГ_ШАДРИНУ

::if F_ЧАСЫ_ЛУНЁВА
Before I leave, I take off Granddad's Shturmanskie, which I've carried on me for forty-five years, like Lunyov, and lay it on the kitchen table, glass up. Where I'm going, it's already there.

On the evening of the twenty-seventh I leave the house. I don't have far to go, thirty minutes downhill, to the dry riverbed. I have nothing in my pockets but my keys.
