On the first of September I go to first grade with gladioli and a pink scar right across my palm, from the thumb to the wrist. On the first day of school every kid carries flowers for the teacher. Mom traded the neighbor a jar of blackcurrant jam for the gladioli, and I got the scar for free, along with four stitches they took out back in August.

By the second day they're already calling me Vodyanoy. People on Rudnichny gossip more about how the Samokhin kid drowned and didn't drown than about Santa Barbara, and even Auntie Lyuba from the school cafeteria knows. She gives me an extra jam bun, like I'm sick.

On Saturday Grandma comes in from her wooden house under Krasnaya Gorka. She brings a jar of pickled mushrooms and takes the storm jacket I was carried home in off the balcony. Mom washed it but couldn't bring herself to throw it out. Grandma holds the jacket on her lap for a long time and asks me to tell her about the old man who pulled me out. I barely saw him myself, so I tell it the way Nika did: he's old, in a striped shirt, and his face looks like Dad's.

"Tisha…?" Grandma says very quietly, and she isn't saying it to me.

Tisha was what they called my grandfather. He died before I was born, "at the mine." In Kemerovo that's what they say about all the grandfathers. Mom takes Grandma into the kitchen, and they rattle cups in there for a long time, though nobody pours any tea.

At supper Dad tells us about ninety-one, when he overslept his shift with a hangover, and that same day the cage, the lift that takes the miners down, broke loose and fell.

"We Samokhins are lucky," he says, and knocks on the table with his knuckles.

::if F_Т02 == ЧЕЙ
"Dad, whose kid are you?" I ask, and I don't know why.

Dad drops his mug. Tea runs all over the oilcloth, Mom swears and runs for a rag, and Dad just sits there and looks at me while she wipes it up. Nobody explains anything to me.
::end

> The cage in ninety-one really did fall. Two men died. Every year after that Dad drank to them on Pokrov, the October church holiday, and never once to himself.

At school there's Shmelyov from 1B, and Shmelyov really likes the word Vodyanoy. Every break he gurgles after me and shows with his hands how I drowned.

## После A
I punch Shmelyov in the nose right by the coat room, and we roll around on the floor until the PE teacher pulls us apart. Shmelyov's nose is bleeding, and I've split my eyebrow on the corner of a bench. They stitch the eyebrow at the same City Hospital No. 2 where they did my palm, and when the doctor sees Mom he says, "You again?"

After that Shmelyov calls me by my last name. The scar over my eyebrow will stay for the rest of my life.

## После B
"Yeah, Vodyanoy. So?" I say to Shmelyov in front of the whole class, and I gurgle right back at him, loud, with bubbles, like a drowned man in a cartoon. The class cracks up. Shmelyov doesn't know what to do, because this time they're not laughing at me.

A week later even the girls call me Vodyanoy, and I like it. The nickname will stick for a long time.

## После C
I write in marker on pages from my exercise book: LOOKING FOR THE GRANDPA IN THE STRIPED SHIRT WHO SAVED ME. Mom sighs, but she helps me stick them up on lampposts all over Rudnichny.

Nobody finds the grandpa. But in October the *Kuzbass* newspaper prints half a column about a boy looking for the man who saved him. Mom cuts it out with scissors and hides it in the china cabinet.

## После D
I don't talk about water anymore. I only go in the bathroom with the door open, and when Mom runs a bath, I go to my room. At first Mom gets angry, then she takes me to the psychologist at the clinic, then she gives up.

Shmelyov soon gets bored of gurgling. What's the fun of teasing somebody who doesn't answer back.
