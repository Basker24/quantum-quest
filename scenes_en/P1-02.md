On the seventeenth of August Kiriyenko speaks on TV, and by lunchtime the cardboard signs at the market gates have no numbers on them. The money changers shut up shop. At the exchange office on Sovetsky the line runs to the corner, old women cry, men swear, and nobody understands anything except me.

::if F_П101 == СВАРНОЙ
Welder and I take three kiosks for a case of vodka and one signature. The owners are glad to hand them over: they have nothing to pay with, and we have dollars. Welder walks around the market smiling his gold-toothed smile, and calls me Grandpa, and so does the whole market after him. I don't object. I am a grandpa, just not theirs.
::end

::if F_П101 == ШАДРИН
A week before, Shadrin moves everything into dollars, because I told him to. On Monday he buys up the debts of three mines for the price of the paper they're printed on. He gives me a Volga with a driver and says in front of people, "Our Nostradamus." The driver reports to him where I go.
::end

::if F_П101 == ЛУНЁВ
Lunyov calls me from his department in Tomsk; his voice is shaking. On Monday they paid him his March wages, and now they'll buy three loaves of bread. He asks whether I could come a bit earlier this Friday.
::end

::if F_П101 == ТИХО
By evening the eight hundred dollars from the coffee can are worth what a one-room apartment was worth a week ago. I give the changer his thousand, as promised. He takes the money and looks at me for a long time, as if I were a sorcerer.
::end

And on Wednesday, two days from now, in Tomsk, at the Oktyabrskaya Hotel, in room three hundred twelve, a man from Moscow will be waiting for Vadim Petrovich Lunyov. The man has an Orpheus foundation business card, and Lunyov will bring him a shoebox with a thermos inside. In the thermos, two test tubes. Forty years from now they'll make the "second chance" from them.

On Wednesday morning I'm sitting in the third-floor corridor on a sagging chair opposite room three hundred twelve. By the door stands a crew-cut guy in a leather jacket pretending I'm not there. The elevator hums. The floor smells of bleach and other people's cigarettes.

## После A
Lunyov steps out of the elevator with the box under his arm and sees me.

::if F_П101 == ЛУНЁВ
"I thought you wouldn't find out," he says, and turns red to the ears.
::end

::if F_П101 != ЛУНЁВ
He doesn't know me, but he looks at me as if he's seen this face somewhere and can't remember where.
::end

I name a sum. He's silent for a long time, looking at the door of the room, at the guy in the leather jacket, at me. Then he puts the box in my hands without looking, the way you hand a child to a stranger. He takes the money in an envelope and doesn't count it. I carry the thermos home on my knees on the commuter train and hold it so it won't knock against the side of the car.

## После B
Welder brings two of his guys, and we go into the room together when Lunyov is already there. The Muscovite in a white shirt is sitting on the bed and doesn't even get up. In the corridor Welder hits the crew-cut guy once, short, and he sits down on the floor and stays there.

I take the box from the table myself. Lunyov looks at me, white, and I see he'll remember me. The Muscovite says to my back, "You don't understand what you're doing; this is science." In the hotel yard, behind the dumpsters, I smash both test tubes against a brick and pour gasoline over them from the can Welder keeps in his trunk. It burns briefly, and blue.

## После C
I intercept Lunyov at the elevator and take him to the stairs, where the maids smoke. He sits down on a step, the box on his knees.

"You'd make more on this than I would," he says. "Why?"

"Because I know what it will turn into."

He looks at me for a long time, then opens the thermos right there on the stairs and pours both test tubes into the sand-filled ashtray bin where the maids put out their cigarettes. The Muscovite in room three hundred twelve will never see him. A week later Lunyov calls me and asks whether I need a lab assistant. I hang up.

## После D
Shadrin arrives in Tomsk by lunchtime, in person. He walks into room three hundred twelve without knocking, and the Muscovite stands up. They talk for twenty minutes. Then the Muscovite leaves without the box, and Shadrin comes out with it and nods to me.

"Good work, Nostradamus," he says. "This is better than mines."

In the car on the way back he holds the box on his knees like a birthday cake. I look out the window at the pines. I know that forty years later his ad will hang at every bus stop, only now it'll be twenty years earlier.

## После E
Lunyov steps out of the elevator with the box, walks past me without looking and knocks on the door. Fifteen minutes later he comes out without the box. He holds the envelope with both hands and walks to the stairs, though the elevator is standing open.

I sit on the chair until the corridor starts to get dark. Everything happened the way it was supposed to. I lost nothing and touched nothing. For some reason that feels worse than if I'd stolen the box myself.
