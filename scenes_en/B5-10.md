After covid Koh Tao never came back to life, and in '21 I moved to Phuket, to Rawai. Ploy's cousin had an empty shop there, and he rented it to me at half price. A one-room shop, six tables, a ceiling fan. For a long time I wondered what to do with it, and then I called Mom and asked how much onion to put in the filling.

"What, is there nothing to eat there?" Mom said, frightened. And she dictated the recipe, gram by gram, and at the end said you have to knead the dough until it's like an earlobe.

The dumpling shop is called the Tom. The Thais call pelmeni "Russian dim sum" and eat them with chili sauce. Until autumn our customers were Thais, the odd German and one elderly couple from Moscow who've wintered here for twenty years. Since October there haven't been enough dumplings to go around.

They fly in every day, four flights. Young, with backpacks, with laptops, with cats in carriers. Dima from Novosibirsk sits with his laptop from six in the evening until four in the morning, working for Moscow on Moscow time, and orders three portions. Lena from Petersburg, with a ginger cat, asks if the cat can have sour cream. Sanya, nineteen, flew in in September with one backpack, and he's from Rudnichny, from Institutskaya Street, two yards over from the school I went to. He sits by the window and says nothing, and when I put a plate in front of him he says "thanks, Uncle Andrei" in a way that makes me want to turn away.

The Moscow couple at the next table say loudly that "they've all come flooding in," and "they should have stayed home." Dima answers them, loudly too. Lena hugs her carrier to her. The cat in the carrier yowls. I serve dumplings and say nothing, because I don't know a single word that can be said here.

## После A
Within a week Sanya makes dumplings better than I do; his grandmother was from Altai. Dima fixes our register and builds a website where the dumplings spin like planets. Lena washes dishes and talks to the Thai cooks in sign language so that they roar with laughter.

It doesn't bring in more money; it brings in more people. In the evening, when everyone leaves, Sanya stays to mop the floor and asks what it was like when I left in '08. I say for me it was anywhere, as long as it was away from there. Sanya nods. "For me it's anywhere, as long as it's not there," he says, and neither of us says anything more.

## После B
I write the sign in marker on a piece of cardboard, in Russian and English, and hang it over the register. The Moscow couple praise the sign. Dima photographs it and posts it somewhere with a caption I don't read.

People stop arguing at the Tom, at least out loud. They eat silently, quickly, looking at their phones. Sanya stops coming in January. Where he is, I don't know. Now there are enough dumplings for everyone.

## После C
Nika comes at closing time, while I'm putting the chairs up on the tables. We sit in the kitchen among sacks of flour drinking Thai rum from plastic cups. She tells me about Novosibirsk, the hospital, her husband who has his mother. I tell her about the jellyfish at Phangan and suddenly realize I'm telling it all the way it was, the death too. She listens and doesn't say I'm crazy.

"You were always like this," she says. "Since the backwater. You never quite surfaced from there."

## После D
On Fridays the Tom is closed, with a sign on the door: "Gone to sea." We borrow her brother's boat, leave Mali with her grandmother and head out to Racha Noi for two dives, like on Koh Tao in 2010.

Underwater, at twenty meters, Ploy takes my hand and holds it. It's quiet there; there's only the whispering, and one day, up on the boat, I tell her about it, for the first time in twelve years. Ploy nods. "Thai people also hear," she says. "Old people. It's okay." And doesn't ask anything more.
