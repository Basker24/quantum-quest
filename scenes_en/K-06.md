Bus number seven goes down the left lane across the whole city, past the post office, past Pritomsky, over the bridge. I sit by the window, alone in the warm bus, watching the signs change outside. The letters are the same, but the words are different. The driver never once turns around. At the last stop he opens the doors and says, "Shcheglovsk. Rudnichny. End of the line."

Rudnichny is where it was. The five-story blocks are where they were, the garages are where they were, only there's no scrap yard on Shakhtyorov; there's a little park with a fountain. Our stairwell is where it was. I climb to the third floor and ring our doorbell. A woman I don't know opens the door in a housecoat and asks whom I want. I say the surname. She shakes her head: there have never been any Samokhins in this stairwell; she's lived here since she was born.

I stay. My money is good here, my card works, and I have nothing else here. I rent an apartment on the embankment and live in Shcheglovsk for twelve years. I don't call Shadrin; his number doesn't exist here. Lera doesn't call. Vitka's phone answers in the voice of a girl who says that subscriber doesn't exist, and she says it backward, and I understand. I look for Sonka for three years and don't find her. At the information bureau they tell me that no Valentina Samokhina was born in the city in 1966, or maybe she was, but under a different surname; who can keep track of all these Valentinas.

The voices in my head, all of those who died instead of me, have been silent since that night. They probably couldn't find me either.

The Tom in Shcheglovsk is full, wide, gray. It flows south. Every morning I go out to the embankment and watch the water go to where back home it always came from, and no one but me notices.

On the twenty-eighth of July 2061 I go out to the embankment at four in the morning. Over the river is the comet, its tail along the riverbed, and the riverbed here is full. There's no Black Backwater in this city; I looked. Where it should be, ordinary water flows, fast, and nothing in it glows.

At 04:12 my right palm goes numb, the one with the scar. The scar fades before my eyes, slowly, like a fingerprint on a misted window, and I look at the smooth skin and can't remember where it came from. In another Kemerovo, in the dry riverbed below Krasnaya Gorka, stands a full backwater, and a boy in shorts is drowning in it, and tonight there's no one beside it.
