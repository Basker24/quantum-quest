I leave Tomsk with one suitcase and a box of books. I drop my graduate program without finishing the last chapter. Shadrin from the foundation calls twice to offer me "interesting work." I don't pick up.

On the first of September I'm standing at the blackboard in the physics classroom of School No. 41, the same school I once walked into with gladioli and a scar on my palm. Above the board hangs the same bearded Kurchatov. Seventh Grade B looks at me with open boredom, and Shmelyov Junior asks from the back row whether it's true I'm the Vodyanoy.

Apparently it is. Nicknames in Rudnichny last longer than Santa Barbara did.

Mom is glad I've come back and doesn't ask why. Dad coughs in the mornings so you can hear it through the wall, and doesn't ask either. In the evenings I walk to the backwater. The water in it is just as black, and the snag sticks up in the same place.

::if СВЯЗЬ_НИКА >= 40
Nika calls from Novosibirsk every Sunday. We talk for a long time, about everything except the main thing. Last time she said, "They're offering me a position at the cardiology center here in Kemerovo. Say something, Samokhin."
::end

In the staff room everyone drinks tea from one shared mug that says "Best Teacher 2009." Olga Sergeyevna, literature, nudges the cookies toward me and asks where I got that look, "as if you've already seen it all."

## После A
"Come," I say.

Nika is silent on the line for so long that I check whether the call dropped.

"Two hundred and fifty-two," she says at last. "That's me counting how long it'd take you to figure it out. You made it."

In December she moves in with two suitcases and a cat. In the summer we sign the papers at the registry office on Lenin Avenue, no dress, no limo. Vitka would have said we've both been sick in the head for a long time.

## После B
"I have," I say. "Many times."

Olga Sergeyevna looks at me closely, without smiling, and tops up my tea. A month later we take Seventh Grade B to the theater together; six months after that I move my books into her place. What exactly I've seen, she never once asks.

## После C
In Seventh Grade B two kids are on the police register, one has been smoking in the bathroom since fifth grade, and Shmelyov Junior, the nephew of that very Shmelyov, has a dad in prison. A year later half the class comes to my extra physics lessons. They don't love physics; I just don't yell at them.

## После D
I rent a one-room flat on Shakhtyorov Street, top floor, with a view of the headframe. Mom brings cutlets in a jar and looks at me the same way she looked at me in the hospital in '96. In the evenings I grade notebooks and listen to the Choir rustle. I don't need anyone, I tell myself, and almost believe it.
