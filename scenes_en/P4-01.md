I stay in 1996. The seam closes behind me with a quiet sound, like a door closing in the next room. There's no way back, and the Choir goes with the seam. My head goes quiet, like an apartment the furniture has been moved out of.

Until evening I sit in the willows, waiting for my striped shirt to dry. Then I come out to the Shakhtyorskaya bus stop, barefoot, and stand by the beer kiosk, because I have nowhere else to go.

A young man in a work jacket buys two mugs of Zhigulyovskoye and pushes one toward me, a stranger, an old man. He's gray with fatigue, he's thirty-four, and he's my father.

"My son almost drowned today," he says. "Some old man pulled him out. Wish I could find him, stand him a bottle."

There's foam on his mustache. His hands are black; the coal has worked into the cracks of his fingers, and it won't wash out until he retires. I remember what these hands will become later, when the dust gets to his lungs. Right now they hold the mug firmly.

"The doctors say he didn't breathe for four minutes," he says. "And he's breathing. The kid's in the hospital with his mom, and they threw me out, said go home, Papa, don't get in the way. How am I supposed to go home."

He looks at me for a long time, wrinkling his forehead, trying to remember where he's seen this face. The bus to City Hospital No. 2 will come in ten minutes.

## После A
I finish my beer, put the mug on the counter and hold out my hand. He shakes it firmly, a miner's handshake, and holds it a second longer than he needs to.

"You'll find that grandpa of yours yet," I say.

He gets on the bus that's pulled up and watches me through the dirty glass until the bus turns the corner.

## После B
He laughs, briefly, into his mug.

"What, Grandpa, you from the union?" Then he stops laughing, because I'm not joking. "How do you know my name?"

"It's written on your jacket," I lie. There's nothing written on his jacket.

He looks at his jacket, then at me, finishes his beer and gets on the bus without saying goodbye. But I can see he's taking the word "respirator" with him.

## После C
"He can swim," says my father, and immediately corrects himself: "Thought he could."

He's silent for a long time, turning his mug on the counter.

"All right. I'll teach him. Myself. Anywhere but there."

## После D
The mug in his hand stops halfway to his mouth. Beer spills on his sleeve; he doesn't notice.

"That's what my old man used to say," he says quietly. "When he called from the mine. Synchronize watches. How do you know him?"

"We worked together," I say. "Long ago."

He sets the mug down on the counter and looks into my face, no longer frowning, but straight. The bus pulls up, opens its doors, stands there and leaves without him.
