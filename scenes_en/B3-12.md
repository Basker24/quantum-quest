Miron has been gone eight years. His grandson Erkin takes me to the Telbes on a quad bike, along the old trail, which is a road now: they've been felling timber here for the Wager, larch and cedar, as much as the trucks can hold. Erkin calls the war the Wager, the way everyone does: the ones fighting it have had the second chance, and they're betting they won't die. The Wager has been going on for two years, somewhere far away, beyond the Urals and beyond the seas, and the timber for it is cut here. Erkin says they took every man under forty in Tashtagol except him, because he's the only one who knows where the ford across the Kondoma is.

The hut stands where it stood, only without a roof. Fireweed has grown waist-high right inside. By the threshold there's a cross, wooden, blackened, with letters cut into it with a knife: "G. I. 1937–2013." Miron buried him himself, in spring, when he found him. That's what Erkin told me. They say the bear still comes to the cross.

The flasks on the shelf are intact. A candle stub in each. I take one down, and a dead tick falls out of it, dry as a sunflower seed.

The ammunition crate stands under the bunk in the same place. Green, with a star. Forty-eight years ago I sat on it. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string, are letters. Envelopes without stamps. On each, in pencil: "For Gosha. Personal." Inside are sheets of graph paper covered in small handwriting, even as in a copybook, and in the margins formulas, arrows, numbers, and everywhere, on every sheet, in the corner, circled: "04:12."

The last letter is dated the twenty-seventh of January '86. I read it standing up, in the fireweed, with the sun coming through the hole in the roof.

"Gosha. Tomorrow we try the forty-first. If anything happens, take the crate somewhere quiet and cold, and don't give it to the gray coat, even if he puts you on your knees. If it doesn't work for me, it'll work for one of ours, later. It gets passed on, I told you, like ears. Let whoever finds this listen, not barge in. Listening is all I've understood in twenty-seven years. Cold, a screen, four-twelve. The rest is in the margins. Tisha."

His signature is round, with a little tail, like Dad's. I didn't know that. I stand there with the letter tapping my knee with my hammer, quietly, without noticing, until Erkin asks from the threshold whether I'm all right.

> It took him twenty-seven years to understand that you have to listen. It took me fifty. Things sink in slowly in our family.

## После A
Once a week Erkin brings me bread, canned stew and batteries for the flashlight. I sit over the letters every day, from morning till dark, on the crate with the star, and work through the margins. The formulas are old, Soviet, the notation isn't what it is now, and at first I don't understand half of it. Then I do. For forty years I calculated how ice in permafrost turns to water and back, and here it's the same thing, only Granddad's ice is inside the head.

By August I have a thick graph-paper notebook in my own handwriting. The whole protocol: how much cold, where the screen goes, how many minutes, at what hour. Why four-twelve. Why the forty-first failed and what should have been done differently. Granddad got one sign wrong, in the margin of a letter from October '85, and corrected it in pencil in January, the day before the experiment. He corrected it wrong.

I close the notebook. That night in the hut I tap the crate with the star with my hammer, and the ringing in my head holds until morning, steady, on one note, and there isn't a single stranger's voice in it.

## После B
I carry the crate home on my knees, on the commuter train to Novokuznetsk and then on the bus, and don't open it. At home I set it in the kitchen, on a stool, and every morning I take out one letter, read it over tea and put it back.

Granddad writes to Gosha about formulas, about cold, about coils, and between the formulas about Grandma, how she pickles cucumbers and scolds him for ash on the tablecloth. About Dad at twenty, who went down to the coal face to spite him and keeps silent when they meet. About being afraid. About not being able to stop. There are a hundred and twelve letters. I read them until spring.

::end

## После C
The stove in the hut has collapsed, so I pile the letters on the rocks by the stream where I once tapped with my hammer toward morning. Erkin stands beside me, silent. I burn them one at a time, reading the first line first. "Gosha, today it was minus twelve on the level." "Gosha, Kolya asked again where I spend the nights." "Gosha, he sat in the chair and laughed." The paper is old, dry; it burns fast and even.

The last one I burn is that one, from January, signed "Tisha." The ash blows into the stream. The stream flows into the Telbes, the Telbes into the Kondoma, the Kondoma into the Tom. Somewhere down there, hundreds of kilometers downstream, the Tom by Krasnaya Gorka has been running shallow for three years now.

Erkin asks what that was. I say: letters. He nods and asks nothing more, just like Miron.
