I go to Novosibirsk on the night train, the way I did as a student. Only now I take a lower berth and hold my heart all night. Not that anyone lets you sleep in the compartment anyway. Two men my age argue about the bridge until one in the morning.

"One bridge in Kemerovo, one!" shouts the one by the window. "I drove across it to work for forty years."

"Three," says the other, and shoves a phone with a photo under his nose. "Look, my grandson took it."

They're both right. They call it the Stratification, and scientists shrug. In '55 the world lives as if it has nothing to lose. On TV they call all this "the doctrine of reasonable risk." People put it more simply: the Wager. You bet that you won't die. The news talks about exercises on the border and about generals with a "second chance" who "aren't afraid to make decisions."

::if F_В224 == ОРФЕЙ
I'm in my eighth year of consulting for Orpheus, and I know what these arguments mean better than the scientists on TV. Everyone who has steered their fate through a "second chance" shifts the shared world a little toward themselves. There are hundreds of thousands of them by now.
::end

::if F_ДАША != УЧЕНИЦА
Dasha is eighteen. I haven't seen her in a long time and only hear her sometimes in the Choir, far off. Three weeks ago that faraway voice broke off in Novosibirsk, on Krasny Avenue, where the Orpheus clinic is. Her mother calls me herself, for the first time in six years, and asks for help.

For the third day a crowd has been standing outside the clinic on Krasny Avenue. These are the relatives of "carriers" who have been "kept for examination." Women in puffer coats, surnames written by hand on pieces of cardboard, all just like outside the pithead offices in '05, only here nobody is brought up in a cage.

Guards in white body armor stand in a line at the entrance. Drones hang over the crowd. Someone throws a snowball at the glass, and the line takes a step forward.

## После A
I go through the crowd sideways, the way I went at sixteen in the crush outside the pithead offices. People shove me, step on my feet, someone elbows me in the ribs, but I make it to the staff entrance. A young, frightened orderly is smoking there. I tell him the surname. He nods silently at the door.

Dasha is sitting on the floor of an empty corridor in hospital pajamas, drawing circles on the glass with her finger. When she sees me, she says, "You're loud. I could hear you all the way from the station."

## После D
I close my eyes and listen. Through glass and concrete I make out her voice, thin, in my pitch. Third floor, left wing. I go around the building through the courtyards, find a fire escape with an unlocked door and come out right at her, at the ward with the number on its plate.

The guards look straight through me. Maybe in this world I'm really not here.

## После E
Yegor recognizes me three steps away and takes off his helmet. His face is grown-up, broad, his father's.

I tell him about Dasha, briefly. He's silent, then goes in through the glass doors, and half an hour later brings her out of the staff entrance, in his uniform jacket over her hospital pajamas. "On my responsibility," he tells the head of security, and the head waves a hand.

"We're even, Nikolaich," says Yegor, and puts his helmet back on. I don't argue, though I count it differently.
