They cool me for four hours, through the machine. The cold comes from below, from the bed, and rises up my spine like water in a bath. Thirty-two degrees, thirty, twenty-six. Lunyov reads out the numbers in an even dispatcher's voice. At twenty-eight my teeth start chattering; at twenty-four they stop, and that's worse.

::if F_В207 == С_НИКОЙ
Nika stands at the monitor in a jacket over her lab coat, keeping her fingers on my wrist, though there are sensors for that. "If anything goes wrong, I'm pulling you out," she said up top. "I don't care about the protocol."
::end

"Eighteen," says Lunyov.

The monitor takes one long note, steady, at one pitch, like the tuning fork at my temple. My heart stops, as promised. It's nothing like the elevator. I'm simply where I was at seven, in the warm, crowded dark.

Only now I can tell the voices apart: the one from the elevator, and many I don't know. Somebody coughing like Dad after a shift, somebody wheezing like an old man. The quietest voice is a child's. It says:

"You're living on my four minutes. Remember?"

My palm starts aching along the scar, from the thumb to the wrist, though I don't have a palm here.

In the ninth minute, outside, muffled, as if through water, the monitor starts beeping, and it's beeping wrong. Lunyov is shouting something. The voices around me get louder and come closer.

::if F_Т05 == ТЕТРАДИ
A sentence from Granddad's notebook suddenly comes back to me: "The observer does not see his own death. Tested." Granddad got one word wrong. The observer doesn't need to see anything. He needs to listen.
::end

Somebody takes my hand with cold fingers, and I can't tell whether it's outside or in here.

Lunyov sees the spike on the EEG and stops the protocol. They warm me through the machine for two hours. My heart restarts on the third shock.
