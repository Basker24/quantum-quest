In August Azot, the big chemical plant, takes me on as a trainee operator in the ammonia unit. Medical board, safety briefing, overalls two sizes too big, and a gas mask in a shoulder bag that you're supposed to carry everywhere, even to the canteen. At the briefing the foreman says ammonia smells like smelling salts, and if you smell it, you run crosswind. I write that down in a notebook and feel incredibly grown-up.

A shift is twelve hours. At night the control panels hum in the operator room, the pressure-gauge needles stand still, and outside the window the synthesis columns glow in the steam, tall as a nine-story building. The senior operator, Gena Shubin, who has a mustache like Lev Leshchenko, the old crooner, teaches me the main thing. You keep the logbook neat, and you don't touch anything until he tells you to.

"Here, Andryukha, you only make a mistake once," says Gena, pouring tea from his thermos into the cup lid. "Whoever they put in your place is the one who learns from it."

I sleep in the daytime, and on Tuesdays and Thursdays I go to night classes at the technical university, chemical engineering. Half the group is from the plant, like me, and in lectures everybody sleeps with their eyes open. That's the first thing you learn there. I give my trainee's wages to Mom. Mom puts them in the china cabinet behind the crystal and says she's saving for my wedding. Sonka laughs so hard she falls off the couch.

In the middle of October Mom brings an envelope into my room. A real one, paper, with a stamp and a Novosibirsk postmark, and our address on it in Nika's handwriting, round, with curlicues.

::if СВЯЗЬ_НИКА >= 40
Nika has filled three sheets of graph paper. She's a first-year at medical school, her roommate in the dorm is from Barnaul and snores. The anatomy room, Nika writes, isn't scary, it's funny; Novosibirsk is huge, and nobody there knows anybody. She's writing by hand because on ICQ I answer her with a single "ok." At the very end, after her signature, she's added in small letters: "Don't go drowning on me, Samokhin. I'm far away now. There's nobody to count."
::end

::if СВЯЗЬ_НИКА < 40
The letter is short, one page. Nika's at medical school in Novosibirsk, everything's fine, and she asks how Vitka's doing. At the end, a polite "write if anything comes up." I read it twice and put it on the windowsill.

That same evening Olya from the third stairwell catches me outside our building. We've lived in the same yard since kindergarten; she's a year younger and works as a cashier at the Maria-Ra supermarket on the corner. She rings up my Baltika without asking for ID. Right now she's got a puffy coat on over her uniform, and her name tag says "OLGA. Happy to help."

"I've got two tickets for *Saw IV* at the Moskva," she says. "I'm scared to go alone. And they say you're a drowned man, so nothing scares you anymore."

And in the afternoon Gena called to ask if I'd take his Friday night. His mother-in-law has a big birthday.

> Gena Shubin worked thirty years in that unit and never made a single mistake. I made mistakes more often, and every time they buried me somewhere other than here.

## После A
I write it on Friday, on the night shift, on the backs of old pages from the shift log. About the columns in the steam, about Gena and his mustache, about how ammonia really does smell like smelling salts and how at night the unit hums like a big refrigerator. Not a word about water. It comes to six pages. Gena looks over my shoulder and says, "That's it, the boy's a goner."

Nika answers two weeks later: "Samokhin, it turns out you can write sentences longer than three words." For New Year's she comes to stay with Vitka's mom, and on the thirtieth we ride the ice slide on Soviets Square until my jeans are soaked right through.

## После B
At *Saw* Olya grabs my hand at every bloody scene, and there are a lot of them, so we come out of the movie already holding hands, out of habit. At a kiosk she buys one shawarma for the two of us and asks if it's true I drowned that time.

"It's true," I say.

"You're lying," she says, and laughs, and I don't argue. I walk her to the third stairwell. Nika's letter stays on my windowsill till spring, and then Mom puts it away in the china cabinet.

## После C
I take Gena's Friday, and then another one, and another. They pay overtime in an envelope, and with the first one I buy Mom a phone, a Sony Ericsson with a camera. Mom never takes a single picture with it; she's afraid of breaking it.

In November, on a double shift, a girl walks into the operator room in a hard hat over a headscarf, carrying a metal sampler that looks like a milk can. "That's Olya from the central lab, my niece," says Gena. "Don't stare like that, she bites." Olya takes her sample, signs the log, and on her way out says I smell of ammonia. I say so does she. I never answer Nika's letter.
