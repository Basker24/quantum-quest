At half past four in the morning the conveyor belt in the main drift of the Glubokaya catches fire. The rubber burns slow and greasy, the smoke from it is yellow, and there's so much carbon monoxide in it that two breaths are enough. There are two hundred ninety men underground.

::if F_В105 == ВГСЧ
The unit is called out at 4:40. Palych is sixty-two; they've been trying to push him into retirement for three years, and he won't go. He leads us down himself, as the first squad. In breathing sets, along the rails, into the draft, the way he taught everyone he ever taught.
::end

::if F_В105 == БРИГАДА
I'm down below, at a face at minus one-eighty, on Dad's old team, only Dad hasn't been in the mine for two years now; he's retired. We go out by the emergency exit, into the draft, with our self-rescuers on, and at the fork we meet the mine rescuers. Palych is in front.
::end

A hundred meters past the fork Palych stops and sits down on a rail. He says into his radio, "Guys, I'm going to sit for a bit." And he lies down on his side, still in his breathing set, slowly, the way old men lie down on a couch after a shift. His heart. Two of us drag him back, and I go on, because farther on, in the smoke, is Vitka's crew.

I find Vitka by the crosscut. The heat has warped a support section above him, and a beam has pinned both his legs below the knees. He's lying on his back, his lamp shining up at the roof, a self-rescuer mouthpiece in his mouth. Next to him lies a hydraulic prop, a jack as tall as a man; somebody dragged it here and dropped it.

Something comes down in the drift, and a rock hits me in the back. The air hose of my breathing set is cut, and I have to switch to the spare self-rescuer, the one we carry for casualties. Thirty minutes or so, if I don't run. The smoke gets thicker; my lamp lights it up and nothing else.

Vitka spits out his mouthpiece for a second.

"Samokha," he says. "That you?"

::if F_Т01 == СПОР
"I put you up to it back then," he says. "The snag. I've remembered it my whole life."
::end

::if F_В106 == СКАЗАЛ
"Seven years I didn't talk to you," he says. "Because of Nika. I'm an idiot. You were right to tell her that time; I'd have done the same. She told me later she remembers too. Those steps of yours."

He shoves the mouthpiece back in. The beam over his legs creaks. It's two hundred meters to the fork, eight hundred to the shaft.

## После A
I take hold of the beam, brace my boots against the floor and pull up. At first the beam won't move, then it gives a finger's width, two, and Vitka yanks one leg out from under it, then the other, and yells into his mouthpiece so loud you can hear it through the rubber. I grab him under the arms and drag. Two hundred meters to the fork I drag him on my back, walking backward over the sleepers, and I count the sleepers like Nika would.

At the fork the guys from the second squad take him. In the cage they give Vitka a shot of painkiller, and all the way up he tells me something about beer. His legs are broken in six places. Six months later he's walking, with a cane, and a year later without one, only limping on the right leg and when the weather changes.

## После B
"I'm going for the guys," I tell Vitka. "I'll come back. Hear me?"

He nods. I go to the fork, two hundred meters, and back, with two men from the second squad and a stretcher. It takes forty minutes. In forty minutes the beam settles another hand's width.

We put the hydraulic prop under it and get Vitka out alive. Up top the doctors look at his legs and say nothing. At the regional hospital they take off both, below the knee. A year later he comes out of rehab on prostheses, gets behind the wheel of a converted Niva and says that now, like a pilot, his feet are one thing and his hands another. He talks to me. Only we never talk about the mine again.

## После C
I take hold of the beam, and Vitka hits my hand.

"Yours is already dead," he says, pointing at my self-rescuer. I can hear it myself; it's wheezing, breathing with effort. Vitka pulls the mouthpiece out of his own mouth, rips his self-rescuer can off himself and shoves it into my hands. "Take it. I'll sit here a while. My legs are resting."

"Vitya."

"You pushed me toward that snag back then," he says. "And went yourself. I remember, Samokha. Twenty-five years I've remembered. Now we're even. Go on."

He shoves me in the chest with both hands, hard, like in the yard in '96 when he was grabbing back his half of the bike. I go. I walk over the sleepers to the fork breathing his self-rescuer, and every breath I take is stolen. The guys from the second squad reach the crosscut forty minutes later. Vitka is lying under the beam on his back, and his lamp is still shining up at the roof. In his fist he's holding my empty self-rescuer.

## После D
I remember how this beam comes down when you pull it without support. So first I set the hydraulic prop under it, the one lying nearby, kick its base into place, and pump the handle until the beam rises a hand's width. Only then do I pull Vitka out.

He doesn't understand anything and keeps asking through his mouthpiece how I knew. I drag him under the arms to the fork, and the second squad takes him. His legs are broken, but they're still his. A year later he walks without a cane and tells everybody at the beer bar on Shakhtyorov Street he got out by himself.

In this world Palych is still lying a hundred meters past the fork, on the rail. I got here in time, but I could never have gotten to him in time. There's no rewind for me from his heart.

## После E
The hydraulic prop is heavy as a rail, and one of its feet is battered. I drag it over, set it under the beam next to Vitka's knee, and knock the base into place with my helmet, because I've got nothing else. Vitka swears into his mouthpiece, at length and very clearly. He thinks I'm wasting time.

The handle is stiff. At every stroke the beam answers with a creak, and rock trickles down from above, fine as salt. At the tenth stroke there's a gap the width of a finger between the beam and his shin. At the twentieth a hand fits in, and I pull Vitka by his belt, smoothly, without a jerk.

His legs are broken, but they're still his. At the fork the second squad takes him, and all the way up Vitka asks where I learned that. I say from Dad. Dad would have done the same, probably.
