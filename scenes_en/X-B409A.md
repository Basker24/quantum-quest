I sit with my back to the kitchen and order tea. The waitress brings it in a bag, with a slice of lemon on the saucer, and goes back to her magazine. I put the lemon in my tea and look at the carnations in the vase. They're dusty; the dust lies on the plastic petals like gray fuzz.

Behind me the kitchen door creaks, quietly, half a hinge. I don't turn around; I'm the sapper here, and a sapper sits and waits.

At first I take the blow in my back for someone shoving me. The table slides into me; the tea spills into my lap, hot. I slide off the chair and see the kitchen door from below, from the floor, and in the doorway a kid in a cook's jacket, about nineteen, with a sawed-off shotgun. His front tooth is chipped, and I know that gap from somewhere, long ago, I just don't remember where. He stands there shaking. "I didn't mean it," he tells someone. "I didn't mean it, he sat down himself." It smells of lemon, gunpowder and Mom's hair, coal dust from the processing plant.

## Пробуждение
…and I'm standing by the laid table at Uyut again, and the chair with its back to the kitchen is waiting for me.

My back burns between the shoulder blades, at a single point, like from a car lighter. I remember the tea in my lap, the dusty carnations, the kid in the doorway. The gap in his front tooth, like Shurup's. It's Deniska, Shurup's youngest. I used to give him rides in the Bychok when he was eight; he'd sit on my knees and steer.

In the kitchen stands Deniska with a sawed-off, and he's more scared than I am. When he used to steer, I'd hit the horn for him, and he'd squeal with joy across the whole depot.
