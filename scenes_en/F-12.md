Lunyov listens to me without turning around, and then asks who I am to tell him what to do. I say I'm nobody. He nods, puts on his jacket and walks off over the stones toward the road. Whether he sold the test tubes later or not, I never found out. Newspapers don't write about such things.
::end

::if F_П402 == СВОИ
I sit on the stone until Lunyov walks off over the stones toward the road. Then I go into the city, to our building.
::end

I rent a corner on the ground floor of our stairwell, from the old woman who frightened us with the vodyanoy all our childhood. I work as a yard keeper for the corner and soup. In the yard they call me Uncle Tisha. I chose the name myself, and when Grandma, Mom's mother, first hears it by the entrance, she drops her bag of potatoes and looks at me for a long time. Then she picks up the potatoes and never asks anything. On Sundays she brings me pirozhki.

The boy with the scar on his palm walks past me to school. At first he's a little afraid of me, then he gets used to me, then he starts sitting beside me on the bench. One day in November he asks whether it ever happens that the back of your neck goes cold, and then something happens.

"It does," I say. "It's not an illness. Listen to it. Only not always."

I teach him what nobody taught me. Not to go where the chill is screaming. Not to test it on purpose. Not to sell it. He listens with half an ear, like all boys, but remembers more than he lets on.

::if F_П401 == ЛЁГКИЕ
His father goes to his shift in a respirator, even in summer, even when the guys laugh. He'll start coughing twenty years later than in the life I remember. A small thing, but to me it's twenty years.
::end

::if F_П401 == ПЛАВАТЬ
The boy's father teaches him to swim himself, that same August, on the Tom at Pritomsky, not at the backwater. I watch from the bank. The boy yells, swallows water, clings to his father's neck, and by September he's swimming overarm and isn't afraid.
::end

::if F_П401 == ДЕД
His father and I drink beer on Fridays at the same kiosk. He calls me Uncle Tish and never once asks how I knew his father's old saying. Once, very drunk, he says, "You look like my old man. Only older. That can't be." I agree that it can't.
::end

I die in 2007, in June, a week after his graduation, at 04:12 in the morning, on my camp bed. The whole stairwell buries me. Grandma stands by the coffin with that very storm jacket in her arms; she never could bring herself to give it away.

A month later the boy chooses where he'll go. What life he'll choose I'll never know, but how to live in it, I told him.

! Ending "Uncle Tisha." You stayed near your own people and taught the boy to live with the gift. My Life shows what other roads led to this morning.
