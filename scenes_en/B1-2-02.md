The Glubokaya mine was closed in '26. Its spoil heap is still there, a gray cone above the Tom by Krasnaya Gorka, a hundred and twenty meters of waste rock that was hauled up top for seventy years and dumped in one pile. Since '29 it's been burning. From the inside, without flame: coal in the rock heats up and smolders, smoke comes off the slopes, white and yellow, and all of Rudnichny smells of rotten eggs. I'm chief engineer at Kuzbass Decommissioning, a subsidiary of the holding company, and this spoil heap is mine under an order with an official stamp on it.

Dad has been gone six years. Before he died, in the regional hospital, after the stroke, he said three things to me, one per breath: "Under Krasnaya Gorka. Granddad's there. Don't open it." And I don't. Under Krasnaya Gorka I've got other worries right now.

Vitka calls at half past five. He drives a BelAZ, one of those giant dump trucks, at the open-pit mine near Belovo, and doesn't come home on weekdays, but today is Friday.

"Samokha," he says, and his voice is raw, like in '96. "My boy's on your spoil heap. Yegorka. Three of them went up on a dare, two came down."

I get there in twelve minutes. Vitka is standing at the bottom by the fence with the sign "DANGER! BURNING SPOIL HEAP," in his work coveralls, white under his ginger stubble. Yegorka is eleven. He's standing in the middle of the slope in a red T-shirt, about forty meters above us, and not moving. Steam is coming out of the cracks around him. Under his feet is crust, baked rock, gray on top, and under it is empty space, six hundred degrees hot. I know from the measurements; there's a pyrometer in my car.

"Yegor!" Vitka yells. "Get down, I said!"

Yegorka shakes his head and cries. His sneakers are smoking.

The wind is blowing off the river, along the slope, carrying the smoke onto the road where we're standing. In my trunk I've got a crowbar, the pyrometer and canvas mittens. The firefighters from the Rudnichny station will be here in about twenty minutes, and a bulldozer from the open pit in an hour.

## После B
I go around the spoil heap from the river side, into the wind, where the smoke is carried away from the slope. I hold the crowbar out in front of me and strike the crust before every step. Where it rings, I step. Where it booms hollow, I go around. The pyrometer on my belt beeps every three meters. Twice the crowbar goes through the crust up to the handle, and heat blasts out as if from a stove when you open the damper.

I reach Yegorka in nine minutes. He clings to me with both hands, and I carry him down along my own tracks, over the ringing crust, step for step. At the bottom Vitka snatches him from me and stands holding him close, saying nothing. Yegorka's sneakers have melted, the soles stuck to his socks, but his feet are fine.

That night Vitka comes to my place with a bottle and sets it on the table. "You're the boss of that pile," he says. "Make it stop burning. Hear me? You're an engineer."

## После C
"Yegor," I say, not loud, evenly, so it carries and he doesn't flinch. "It's Uncle Andrei. Look down at your feet. See your footprints? Walk back on them. Backwards. Like down a ladder. Take your time; I'm right here."

He looks at me, then at his footprints, gray on gray. He takes a step back, then another. Beside me Vitka jerks to run to him, and I hold him by his coveralls with both hands. "Stay," I say. "He can do it himself. He knows how." Yegorka comes down in his own footprints in five minutes, backwards, one step at a time, and the whole way down I talk to him about soccer.

At the bottom he cries so hard he hiccups, and Vitka cries with him. Then Vitka tells me, without raising his eyes, "I'd have gone up. I'd have stayed up there." That night he comes over with a bottle and says, "You're the boss of that pile. Make it stop burning."

## После D
I grab Vitka by the shoulders, and he elbows me in the stomach, and I hold on. "You won't help him, you'll break through," I yell in his ear. "Wait." He pulls away once more and goes limp. We stand at the fence and watch Yegorka standing in the smoke.

The firefighters come in eighteen minutes, with ladders, and they're scared to go up too. An hour later the bulldozer arrives from the open pit and lays a path of cold rock up the slope. That's how they get Yegorka down. He stood on the crust for almost an hour and a half, and it heated up under him. He has second-degree burns on the soles of his feet, spends a month in the burn unit, and wears flip-flops all autumn.

Vitka doesn't talk to me for a year. In the garages, people say, he said about me, "Samokha's an engineer. He calculates." That night, when I'm already in bed, he calls anyway and says just one thing into the phone: "Make it stop burning."

## После E
I go around the slope from the river side, into the wind, watching where I step. Nothing grows on the dead crust. But where the rock has cooled and holds, wormwood stands, gray and dusty, knee-high, and a crooked strip of it runs up toward Yegorka. I walk on the wormwood. Where it ends, I stop and wait until I see the next clump.

I reach Yegorka in six minutes and carry him down the same way, over the wormwood, step for step. The soles of his sneakers have melted; his feet are fine. Vitka looks from me to the slope and back, at the strip of grass you can see now, once you know where to look.

"It's like you knew," he says.

I say I'm an engineer. That night he comes over with a bottle and asks me to make the pile stop burning.
