After the storm Zoya Arkadyevna doesn't let me go past camp alone for two weeks. Then she gives in and sends me with Miron to drive the horses to the far sector, beyond the Telbes. Two days on foot. For the night Miron turns off the trail toward a winter hut that isn't on the map.

"Gosha," says Miron, as if that explains everything.

The hut stands above a stream, low, up to its windows in fireweed. Smoke rises from the chimney. On the threshold sits an old man in a quilted jacket over his bare skin, his white beard sticking out in every direction, his glasses held together with electrical tape. He's picking a tick off himself; he holds it in his fingernails, studies it against the light and drops it into a jar of kerosene. There are about forty in the jar.

"My collection," he says to me instead of hello. "Twenty-two years I've lived here, never once had the shot. Not one of them's infected, eh? Miron, who's this?"

"Field hand. Came down off the golets alive."

The old man looks at me over his glasses. Then at the hammer on my belt. Then at me again, at my face, for a long time, so that I get uncomfortable and start studying the jar of ticks.

"Surname," he says.

"Samokhin."

He doesn't say anything. He gets up, goes into the hut and rattles dishes in there. Miron shrugs and leads the horses to the stream.

Inside it's cramped and hot and smells of smoke and larch resin. On a shelf along the wall stand flasks, lab flasks with graduations, and in each one a candle stub. Under the bunk is an ammunition crate, green, with a star. Gosha pours tea into two mugs, for me and for himself; he doesn't pour for Miron. Miron doesn't drink tea.

"Tikhon Savelyevich's grandson," he says, not asking. "His face. His ears. I washed test tubes for him, boy. Seven years I washed them, underground, down at minus six hundred forty. He called me Gosha, and so did everyone after him. Georgy Ilyich, that's me. Twenty-two years nobody's called me that."

"You came off the golets," says Gosha. "And on the golets that day there was a storm. Miron told me. You stood where it strikes, and you came back. So it passed to you too. He used to say it gets passed on. Through the son, through the grandson. Like ears. He told his graduate student straight out: you've got no one to pass it to, Vadik, your boy drowned…" Gosha breaks off and blows on his mug for a long time, though the tea went cold long ago.

I keep quiet. I haven't told a single person what happened on the golets. That I died up there, and then I was standing in the rain again. That I remember how the scorched needles smelled. I don't even tell myself. I think it was a waking dream, from fear; that happens.

> Back then I decided Gosha was crazy. I kept deciding that for a long time, thirty years or so. It's convenient when the person who explains everything to you is crazy. You don't have to believe him.

## После A
Gosha talks until three in the morning. The candles in the flasks burn down one after another, and he lights new ones from the old. He says my grandfather was a great man and a fool. That under Krasnaya Gorka, under the old mine, they had a level, cold as a root cellar, and in it coils, screens and a chair that people sat in. That people in that chair died and remembered how they died, and then got up.

"Tikhon wanted to turn the Tom around," says Gosha, and raps his finger on the table. "Not the water. Time. So it would flow backward, even for a minute. He used to say: where it's cold and quiet, time is weak, you can bend it. On the twenty-eighth of January '86 he bent it, and it sprang back. At four-twelve. I'd gone up to the surface for kefir. I came back, and the level was sealed, and a man in a gray coat says, 'You were never here.' So I left, so I'd never be there. Came here."

I don't believe a word. But toward morning, when Gosha falls asleep sitting up, I go out of the hut and tap a rock by the stream with my hammer. The rock rings. The ringing in my head lasts until it starts to get light.

## После B
"Drowned," Gosha repeats, and his face turns very old. "I said that, did I? Well. Tikhon had a graduate student, Vadim. Young, smart, angry. Vadim had a son, Mitka. In '88 that Mitka went swimming in the Tom by Krasnaya Gorka, in the pit, the backwater. Nine years old. They never found him. Not then, not later."

I sit holding my mug and can't put it down. I know the pit by Krasnaya Gorka. I was in it, in '96, for four minutes.

"Vadim wasn't the same after that," says Gosha. "He wasn't right to begin with, but after that, not at all. He thought Tikhon would have pulled Mitka out if he'd had time to finish his machine. But by then Tikhon had been gone two years. Don't you cross Vadim's path, boy. He's a professor somewhere now. He'll see you and want to find Mitka in you."

Gosha falls asleep sitting up, toward morning. I go out of the hut. There's fog over the stream, and in the fog it keeps seeming to me that someone small is standing knee-deep in the water, looking down at his feet.

## После C
"Miron," I say by the stream. "Is he, you know, off?"

Miron is cleaning Chalkan's hoof and takes a long time to answer.

"He's been here twenty years," he says at last. "Alone. In winter a bear comes to see him, and he pours the bear tea. That's normal."

I lie down on a horse blanket by the fire, next to the horses, and can't sleep for a long time. From the hut I can hear Gosha talking, quietly, in two voices, as if he's arguing with someone who answers him. I can't make out the words. Toward morning he goes quiet. In the morning, as we leave, he stands on the threshold with his jar of ticks and shouts after us: "Samokhin! Hold on to the cold! You're going to need it!" I don't turn around.
