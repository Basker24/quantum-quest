I come back from Altai in November, twelve kilos heavier than when I left. This is my sixth year on the pills. The voices are still sitting behind the cotton wool, quiet, and I hardly talk to them, even in my head. I sleep ten hours a night, walk slowly, rarely laugh. Mom is pleased. She says I've become calm, "like your father."

Sonka has shot up in these four years. She's fourteen, with purple streaks and earbuds in her ears; she listens to something that gives Mom a headache. On the evening of the thirtieth we're sitting in the kitchen dicing Olivier salad for tomorrow, and Sonka suddenly takes out one earbud.

"Andryukh. Is it true you hear dead people?"

Mom is in the other room ironing the tablecloth; Dad is on the balcony. I dice potatoes and think about what to say.

"Not all of them," I say. "Just my own. The ones who are me."

"And I'm not in there?"

"You're not in there."

"Good, then," says Sonka, and puts the earbud back in. Then takes it out again. "Just don't go away for so long again. At night Mom stands by your door. Just stands there."

::if lived(В7-02)
"And another thing," she says, earbuds already in, loudly, the way everyone in earbuds talks. "I dreamed I caught the bouquet at your wedding. In a canteen with tiles and balloons. Can you imagine, the embarrassment."

I'm not married. My knife slips, and the potato falls apart not into cubes but in half.
::end

On the second of January I go to see Anna Markovna. She's gotten older; the glasses on the cord are different now, but the glass holder is the same. She listens to me for half an hour and doesn't write down a word.

"My ward needs an orderly," she says. "A man, a non-drinker, calm. A room in the hospital dormitory. Your pills. The pay is laughable. It'll be quiet, Andrei. I promise you that."

But in Altai, before I left, Matvei told me about an old man. A monk who lives alone in a hermitage on the upper Mras-Su, beyond Ust-Kabyrza; you can only get there by river or by winter road. They say people who hear things go to him. They say he doesn't turn them away.

## После B
I tell Anna Markovna "thank you" and "no." She takes off her glasses and wipes them for a long time.

"Take the pills anyway," she says. "If not to take, then to have. And if they come back so you can't sleep, come here. I'll be here until I retire."

A week later I take the bus to Tashtagol, from there a passing UAZ to Ust-Kabyrza, and from there the winter road, over the ice of the Mras-Su, seventy kilometers more with a hunter on a Buran snowmobile. The hunter is silent the whole way, and when we part he says, "Serafim is strict. But he feeds you."

## После C
Vitka drives a BelAZ dump truck at the open-pit mine and drinks on Fridays. I find him in the garage by his building. He's lying under his Lada and doesn't come out while I talk.

"A monk," he says from under the car. "In the taiga. Listens to dead people."

"Yeah."

He slides out, wipes his hands on a rag and looks at me for a long time. Then he says, "Why not. Let's go. At least I'll quit drinking." The mine lets him go without a word; they're cutting staff anyway. Vitka's mother cries, and mine, it seems, is calm for the first time in five years, because Vitka will be with me.

> Vitka went back then because of me. Later he stayed because of himself, and that was worse.

## После D
Nika works in Novosibirsk, at the hospital on Nemirovich-Danchenko, as a somnologist. That's what she says, "a sleep doctor," and she laughs when I ask whether there's such a thing. We sit in her staff room at night; she's on duty; on the monitors strangers sleep, all wired up.

"You're just Andrei who sleeps badly," she says when I've told her everything. "I see people like you every night. Only they don't have voices, they have snoring."

She gives me her business card, and on the back writes her home number and "call at four, I'm awake." She doesn't see me off at the station; she has rounds. In the morning I leave for Tashtagol.
