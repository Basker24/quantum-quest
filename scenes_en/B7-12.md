Andryusha doesn't remember the Tom with water in it. He was five when it went, and for him the river is a road you can take from our building to the bridge without crossing a single street. Under the bridge he tips his head back and counts the dark bands on the piers, one above the other, like rings on a stump. I tell him that's the water signing its name, how high it came each year, and he asks where I signed mine.

There are so many fishhooks on the bottom that he collects them in a candy tin, the dark ones separate from the shiny ones, and asks about every one whether it's mine. Bloggers fly drones over the backwater, the drones fall into the black water, and nobody gets them out.

In the evenings a comet hangs in the sky. "It'll come back on the twenty-eighth of July, sixty-one," Galina Ivanovna said at graduation, and we cracked up: seventy-two was never.

Andryusha is seven, named after me, and he spends the summer with me. Kolka brings him in June with a backpack and a tablet and says, "Dad, no experiments." Andryusha doesn't know about experiments. He knows that all the fish from the Tom have gone into the backwater, because they've got nowhere else to go, and the boys from the yard pull perch out of it at first light, until the police chase them off.

"Granddad, let's go tomorrow at first light," he says for the tenth time this evening. "Come on, Granddad. I dug worms."

I'm drawn there myself. Every evening my feet turn toward the river by themselves, the way at eighteen they carried me past the bus stop after a night shift, down to the Tom. In the past year the whisper behind my ear has turned into a steady hum, like the hum at night in the unit by the synthesis columns. Granddad, it turns out, called it a choir, and I've gotten used to it.

The fishing rods stand in the front hall. Andryusha's rubber boots stand next to my shoes, toes pointing at the door.

"Granddad," says Andryusha from under the blanket, "if I fall in the water, will you catch me?"

"I'll catch you," I say.

## После A
At three I wake Andryusha, and he jumps up as if he hadn't been asleep at all. He dresses himself, pulls his boots on the wrong feet, switches them. He carries the jar of worms out in front of him with both hands, the way I once pushed a stroller. On the stairs he whispers, do fish bite in the dark? I say we're about to find out.

The yard smells of dust and dry grass. The comet hangs over the roofs. Andryusha holds my hand tight and doesn't let go all the way to the riverbed.

At three I get up, dress in the dark and stand over Andryusha for a long time. He's asleep with his arms flung out, his fishing rod lying on the blanket. I take the rod, tuck him in and write on a slip of paper: "Gone fishing. Go back to sleep. Granddad." I put the note on his boots.

I close the door quietly, two turns of the key. The yard smells of dust and dry grass, and the comet hangs over the roofs.

On the stairs she goes first, holding the railing. In the yard she takes my arm, and we walk like that all the way to the riverbed, slowly, the way old people walk, and the comet hangs over us.
