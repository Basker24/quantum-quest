Since the fall I've been mine foreman of section seven, still without a diploma, in my last year of correspondence school. They haven't given me a white helmet yet; they gave me an orange one with a white stripe and a desk in the assignment room, by the window, where you can see the headframe. At this desk every morning I write the work order: who goes where, on what job, how many meters. Twenty-three names in a column. Seryoga Pakhomov on the fifth line, Vitka on the eleventh.

In '15 the ruble falls by half, and the holding company announces that bonuses will only be paid to sections that make quota. Longwall seven doesn't make quota, because it's gassy. The fixed sensor at the junction reads one and a half percent methane and cuts the power to the shearer; twenty minutes later the gas thins out and the shearer goes back on; forty minutes after that it all happens again.

The section chief, Bondarenko, sits down on the edge of my desk, and the desk creaks. Bondarenko is fat and honest in his fatness, twenty years underground, two kids in paid colleges in Kemerovo.

"Andryukha," he says. "You're the foreman. It's your shift and your work order. I'm not telling you anything, I'm just showing you." He lays a Maria-Ra bag with handles and a roll of blue electrical tape on the work order. "Pakhomov's got a kid on the way. Your Vitka's paying off a loan on his Lada. You're one of us. Sort it out."

He goes out to smoke. The bag lies on my work order, across twenty-three names.

Vitka comes into the assignment room for his lamp, sees the bag and whistles.

"Oh," he says. "Management's growing up. Samokha, whatever you do, sign the work order, or they won't let us down without one." He laughs and leaves.

## После A
Seryoga takes the bag from my hands and doesn't look me in the face. At the face he pulls it over the sensor and winds the tape, three turns, like a bandage. I stand next to him with a portable instrument on my belt and don't take my eyes off the screen all shift. The gas doesn't go above two. The shearer doesn't stop once that shift, and seven makes quota for the first time in six months.

In March a commission from the region checks the sensor without warning. By then there's no bag on it; I took it off myself on the last day of the month. Bondarenko gets his bonus and shakes my hand in the bathhouse with a wet palm. "Good man," he says. From that bag on, everything went for me, as they say in the mine, on a level floor.

At night I dream I'm writing the work order and the names are sliding down the page like water into a drain. I wake up in the dark and count them from memory, down to Vitka on the eleventh line.

## После B
"What are you doing, Samokhin," says Bondarenko when he reads the work order. He stands there a long time, turning red, then tears my page out of the book and throws it in the trash. I write it again, the same words, and sign again. He doesn't tear out the second page.

The face stands idle for three days while they measure and remeasure the gas. The section loses its bonus. The men from seven don't say hello to me for a month, and Vitka does, but loudly, so everyone hears: "Hi there, boss." They haven't made me section chief yet that year, and that's worse than swearing.

In April the chief engineer calls me in and says that a foreman who stops a face doesn't last long in a section. I say I'll last. He looks at me over his glasses and writes something in a notebook.

## После C
The chief engineer, Grinberg, tall and stooped, listens without interrupting and looks at the ventilation diagram I drew on the back of a work sheet. "You did this yourself?" he says. "Without the institute?" I say with the institute, by correspondence. He snorts and signs the request for an auxiliary fan, and tells me to put the downtime on my section.

For a week seven stands idle while the fitters run the duct and install the fan. Bondarenko doesn't talk to me, and the men count my money out loud. Then the fan is switched on, the gas at the junction drops to half a percent and doesn't go up again. The face runs.

In the bathhouse Bondarenko hands me a birch whisk without a word. He takes the Maria-Ra bag off my desk himself and carries his pork fat to work in it.
