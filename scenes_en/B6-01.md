After graduation I stop sleeping. I've had the voices since I was seven, a whispering under water as you fall asleep, and I got used to it the way you get used to a tram outside the window. But that morning on the embankment, when every Nokia showed 04:12, someone in my head turned up the volume and never turned it back down.

They speak in my voice. One younger, one older, one wheezing like Dad after a shift. They all talk at once, each about his own thing. In the daytime they're drowned out by people, the TV, Sonka with her Star Factory show. But at night there are no people, and that's when I answer. Three nights in a row Mom finds me in the kitchen at four in the morning. I'm sitting by the window arguing with an empty stool.

On the fourth night Mom calls an ambulance. Dad says nothing, just smokes on the balcony, though he'd quit. In the morning he drives me to the Regional in the neighbor's Lada, and the whole way we're silent, and the radio sings that everything will be fine.

The doctor's name is Anna Markovna. She's about fifty, with glasses on a cord that she keeps putting on and taking off. She drinks tea from a glass in a metal holder, like on a train. The office is on the second floor; there are bars on the window, and beyond the bars poplar fluff is drifting.

"How many of them are there?" she asks, as if she were asking my age.

"Lots. Like the choir at the House of Culture on Victory Day. Only each one sings his own song."

She nods and writes in a thick cardboard folder. I read it upside down; I could do that back in school: "Voices multiple, commenting, 'Choir.'" She puts the word "Choir" in quotation marks, as someone else's. So now they have a name, and she gave it to them, but I came up with it.

Then she takes a clean sheet and divides it into three columns with her pen. At the top she writes: "Who," "What he says," "When."

"I need to understand what they want from you, Andrei," she says. "Will you help me?"

::if F_Т02 == ХОР
"They count," I say. "Since I was seven. Under the water they counted, along with the girl."

Anna Markovna takes off her glasses and wipes them for a long time with the edge of her white coat.
::end

## После A
I talk for an hour. Anna Markovna writes in three columns, and the "Who" column grows fastest. "The one under water." "The one who coughs." "The one who's oldest and keeps silent the whole time, and then says 'well, well.'" The tea in the glass goes cold, with a skin on it.

"You can tell them apart," she says at last. "That's good. That means you're still here."

Then she asks when I last ate, and herself brings a raisin bun in paper from the canteen. I eat the bun in front of her. For the first time in four days I'm hungry.

## После B
"It's all gone," I say. "I got stressed. Exams."

Anna Markovna looks at me over her glasses and writes nothing. Then she says, "Good. Then we'll observe you for two weeks, since it's all gone." Her voice is kind, like a shop assistant's who knows for certain you're about to steal a pack of gum.

In the ward I sit on the bed and wait for someone to come for me. Nobody comes. The voices laugh quietly at the back of my head, all at once, and the oldest one says, "Well, well."

## После C
Anna Markovna thinks, then opens a drawer and takes out a thick notebook in an oilcloth cover, ninety-six sheets, green. On the cover someone before me started writing "Diary" and gave up at "Dia."

"Pens aren't allowed in the wards," she says. "You'll write here, with me, half an hour after lunch. The notebook stays in the drawer."

I rule the first page the way she did, into three columns. I write "Who" and for a long time don't know what to write next. Then I write: "The one under water. He's seven."

## После D
They give me the pills in a little plastic cup, three of them, white, and one yellow. The nurse stands watching while I swallow and asks me to open my mouth, like at the dentist's.

By evening the voices recede, as if they'd been herded into the next room and the door pulled shut. You can tell they're in there, but you can't make out the words. My tongue gets thick, my arms heavy, and that night I sleep thirteen hours, for the first time in a week. In the morning there's a wet patch of drool on the pillow.
