Up by the roof the gas detector goes frantic; down by the floor it beeps on and off. The gas is lying in the face in a layer, from the roof down, and it's sinking. It's being drawn toward the shaft, the same way everyone will run. The crosscut opens onto a different air current, from a different fan. I've seen the ventilation plan on the shift office wall a hundred times, between the shift schedule and the poster about helmets, and I only understand it now, by the beeping.
::end

The crosscut is dark and smells of old rock, like a root cellar. My lamp beam hits a concrete stopping, and in it is a steel door, small, chest-high, with a white arrow painted on it by hand, crooked. The arrow says "pull." In front of the door is knee-deep fallen rock from the roof, and the door is wedged against it.

I try the handle. The door sits as if it had grown into the rock. Cold, damp air from the next drift comes through the gap under it, down by the floor, and I hold my palm under the gap as if under a faucet. The self-rescuer is sour in my mouth, the mouthpiece rubs my gums, and every breath from it is hot and tastes of rubber.

> A door in a stopping opens toward you, so the air current presses it shut against the frame. I read that later too. Back then all I knew was the arrow.

## После A
::if !F_ГИПОКСИЯ
I hit the edge with my shoulder, by the hinges, where the rock holds it least. The door shudders; on the second blow the gap opens a hand's width, on the third two, and I squeeze through sideways, scraping my jacket on the frame. Rock spills into my boots. On the other side there's a draft as cold as an open window in winter, and the gas detector on my belt goes quiet in mid-beep.

I sit on the other side, on a rail, breathing without the mouthpiece. The rescuers come an hour later. They bring Seryoga out the same way, through our crosscut, and as he passes he slaps me on the helmet:

"You rat, Samokhin."

That's his way of praising me.
::end

::if F_ГИПОКСИЯ
I hit the door with my shoulder, once, twice, five times. The door booms like a barrel and doesn't move. The rock is holding it from below, and I'm pushing it away from me, against the arrow, and I don't even notice. My shoulder goes numb. Then my arms go numb, and the self-rescuer gets heavy and pointless, and I sit down by the door, face to the gap under it where the cold comes from, and breathe through that gap for as long as I can.

They bring us up after six hours. I throw up twice in the cage. The doctor at the first-aid post shines a flashlight in my eyes: "Hypoxia. You'll live." For a month afterward my head aches in a particular way, over my left eyebrow, like there's a nail in it.
::end

## После B
I get down on my knees and dig the rock away with my hands, stone by stone, throwing them back into the dark. My gloves tear in the third minute. More rock trickles down from the roof, a little at a time, and I dig again. I count stones so I won't count breaths. At the hundred and twelfth stone the bottom of the door comes free. I pull the handle toward me, the way the arrow says; the door creaks, sticks, and then suddenly swings all at once, and I sit down hard on my backside.

A fresh current blows from the other side. I climb through, close the door behind me the way you're supposed to, and only then sit down on a rail. The gas detector in my hand is silent; I shake it, and it's still silent.

The rescuers find me an hour later by the knocking: I'm sitting there hitting a pipe with a wrench, once a minute. The squad leader looks at the cleared door, at my bare hands, and says, "By the book, then." They bring Seryoga out the same way. He doesn't say anything, just gives me his gloves.
