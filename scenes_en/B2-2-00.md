Two weeks later, a little after five in the morning, the duty officer from the Glubokaya calls me. The phone crawls across the nightstand on vibrate, and I watch it until it reaches the edge. Professor Lunyov went down to the level alone, on a foundation pass, and hasn't answered since 04:12. I know what I'll find down there before I even get into the taxi.

I find him in Granddad's chamber. He's lying on the cot, covered with his coat, hands folded on his chest. He's taken off his glasses and put them in his breast pocket, earpieces up, the way he always did before rubbing his eyes. There's frost on his eyebrows. The cot is cold, the monitor is off. On the wall next to the worn-away "41," someone has written "42" in fresh chalk, and on the floor under it lie white crumbs.

In his coat pocket there's an envelope. On it, in his small handwriting: "For Andrei. Open if I don't come back." The envelope is thick, ten pages or so.

The investigator arrives around lunchtime. He's young, in a wet jacket, and keeps saying "Citizen Samokhin." He hasn't seen the envelope yet.

> He was fifty-nine. I outlived him by thirteen years, and every twenty-eighth of April I set out two glasses in my kitchen.

## После A
I sit down on the floor of the chamber, at his feet, and read. The investigator waits outside the door and doesn't interrupt.

The handwriting is small and even, like in a lab log.

"Andrei. I'll start with what I should have told you when you were twenty. I had a son, Mitya. He was nine. We lived on Shakhtyorskaya Street then, and he ran down to your backwater every day. In '88 I went there with him myself to measure the water, because through the old adit the river drains into the workings, toward your grandfather's chamber. I looked away for two minutes. They never found the body. All these years I've believed that somewhere, in another world, Mitya is alive. After all, you got out."

Then comes '96.

"The two test tubes of your blood I took at the hospital were not for tests. In '98, during the default, when the department hadn't been paid for six months, I sold them to a foreign foundation, Orpheus, along with a copy of your grandfather's archive. The money went into searching for Mitya. Forgive me, Andrei, if you can. I sold what was yours, and I don't even know what they did with it."

The last page is short, and the handwriting on it is already different, large, the letters sliding downhill: "Synchronize watches. I've gone to check whether your grandfather was wrong."

## После B
The investigator takes the envelope, weighs it on his palm and puts it in an evidence bag.

"We'll let you see it, Citizen Samokhin, if needed."

They never do let me read it. A month later the case is closed as an accident. The bag with the envelope, I was told later, was taken "at the foundation's request," and I never saw it again.

## После C
There's no fire down below and there can't be: with a lighter they wouldn't even have let me into the cage. I carry the envelope up to the surface under my jacket. At the mine gates the watchman is smoking on the steps, and I bum some matches off him. The paper doesn't want to burn at first, then catches all at once. I hold it until it burns my fingers and shake the ash into the storm drain grate.

Let all of it stay down there, with him and with Granddad. I'm twenty-seven, and I just want to live.

## После D
I put the envelope in the inside pocket of my jacket. In the evening, back in Tomsk, I stop by the department. The kettle in 218 is cold. I open his desk drawer and take the watch, a man's Shturmanskie with the little star. It's stopped at 04:12. Engraved on the back: "Synchronize watches. 12.04.61." Those are Granddad's words from the tape, so the watch is Granddad's too. I put it in my inside pocket and carry it the way he did, on me. Nobody sees it.

At home I put the envelope in a book on the top shelf. I can't open it, not after a month, not after a year. The watch stays in my inside pocket, even though it doesn't run.
