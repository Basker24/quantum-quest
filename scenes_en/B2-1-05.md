Shadrin is eighty and looks sixty. Medicine, they say at Site Nine, and say nothing more. He goes down the stairs to the basement of building three faster than I do.

In February Sonka broke through to Site Nine. She's thirty, a doctor, and she brought me a jar of Mom's meatballs and a blood-pressure monitor. She had no pass, and for half an hour she fought with the guards at the gate so hard that in the end they called me themselves. In the visiting room she took my blood pressure, silently, twice, and silently wrote down the numbers. "You've lost eight kilos," she said at last. "Andryush. Tell me, did you come here on your own?" I said I did.

On the door hangs a new plastic sign: "Antenna-1." Inside is a bed with rails, like in intensive care, and IV stands beside it. Over the pillow, on a long boom, hangs a microphone like the ones in radio studios, with a foam cover. Behind a glass wall, a control desk and three chairs with headphones.

"Have a look, Andrei Nikolayevich," says Shadrin. "This is for you."

He explains it calmly, like a realtor showing an apartment. Continuous sedation, body temperature thirty-three. The Choir will speak through my mouth on its own, day and night, no breaks for lunch or sleep. Behind the glass, three transcribers in shifts. Not a single minute will be lost anymore.

"You won't have to die for them anymore," he says. "You'll sleep. The Choir will do the talking."

After eleven years of sessions two fingers on my left hand can't feel the cold, the little finger and the ring finger. I rub them while he talks, and they stay wooden. We never once talk about the water heater. He knows I know, and that's enough for him.

"You're tired, aren't you?" says Shadrin. "I can see it. You haven't had a proper night's sleep in two years. Here you'll always sleep well."

He goes upstairs and leaves me alone. I sit down on the edge of the bed. The mattress slowly sinks and adjusts under my back, warm, heated. The microphone over my head hums quietly, like the tuning fork at my temple in room 218. In the Choir, at this minute, one of mine is drowning in the Ob near Barnaul, and another is burning in a car near Tula, and they're all talking at once.

I lie down, just to try it. It really is very comfortable here.

Shadrin looks at me for a long time, then nods. The Antenna, he says, has been decided to be built in Novosibirsk anyway, closer to our coal business and the new clinic, and that's about five years. "We'll wait. You're not going anywhere, Andrei Nikolayevich."

That same evening, in the one-room apartment by the station, I open Dad's parcel for the first time. I don't take the storm jacket out, I only touch the canvas.
