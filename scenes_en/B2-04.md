In April I go to the department's open seminar on quantum measurement. Nobody invites second-years, but nobody throws them out either. Professor Lunyov runs it, the one from the card on the noticeboard. He speaks quietly, holds the chalk like a cigarette, and keeps rubbing his eyes. At the second session I finally remember where I've seen him.

City Hospital No. 2, the children's ward, July of '96. A gray suit, a briefcase, two test tubes of blood. "What did you hear down there?"

After the seminar he says, "Samokhin, stay a moment." We go up to 218. He locks the door, switches on the kettle and sits down across from me, so close I can see how red his eyes are.

The cup in my hand starts rattling against the saucer, and I set it down on the table so he won't see.

He pulls out a drawer and lays an old steel tuning fork in front of me, dark with age, with a worn inscription on the stem: "Miners' House of Culture. Rudnichny Choir." Then he strikes it on the edge of the desk and presses the stem to my temple.

The fork hums, steady, on one note, right into the bone. And under the hum, quietly, as if through water, I hear them. The crowded whisper from under the snag. That same one, from '96. They're counting.

I jerk so hard I knock over my cup. Lunyov nods, as if I'd answered a question.

::if lived(В3-02)
"A hammer hummed like that," I say, I don't know why. "A geologist's hammer, on a long birch handle. Right by my shoulder, on some bald peak."

Lunyov raises his eyes to me and is silent for a long time.

"A yellow handle, polished smooth?" he asks. "Tikhon used to say geologists always have a tuning fork in their hand, only they don't know it. How do you know about the hammer, Andrei?"

I don't know how. I've never held a hammer like that.
::end

::if F_Т02 == ХОР
"In the hospital you told me, 'They were counting,'" he says. "For thirteen years I've been thinking about those three words."
::end

He points to a photo above the desk. A miners' choir in a House of Culture, men in identical jackets, and in front of them a man with his hand raised. In his hand is a tuning fork.

"Your grandfather. Tikhon Savelyevich Samokhin. On Saturdays the choirmaster in a miners' choir, on weekdays a physicist. I was his grad student. He used to say every person has a tuning fork in their head. In most people it stays silent all their life. But if you strike it, it sounds. He struck his own in '61, in an ice hole on the Ob Sea, on Gagarin's day. That's why the project was called Tuning Fork."

Granddad's lab was underground, Lunyov says, at level minus six hundred forty, under Krasnaya Gorka. At the same Glubokaya mine where my father has been cutting coal for twenty years without knowing what's under his feet.

::if F_Т05 == ТЕТРАДИ
"'The observer does not see his own death. Tested,'" I say suddenly. "It was in his notebook, in the margin."

Lunyov sits without moving for a long time, and then laughs with his shoulders only, almost silently, and can't stop.
::end

"And he called the voices the Choir," says Lunyov. "A choirmaster, what do you expect. The Choir is you, Andrei. All the yous that died where you chose differently. They don't go anywhere. Their last minute flows into the one who survived. Into you."

He takes a Mayak reel-to-reel tape recorder out of the cupboard and threads a Svema reel. His hands aren't shaking at all now, like a man who has done this many times.

"The twenty-eighth of January, '86," he says. "He called me from below, from the level. I was standing up top by the shaft, recording everything that came over the line."

He presses a key. Crackle, hum, the distant ring of dripping water. Then a voice, tired and even, like a man who hasn't slept in a long time:

"Vadim. Synchronize watches. Four-twelve. I don't know how many times I've been here now, I've lost count. I can't get them out, Vadim, not one of them. I can hear them. They're singing. It's all me… shut it down…"

After that, only noise. Lunyov doesn't turn off the recorder until the tape runs out and the end starts slapping against the reel.

"Six dead, and he was the seventh," he says. "He was trying to get everyone out. He went back, into the same night, again and again, until he lost count himself. It was his forty-first experiment, and he didn't come back from it. The report said 'sudden gas outburst.'"

He takes off his glasses and rubs the bridge of his nose for a long time.

"For twenty years I've been thinking about what it was like for him," he says. "For us, he died. But he never once saw himself dead; nobody sees their own death. Somewhere he got out of that chamber, Andrei. Only for him to survive there, everything around him had to turn out differently, in small ways and big ones, over and over. That world is so unlikely and so unlike ours that your grandmother and your father probably aren't in it. He got out somewhere nobody was waiting for him."

Then he looks at me over his glasses and says what, it seems, he called me in for:

"I've been waiting for you, Andrei. Thirteen years. You've died already, haven't you? Recently?"

I nod. I can't say it out loud.

::if F_Т01 == ЧАСЫ
He notices the watch on my wrist. I've worn it since '96; it's stopped at 04:12, and in the dorm they laugh at me for it. Lunyov presses his palm to his chest. Then, without a word, he pulls out another drawer and lays a second one beside it. The same Shturmanskie watch with a little star on the dial, even the same scratch on the glass.

"This is his," says Lunyov. "He took it off before he went down and gave it to me; in the lab you had to take off anything metal. I've kept it with me for twenty-three years. I could never bring myself to wear it."

Only mine is worn as if it had been worn sixty years longer. He turns his over; on the back is an engraving: "Synchronize watches. 12.04.61." The twelfth of April, '61. Gagarin's day. Turn the day and month around and you get 04:12. I turn mine over. The same words.

"Synchronize watches," Lunyov says in Granddad's words, and his voice goes hoarse.
::end

## После A
"Teach me," I say.

Lunyov nods as if he'd expected no other answer, and puts the tuning fork in my palm. "Keep it. It's half a century old and still holds its pitch." Then he writes a list of books on a sheet of paper. Half of them in English, one handwritten, in Granddad's hand, a photocopy.

At the end of May I call Dad and tell him where I spend my evenings now. Dad listens, and then says, "Don't go there, Andryukha. Granddad did." And hangs up. We don't talk until the fall.

## После B
"You're sick," I say, and go to the door.

He doesn't stop me; he only says to my back, "The door's locked. The key's in it."

For a week I skip the seminar. And on Friday night I wake up at 04:12 because the radiator is humming. Steady, on one note, like the tuning fork at my temple. Under the hum somebody is counting. On Monday I knock on 218 myself.

"Let's go again," I say. "From the beginning."

## После C
I go out into the hallway and call Dad on my cell, with the last money on it. I tell him everything, getting it tangled, jumping around. Dad is silent on the line for a long time; I can hear him smoking.

"Don't go there, Andryukha," he says at last. "Granddad did."

For the first time in his life, he's brought up Granddad himself. I go back into 218, and Lunyov, without asking anything, pours me fresh tea.

## После D
"Was it you standing by the backwater every July?"

Lunyov takes off his glasses and wipes them for a long time.

"The buoy keeper told you?.. Yes. My son, Mitya. '88, nine years old. Went under by the snag; they never found the body. As long as there's no body…" He doesn't finish. "All right. Drink your tea."

From that day on he talks to me differently, like someone who knows something about him that nobody else knows.
