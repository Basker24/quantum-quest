In the willows, while the seam still holds, I don't choose either end. I have no reason to go back to 2061, and I can't stay in 1996. I step onto the seam itself, the way kids step onto a rail, arms spread, and hold my balance.

The seam closes, and the bundle behind me collapses along the most probable path, with the "second chance" and the Wager. Only I'm not in it. I stand at the edge, where no one grows old. All my life I heard only my own. From here I can hear everyone who will die in the next forty minutes, whichever way I turn.

The first one I take is a mine.

The twenty-ninth of October 1968, Leninsk-Kuznetsky, the Talaya mine, section three. Forty minutes to the outburst. I'm standing in the drift among the shift, in someone else's hard hat, and no one notices me; the world doesn't record me. When the gas comes, I lie down with everyone else, face in the wet coal grit, and die, to the last breath, like that time on the cot at Sosnovka. Then I'm standing in the drift again, forty minutes before, and I go up to the dispatcher.

The dispatcher hears an old man's voice on the line: "Get section three out. Forty minutes." At first he swears. Then he gets them out. The gas comes into an empty drift. Later he can't describe the voice on the phone; he remembers only that it was old and talked like one of us, from Rudnichny.

In my head, where the Choir rustled all my life, someone is ruling a page. Number, date, time, place, how, what was heard. Granddad's columns, Lunyov's, mine. In the "number" column stands a one.

Then a bus on the R-255, in fog, twenty-three people and the driver, and a two appears in the column. Then a ferry on the Ob whose engine failed at night. Then a children's camp near Myski where the wiring caught fire at three in the morning. I burn in every building in turn until I find the one where it started. The numbers in the column come by themselves; I no longer write them.

I pass forty-one without noticing. Granddad stopped at forty-one.

At four hundred I stop remembering faces; I remember only minutes. A sapper makes one mistake, they used to say at the mine. I make a mistake every time, all the way to the end, and only then go back and do it right.

Nine years later, at four in the morning, the phone rings at Nika's in Akademgorodok. "Get out of the building," an old man's voice tells her. "Twelve minutes." She goes out in her housecoat and stands under the birches until the gas explodes in her building's basement. She recognizes the voice by the way it falls silent after the words.
::end

My instruments on the bank record until morning. Scientists from Tomsk later pull the recording off the magnetometer: an even note, forty hertz, with no beginning and no end. For a long time they play it at conferences, and no one can explain where it comes from.

I don't fix the world. The Wager stays the Wager; the generals with their "second chance" stay generals. I just walk through it ahead of everyone and step where they would have stepped forty minutes later.

When the column says ten thousand, a tired, even voice from a Svema tape speaks to me for the first time. I recognize it the way you recognize footsteps in the stairwell.

"These aren't your deaths anymore," says Granddad. "This is work."

The next number appears in the "number" column, and I go.

! Ending "The Sapper." A loop in which you die ahead, for others. My Life shows what other roads led to this morning.
