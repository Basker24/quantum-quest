At the bus stop across from my booth there's been a screen since '44. A young general with ribbon bars looks out from it, calm and a little tired, and under him it says: "Orpheus. Second Chance. For those who make the decisions." What a "second chance" is, they explain vaguely: controlled cardiac arrest under cold, awakening of the neural reserve. I know it better than they do. Thirty-two degrees, four-twelve, one step back. Those voices in my pitch that have been sounding in the Choir for eleven years now are them, the ones who bought themselves a step.

::end

::if !F_ОБРАЗЕЦ
Where they got my pitch, I can only guess. Sometimes I remember the doctor from Tomsk with his two test tubes, but I don't want to think it through.
::end

In the pharmacy on Lenin Avenue two old women in line are arguing about whether Kemerovo had a metro or not.

"Three stations," says one, banging her cane. "I got off at Rudnichnaya for forty years!"

"What metro, Lyusya, what metro…"

They're both right. They call it the Stratification now. There are hundreds of thousands of people steering their fates through Second Chance, and each of them pulls the shared world a little toward themselves.

On TV they talk about exercises on the border between two blocs that stopped being afraid long ago. It's called the doctrine of acceptable risk, and people just call it the Wager: you bet that you'll survive. My neighbor's grandson was taken off to these exercises in January. Every evening he writes her the same thing: "All good, Gran. We don't die."

In February some website posts the "Site Nine Registry." It stays up for four days. I read it in my booth, on my phone, for two days straight, until the battery dies.

"No. 1. 14.03.2017. 04:52. Talaya mine, sec. 3. Client I. Confirmed."

By the thirties the "client" column says "X" almost everywhere. An expert on TV, bald and polite, says the doctrine of risk grew out of "the predictive technologies of the twenties." That was when the generals first understood you could know forty minutes ahead. That's where it all began. I turn off the TV and sit in the dark for a long time. Those forty minutes were sold out of my head.

In the evening I take the shoebox of Lunyov's notebooks down from the wardrobe.

::if F_В217 == ТЕТРАДИ
I read all of them but the last one eleven years ago. The last one is nothing but columns of numbers.
::end

::if F_В217 != ТЕТРАДИ
For eleven years I haven't opened them.
::end

I recognize the numbers in the last notebook at once: they're my minutes. Forty, thirty-eight, forty-one, all two hundred and some, copied out of the registry. How Lunyov got them, I don't know. Next to them, his curve, and at the end, on the last page, in an old man's handwriting with the letters sliding downhill:

"The horizon grows. At the root it equals 65 years. Root: 28.07.2061, 04:12. Bl. backwater. Halley at perihelion. He will be there. I won't."

## После A
I buy an optical magnetometer at the flea market by the station, from the Chinese traders who sell decommissioned equipment from the Second Chance clinics. It's the size of a soap dish and costs as much as my booth makes in a month. The trader swears it's hardly been used. I believe him: the Orpheus logo sticker on the case hasn't even worn off.

At home I pry it open with a butter knife and spend half the night over it in my glasses, with an old soldering iron. I draw the circuit on the back of Lunyov's page, next to his curve. I'm sixty-three, my fingers aren't what they were, and the little finger and ring finger of my left hand can't feel the soldering iron at all. But I'm measuring something again, like at twenty.

## После B
I find Nika in Akademgorodok. She's a professor, gray-haired, cropped short, with the same strict vice principal's face. I wait for her on the steps of the Institute of Physiology, by the birches, where squirrels still jump around.

She comes out, sees me and stops on a step. It isn't my face she recognizes me by. I hold out Lunyov's notebook, and she looks at my palm, at the scar from thumb to wrist.

"Took your time, Samokhin," she says. Then she reads the last page, moving her lips, the way you read a prescription. "The twenty-eighth of July. All right. Call me, and I'll come."

## После C
I burn the notebooks in the stove on Tatarskaya Street, one at a time, page by page. The oilcloth burns badly and stinks; the paper catches at once. The last page, with the curve and the date, I hold over the fire longest of all, until it burns my fingers.

After that the Choir gets quieter, like a radio with the volume turned down. But the date stays in my head whole, minutes and all. Fire can't touch it.

## После D
I buy a magnetometer at the flea market by the station, from the same Chinese traders selling off Orpheus equipment. At home I don't open it up. I sit down on a stool in the dark, put the instrument on my knees and put on the headphones from an old player, so I won't hear the street.

The Choir comes fast, the way it did at Site Nine when I listened to the end. In the neighboring worlds mine are dying right now, one by one: one in an elevator, one on a highway, one in a banya. I listen to each one until his last minute and turn the sensitivity knob until the needle twitches at the instant the voice breaks off. By morning it's twitching on its own, without me, right at the fortieth minute.

I switch off the magnetometer and sit for a long time with the headphones around my neck. The ringing in my ears after that night doesn't go away until lunch.
