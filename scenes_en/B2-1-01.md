Outside Moscow they keep me at Sosnovka. It's a former departmental sanatorium in a pine forest, forty kilometers past the ring road. A concrete fence with barbed wire on top, buildings of yellow brick, an empty pool with peeling dolphins on the bottom. The sign at the gate says NKTs-9, and everybody here just calls it Site Nine. The money came from Siberian Resonance, and it shows. The canteen serves salmon, and in the basement of building two stands a chamber lined with lead, exactly like Granddad's at six hundred forty, only with air conditioning.

Sessions three times a week, at 04:12. They don't take me as far as cardiac arrest anymore; thirty-two degrees is enough. Since the Glubokaya the Choir talks anyway; you only have to listen.

Listening with me is an operator, Polina, a girl from Podolsk with a stopwatch around her neck. Her voice is like a dispatcher's at a bus station. Lunyov sits in the corner on a chair, watching the EEG. On the first day he brought me a thick notebook with an oilcloth cover, ruled by hand into columns: number, date, time, place, how, what I heard.

"Your grandfather kept it like this," he said. "Give them whatever you want, but write everything here."

Today is the first real assignment. Shadrin dictated it over the phone, and Polina reads it off a sheet: "Follow the voice to the end. Bring back the place and time."

I find the voice fast. It's one of mine, older, with a rattle in his chest. In his world I must have stayed in the Kuzbass and gone down the mine. He's lying in a drift face down in wet coal grit, and there's nothing to breathe. There are other men nearby; I hear them through him, like neighbors through a wall. Somebody is calling the foreman by his patronymic, Mikhalych, Mikhalych, and nobody answers.

"Forty minutes," I say. How I know that, I don't understand myself. For him it's happening now, and here it will happen in forty minutes. "A mine. Gas."

"Which mine?" Polina clicks her stopwatch. "Andrei Nikolayevich, which mine?"

He doesn't remember the name; he has other things on his mind. You can only find out by going with him to his last breath and looking through his eyes at the sign by the crosscut, if he looks there at all. My body on the bed starts twitching. I feel it from far away, like somebody else's. Lunyov gets up from his chair.

> Forty minutes. Later they explained to me that in forty minutes you can get a shift out of a mine from any level. If you call.

## После A
I follow him. It's dark in the drift, my chest squeezed by the same hoop as his. He crawls toward the crosscut, and I crawl with him. On his last breath he raises his head, and in the light of his lamp a tin sign hangs on a prop: "TALAYA MINE. SECTION 3." Then the light goes out for both of us.

I open my eyes on the bed. Polina has her pen ready.

"Talaya," I say. "Section three. Eight minutes to five."

Polina writes it down, goes out into the hallway and makes a call. Through the door I hear: "Confirming. Four fifty-two." She isn't calling the mine. At six in the morning the news reports a methane outburst at the Talaya mine in Leninsk-Kuznetsky, section three, eleven dead. Polina puts a check mark on her sheet. In the evening I write the first line in Lunyov's notebook. In the column "what I heard," I write: "Mikhalych."

## После B
I tear the electrode cap off my head. The wires whip my face, the sensor on my chest comes off with hair. The monitor wails. Polina shouts, "Andrei Nikolayevich, you can't!" and I sit on the bed, soaked, shaking all over.

At six in the morning the news reports a methane outburst at the Talaya mine in Leninsk-Kuznetsky, eleven dead. I knew about the mine and the forty minutes. I just didn't know which mine, and there are over a hundred in the Kuzbass.

## После C
"Whose are they?" I ask without opening my eyes. "Whose deaths are these?"

Polina is silent; that question isn't on her sheet. Lunyov answers from the corner.

"Yours, Andrei. All of them yours. There's nobody in there but you."

I understand it slowly, while the Choir withdraws. I only hear the disasters I could have been caught in myself, in one of my worlds. A mine where I could have worked. A highway I could have been driving on. Everything else is mute to me. At six in the morning the news talks about the Talaya mine in Leninsk-Kuznetsky, eleven dead, and Polina writes in the margin of her sheet: "Instrument limitation: own branches only." In the evening, through the wall, I hear Shadrin tell Lunyov on the phone, "Then we have to move him around more."
