All through August the TV shows the submarine *Kursk*, all day long. Mom watches the news and cries; Dad turns off the TV and goes out on the balcony to smoke. They think there's nothing in the world scarier than down there in the dark. I've been there. It's crowded, but it isn't scary. Only you can't talk about it, I've figured that out already, so I sit there and keep my mouth shut.

To keep me from getting under her feet, Mom sends me to clear out the shed. In there are skis without bindings, Dad's fishing rods, jars of nails, and a box from a pair of Yugoslav boots, wrapped round and round with blue electrical tape. In the box there are graph-paper notebooks full of formulas, one photograph, and an envelope with a wax seal. On the envelope it says "For Kolya." Kolya is Dad.

The photo is of two men by a river. One is in glasses and a hat and looks like Dad, only older. The other is young and skinny, with a briefcase. On the back, in faded ink: "T. S. and V. P., the Tom, 1981."

Mom looks into the shed, sees the envelope, and takes it right away. "That's your father's. Don't touch it." The envelope goes into the china cabinet, behind the crystal glasses, where the documents and the rainy-day money are kept.

## После A
I read the notebooks in the shed by flashlight, three evenings in a row. Out of all the formulas, the only thing I understand is one sentence penciled in a margin: "The observer does not see his own death. Tested."

The last notebook has no formulas. There's a table in it, ruled with a ruler: number, date, time, place, how. And a last column, a narrow one: "what I heard." There are forty rows, and almost all of them have the same time, 4:12.

I copy the table onto the last page of my school notebook. I tape the box shut again, and nobody notices a thing.

## После B
"That's your grandpa's grad student," Mom says, after looking at the photo, and takes it away right off. "Don't ask your father about Grandpa. Grandpa loved his work more than he loved them, your dad and your grandma. Your father never forgave him for that."

The photo goes into the china cabinet with the envelope. Mom closes the glass door and stands next to it for another minute.

## После C
For the box, the junk dealer on Sovetsky gives me a *Mortal Kombat 3* cartridge and fifty rubles on top. He says yes suspiciously fast, but I'm eleven, and I don't know how to be suspicious yet.

I spend the rest of the summer in front of the Sega, learning Sub-Zero's fatality. Mom doesn't ask about the box until September. I tell her I threw it out with the trash.

## После D
Dad takes the box without a word and carries it out to the garage. I follow him and watch him throw the notebooks into the potbelly stove one at a time, without looking. On the floor there's one scorched page left, with half of some formula on it.

"Your grandpa was smart," Dad says. "Too smart."

He shuts the stove door, and we walk home together. He puts his hand on my shoulder, the first time all summer.
