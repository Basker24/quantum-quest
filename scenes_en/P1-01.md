The first three nights I sleep in the heating-main trench behind the boiler house, on pipes wrapped in glass wool. The glass wool works its way into my striped shirt and prickles. My boots stayed in 2061, on the stone by the backwater, and I walk the city barefoot, like the village idiot of Rudnichny.

On the fourth day I go to the Central Market. Rows of tin kiosks, Snickers by the box, cassettes of Laskovy Mai, old women selling sunflower seeds in paper cones made from Kuzbass. At the gates stand money changers in leather jackets, and one has the rate chalked on a piece of cardboard: five thousand two hundred. I stand in front of the cardboard so long that the changer spits at my feet and asks what I want, Grandpa.

In two years, on the seventeenth of August, that cardboard will be garbage. I know which kiosk will burn down in November and who'll set it on fire. I know who'll run this market in ten years and which of these changers will be found in the Tom in spring. All of it lies in my head like goods in a warehouse, and I sit on pipes eating bread an old woman at the bus stop pushed into my hand.

Behind the market, by the garages on Shakhtyorov, a welding torch is crackling. The Welder here is thirty-nine, not fat yet, no umbrella yet, and earning his nickname honestly, with an electrode holder. I know things about him his wife doesn't: whom he owes for stolen copper and what's lying under the floor of his third garage.
::end

::if F_ПУТЬ == ЛАМПА || F_ЗНАЕТ_ШАДРИНА
In the newspaper I spread on the pipes, on page three, there's a photo: L. A. Shadrin, forty-eight, chairman of something to do with coal, cutting a ribbon at an open-pit mine. In five years he'll buy up half the mines in the region for unpaid wages. I know which of them will produce coal and which will eat up his money and his people.
::end

::if F_ПУТЬ == ПРОТОКОЛ || F_ЗНАЕТ_ДЕДА
In Tomsk, two and a half hours away by commuter train, Vadim Petrovich Lunyov, forty, sits in his department, unpaid for six months. This week he'll come to City Hospital No. 2 and take two test tubes of blood from a boy. In two years he'll sell them. To whom, I know as well.
::end

The changer spits again. I walk away from the cardboard, because I have to decide today, while I'm not yet as dirty as this market and someone might still believe me.

## После A
The third garage smells of carbide and burnt paint. The Welder lifts his mask and looks at me, at my bare feet, at my striped shirt. I tell him how much copper he took in June from the substation at Pritomsky, and where he took it, and how much they underpaid him for it. Then I tell him that on Wednesday the economic crimes police will come to see him, and he'd better not keep anything under the floor.

He's silent for a long time, the electrode holder hissing in his hand. Then he switches off the machine and asks where the hell I've come from.

"From the river," I say.

On Wednesday they really do come. There's nothing under the floor. On Thursday the Welder finds me on the pipes himself, brings me boots, his own, two sizes too big, and a bottle of Bely Oryol vodka. From that day on I sleep in his garage on a camp bed and tell him where not to go.

## После B
They don't let me in to see Shadrin, of course. For three days I stand outside the association's building on Sovetsky until the guard starts saying hello to me. On the fourth I hand him a note for the boss. The note has two lines: which open-pit mine will flood in a year, and that it would be better not to go fishing in October with the chief engineer of the Severnaya.

A week later a Volga comes for me. Shadrin receives me in an office with a carpet on the wall, pours the tea himself and looks into my face for a long time.

"How did you know about the engineer?" he asks. "He drowned the day before yesterday."

"I'll tell you a great deal more, Leonid Arkadyevich," I say. "Just pay me."

He pays. They rent me an apartment on Vesennyaya Street and issue me a passport in someone else's name, with my photo. Shadrin knows how to buy people, and I know he's bought me too.

## После C
Lunyov opens the door of his lab in the old building of Tomsk University, in a sweater with a hole at the elbow, unshaven. On his desk stand a hot plate and a pot of macaroni. I give him the number of Granddad's experiment, forty-one, and the time, four-twelve, and tell him the most interesting part of his life is about to begin.

"Who are you?" he asks, and sits down without letting go of the door handle.

"A man who has money," I say. "Soon will. I need you to keep everything you take from that boy at City Hospital No. 2 for yourself and sell it to no one. Except me."

I don't have any money yet, but a month later I bring him the first of it, won betting on football. Six months later he's waiting for me on Fridays with the kettle on. He doesn't know I'm buying him. He thinks he's found a patron.

## После D
The money changer who spat at my feet lends me a hundred dollars at twenty percent a month. He finds it funny: a barefoot old man asking for a loan on his word of honor. I tell him that in two years I'll pay him back a thousand, and he laughs even louder, but he gives it to me.

I rent a corner from an old woman on Krasnaya Gorka, wash stairwells for food, and every month, to the day, I bring the changer his interest. By August '97 I have not a hundred dollars but eight hundred, all in one coffee can under a floorboard. The changer stops laughing and starts saying hello to me.
