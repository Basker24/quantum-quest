An iron fence runs along the soi, a grille with sharp spikes, and its top rail sticks out above the water. You can walk along it holding on with your hands and keep your chest out of the water. I remember where it struck, and I give that pole a wide berth, right along the fence.

I take hold of the rail with both hands.

My fingers clench on their own, so hard my nails dig into my palms through the rust. I can't open them. The fence is holding me, and through the fence, through my hands, straight into my chest, comes that same thing, fine and fast. The fence stands in the same water as the pole. I realize that when I can no longer do anything.

Ploy screams behind me. I want to tell her not to touch me, but only a moan comes out. My jaw has locked; my teeth chatter on their own and ring, like a spoon against a glass on a train. My heart stumbles, and then stops. The last thing is the smell of Mom's coal-dust hair, and I still have time to wonder where it's coming from, here, on a Bangkok fence.

## Пробуждение
…and I'm sitting in the boat by the bridge again, and Ploy is already swinging a leg over the side.

My palms are burning. I open them and look; there's nothing on them, no rust, no wounds. My fingers curl into a fist on their own, and I pry them open with my other hand.

I knew where it struck, and I thought that would be enough. The fence stands in the same water as the pole. The whole street stands in the same water.
