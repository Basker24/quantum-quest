Our office is on the nineteenth floor, on Kuznetsky Prospekt, with windows facing the Tom. On a clear day you can see my spoil heap from here: a gray cone by Krasnaya Gorka and the smoke over it, white against the gray sky. In November you can see the smoke especially well, because there's snow on the ground all around, and on the slopes it melts in circles.

On Sunday Sonka video-calls from Moscow, like always at eight our time. It's raining outside her window and snowing outside mine. She tells me about her hospital and the waiting list for MRIs, and then she looks at me through the screen for a long time and asks, "What's wrong with you? Are they bullying you on that mountain of yours?" I say I'm the boss myself now, and there's nobody to bully me. "That's exactly what I'm afraid of," she says.

The holding company has new owners now, from Moscow. I've seen them once, on a screen, in a video-call window. Their man in Kemerovo, Arseny Olegovich, is ten years younger than me and wears a sweater that costs as much as my UAZ Patriot SUV. He puts a certificate in front of me: "Fire at the waste rock dump of the Glubokaya mine extinguished. Dump reclaimed." At the bottom is a line for the chief engineer's signature: mine.

"We'll cap it with clay before New Year's," he says. "Lawn on top, birches in spring. The land under the dump has already been bought; there'll be a development, Gorki, a hundred and twenty houses with a river view. This is what you wanted, Andrei Nikolayevich. For it to stop burning."

Under the clay the spoil heap will go on burning for another thirty years. I know it, he knows it, and so does the pyrometer in my desk drawer. Putting it out honestly means taking it apart with excavators layer by layer, flooding it, cooling it: seven years and money the new owners won't spend on it.

"The Kuzbass director's post opens up in January," says Arseny Olegovich, looking out the window. "We need a man who signs. And a house in Gorki, if you like. Front row."

The pen lies across the signature line. Expensive, heavy, with a gold nib. I've never held one like it.

> Can you get everyone out? I'd asked myself that since '09, since my first death at the face. Sitting at a desk, it turned out, you could get nobody out and still sign for everyone.

## После B
I set the pen down on the desk next to the certificate, straight, the way you put down a tool after a shift. Arseny Olegovich looks at it, then at me, and isn't surprised. "A pity," he says. "They'd have appreciated you in Moscow." He takes the certificate with him and in a week finds somebody to sign it.

Only by then it can't be signed. On Monday I hand over the chief engineer's job and stay at the same Kuzbass Decommissioning as an ordinary section chief, taking apart waste dumps, at a third of the pay, and on Tuesday I take a copy of the reclamation plan and my own pyrometer readings, dated and signed, to the regional prosecutor's office. The Gorki development is never built. We take the spoil heap apart for seven years, with excavators, layer by layer, and I see the last smoke over it in the spring of '40.

Vitka comes on the last day, with a bottle, and we drink on the cold rock at the very top. "You put it out," he says. I say I did. Below us is the Tom, and the ice on it is cracking.

## После C
I leave the certificate on the desk unsigned, with the pen across it. I write my resignation by hand on a sheet from the printer and give it to the secretary. That evening Arseny Olegovich calls and says I'm crazy. I say maybe so.

Tanya opens the door of her apartment on Krasnaya Gorka in a bathrobe with a towel around her head. She looks at me, at my bag, at the clock on the wall. "For good, is it?" she says. I say for good. She takes my bag and sets it down in the front hall, the way you set a lamp on the rack.

A week later my deputy signs the certificate. They cap the spoil heap with clay before New Year's and plant birches in the spring. The development is never built on it: the birches go yellow in circles, and the buyers get scared. Every day I see it from Tanya's window, green and warm, and in winter snow doesn't lie on it.
