My cough started in November and still hasn't gone away by February. In the mornings I cough for about ten minutes, sitting on the edge of the bed, and spit gray phlegm into the sink. All the decommissioners cough like that, Gosha says; it's the mine coming out of us. I'm fifty-six, and thirty-six of those years I've spent underground.

::if F_ГИПОКСИЯ
My head aches over my left eyebrow every morning now, in that same spot that remembers the puddle at the face. I'm used to it and walk around with it like a watch on my wrist.
::end

Nika sees me in pulmonology at the regional hospital. She's head of the department now, gray-haired, cropped short, with glasses on a chain. Twenty-five years ago, they say, she didn't leave this place for three days at a stretch when covid came to the Kuzbass. Seryoga is retired; Mishka is with the mine rescue unit.

::if F_В105 == ВГСЧ
He serves where I served.
::end

::if F_В106 == СКАЗАЛ
"Well, hello, Samokhin," she says, looking at me over her glasses. "Thirty years you haven't come to see me. You said what you said at the wedding and disappeared."
::end

::if F_В106 != СКАЗАЛ
"Well, hello, Samokhin," she says. "Get undressed. Don't be shy, I saw you in your underpants back at the backwater."
::end

She hangs my X-ray on the light box and studies it for a long time, tapping her pen against her teeth.

::if ТЕЛ < 30
On the X-ray my lungs are gray, finely speckled, like a spoil heap under the first snow. Nika is silent longer than she should be. Then she takes off her glasses, and they hang on their chain.

"In Novosibirsk they do lung lavage," she says. "One lung at a time, under anesthesia. There's a state-funded slot next year; if you pay, they can do it tomorrow. Only there's no point if you go back down in April."
::end

::if ТЕЛ >= 30
On the X-ray my lungs are spotted, finely speckled around the edges, but clear in the middle. "You'll live," says Nika. "If you stop going down. Dust is slow, Samokhin. You can't make a deal with it at the last minute, the way you like to." She mentions Novosibirsk, where they wash out lungs for money, in passing, like a spa for the rich.
::end

On her wall hangs a glossy promotional calendar from Moscow: "Second Chance. The Siberian Resonance Foundation." Nika catches my look.

"A procedure for people with more money than conscience," she says. "They put something in your blood, and then you don't die when you're supposed to. They offered me a department to run here. I said no. I don't understand what they make it from, and when I don't understand something, I don't take it on."

::if F_В101 == ТАНЯ

::end

## После A
Nika puts me in the ward for three weeks. Bronchoscopy, IV drips, breathing exercises with old women who blow up balloons and curse the government. I blow up balloons with them.

::if ТЕЛ < 30
When I'm discharged, Nika is silent for a long time, holding my chart. "It's anthracosis, Andrei," she says at last. "Dust. I'm not promising you anything." It's the first time she's ever called me by my first name.
::end

::if ТЕЛ >= 30
When I'm discharged, Nika hands me my chart and says, "Don't set foot underground again, understand? Not even on a tour." I promise. The way she looks at me makes it clear how little she believes me.
::end

## После B
"I'm closing the Severnaya in April," I tell Nika. "The last one. Then I'll check in."

She shrugs and prescribes pills I don't take. In April I go down into the Severnaya and seal her shaft with concrete, twelve truckloads. I stand up top by the shaft until the concrete comes up level with the ground, coughing.

::if ТЕЛ < 30
By summer I can't climb to the third floor without stopping. Nika puts me in the ward by force and doesn't joke anymore.
::end

::if ТЕЛ >= 30
In summer Gosha says there's nothing left in the Kuzbass to decommission. I retire on my own, without a cake, with one certificate of merit, and hang it in the toilet.
::end

## После C
I write my retirement application the same day, in the hallway of the regional hospital, on my knee. Tanya reads it over my shoulder and corrects one comma.

That evening she comes to my place with a bag, sets her slippers in the front hall and hangs her quilted jacket on the hook, the same one from the lamp room. In the mornings she makes me drink some herbal brew, foul as water from the mine sump. A month later I cough for five minutes in the morning instead of ten. Six months later, two.

::if F_В101 == ТАНЯ

::end

::if F_В101 != ТАНЯ

::end

## После D
I dictate the application to Gosha over the phone, right from the hospital hallway. Gosha breathes heavily into the phone for a long time. "And the Severnaya?" he says. "Who's going to seal her, me?" Turns out it will be him. I don't call the guys, not one of them, because I don't know what to say to men I've ridden the cage with for thirty years.

I sell the garage within a week, along with Dad's keys on their nail, the jars of bolts, the workbench where Dad gave me Granddad's storm jacket. Everything else goes to the neighbor at half price. Tanya is the last to find out, from that same neighbor.

"You didn't even ask me," she says at the station. "Thirty-six years, Samokhin. I'd have come with you."

::if ТЕЛ < 30
In Novosibirsk they wash out my left lung first, then three weeks later the right. Cloudy gray water comes out of me by the liter; the nurse holds the jar up to the light and says, "Well, there's a miner for you." By summer I'm climbing to the third floor without stopping. The cough stays, but I don't sit on the edge of the bed for ten minutes in the mornings anymore.
::end

::if ТЕЛ >= 30
In Novosibirsk they wash out one lung; the doctor advises leaving the other alone, it'll do. Gray water comes out of me into a jar; the nurse holds it up to the light and says, "Well, there's a miner for you." I almost stop coughing.
::end

I come home to Rudnichny in September. I call Tanya from the front hall; she answers on the fifth ring and talks to me politely, like a neighbor.
