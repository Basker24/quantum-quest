Last spring we took out a mortgage on a two-room apartment in Lesnaya Polyana, ninth floor, twenty years. Twenty years seems to me like a sentence you could serve and walk out of. Our windows look out on pine trees, the elevator works every other day, and Varya, who's two, calls it "the elevator-schmelevator."

::if F_ПАРТНЁР == НИКА
Last fall Nika's father retired from his institute in Akademgorodok, sold his garage, and bought a dacha in Yagunovsky, "closer to the granddaughter." My mother-in-law travels back and forth to Novosibirsk and calls it "living for two families."
::end

::if F_ПАРТНЁР == ОЛЯ
Olya's father spent forty years in a crane cab at the coking plant, and he built the dacha in Yagunovsky himself, log by log. The banya too, the steam bathhouse out back, and he's prouder of that than of his granddaughter.
::end

For the Eighth of March, Women's Day, we're invited to my father-in-law's dacha. The women are in the house, Varya is asleep on top of the big brick stove, and my father-in-law and I are in the steam room of the bathhouse for the third time. Outside it's twelve below, the snow blue in the moonlight. He stokes it his own way, hot and without a break, and he doesn't trust anybody else with the damper.

The coals are smoldering in the stove, and little blue flames run over them, light as on a gas burner. My head is heavy, my temples are pounding, like I've just worked two shifts back to back. My father-in-law sits on the bench all crimson, smiling at the wall.

::if РАЗ >= 35
All through second year at night school they drilled carbon monoxide into us. Blue flame over the coals, a heavy head, cherry-red cheeks. I passed that exam with a B, and now I'm sitting here trying to remember what came next on the exam card.
::end

::if ЭМП >= 40
My father-in-law talks slowly, almost syllable by syllable, and laughs at the wrong moments, at the birch whisk. I've never seen him like this, not even at the wedding, where he drank more than anyone.
::end

::if ДАР >= 20
The back of my neck goes cold, exactly like that time at the corner of the apartment block, with the stroller.
::end

"Andryukha," says my father-in-law, waving at the flue. "Shut the damper. Or all the heat'll go."

## После B
I kick open the door to the changing room, then the one to the outside. The frost bursts into the steam room in a white cloud. My father-in-law is heavy, wet and slippery as a burbot, and doesn't understand what I want from him. I drag him under the arms, he braces his heels against the threshold, and we both fall in the snow. The snow hisses under him.

He throws up in the snowdrift. Then he sits up, shakes his head and asks, "What's got into you, Andryukha?" My wife runs out of the house in rubber galoshes on bare feet, my mother-in-law behind her with a towel. The ambulance from Kemerovo takes forty minutes. The doctor takes his blood pressure and says that ten minutes more and we wouldn't have woken him.

"How did you know?" my wife asks that night.

"Blue flames over the coals," I say. "It was on the exam."

> Carbon monoxide has no smell. To this day, in every banya I walk into, the first thing I do is sniff the air, and people laugh at me.

## После C
I step out into the changing room, then onto the porch, and sit down right in the snow in nothing but a towel. The frost hits my head like a bucket of ice water, I feel sick, and I sit there until my head starts working. It works slowly.

::if ДАР >= 20
The chill at the back of my neck goes away as soon as I'm out in the air, and that calms me down.
::end

About three minutes later I realize my father-in-law hasn't come out.

He's lying on the lower bench with his cheek on the wet boards, and he isn't snoring. I drag him out into the snow by the legs and yell toward the house, and my wife, in galoshes on bare feet, does CPR the way they taught her in the course, counting out loud. The ambulance from Kemerovo takes forty minutes. The doctor doesn't even open his bag.

We bury him on Wednesday. At the wake my mother-in-law tells everyone that I pulled him out and that none of it is my fault. People feel sorry for me. Nobody asks why I walked out of the banya alone and sat on the porch for three minutes while he lay in there. I keep waiting to wake up on the bench in the steam room and see him wave at the flue again. That happens in dreams. It doesn't happen here: I wasn't the one who died.

> The gift only looks after me. I understood that that night in Yagunovsky, and too late.

## После D
I remember wet boards under my cheek and the snoring stopping. So I don't think. I kick the door open, grab my father-in-law under the arms and drag him outside into the frost, and he swears and grabs at the doorframe.

In the snow he throws up. Then he sits in the snowdrift, red, steaming, grumbling that I've let all the heat out and now the banya has to be fired up all over again. My wife runs out in her galoshes. I stand over them and don't say anything. I'll never be able to explain to anybody how I know the way people fall asleep in there.

The chill at the back of my neck lets go as soon as my father-in-law starts cursing.

"My old man got gassed the same way," he tells my wife, "in sixty-eight, in Granddad's banya out near Izhmorka." My wife nods; she's known that since she was little. But at the wedding my father-in-law told me his father drowned in the Tom, fishing, and showed me the spot.

## После E
I remember a rented one-room place by the train station, a gas water heater on the other side of the wall, and a hand on the blanket that wouldn't lift. I've never had a water heater like that. The blue flames over the coals flicker exactly like those reflections on the ceiling, and I lose count of them at seven.

So I get up, kick open the door to the changing room, then the one outside, and yank the damper all the way open, burning my palm. My father-in-law is heavy, wet and slippery as a burbot. I drag him under the arms, and we both fall in the snow. The snow hisses under him.

He throws up in the snowdrift. Then he sits up, shakes his head and asks, "What's got into you, Andryukha?" I sit next to him in nothing but a towel, watching the snow hiss under him, and I can't answer. The one in the one-room place by the station was lying there alone, and there was nobody to drag him out.
