I fly to Moscow on an earlier flight and walk his route an hour ahead of him: taxi, the bus from Domodedovo, the crossing by the hotel. An hour is a lot; I win it only by dying more often and calling him more often. Kolka comes home from the Olympiad with a certificate and without a single scratch.

After that I can't stop. Every time they leave the house it's a road, and on every one I have to be a minute ahead. I die on the black ice by Varya's institute in Akademgorodok. Under a train at the crossing by the dacha. In the stairwell of Kolka's building in Moscow. I lose count after three hundred.

They're all alive. Not one of them has ever ended up in the hospital. But with each of my deaths the world slides by a hair, and in three years the hairs add up to a whole life. My wife loves melodramas she used to switch off. Kolka doesn't remember how we went fishing, because in his life we never went. Varya calls me by my name and patronymic when she's angry, and she's angry often, and I don't remember her ever doing that.

In October '38 I find my wife on the stairs, between the fourth and fifth floors, on the windowsill. Her face is gray, yellowish. I get into the car to drive ahead of her and realize there's nowhere to drive. This isn't a road. This misfortune has no minute in which I can die instead of her.

The last map hangs in my garage, the whole wall, and there's no room left on it. I sit in front of it on a stool, and in the house behind me live people I saved who don't know me.

! Dead end "The Scout." You saved your own by dying for them in advance, until the world drifted and they became strangers. And from a slow misfortune the gift doesn't save you. My Life shows where you could have stopped.
