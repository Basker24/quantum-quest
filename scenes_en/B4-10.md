The casino downstairs has been closed for three months now, since March, the zone is empty, and Lera goes to her shift in the empty hall out of habit: she wipes down the felt, counts the chips. Her shift ends at five in the morning. She comes up without changing, in a croupier's white shirt and black vest, sits on the balcony floor with her back to the railing and drinks coffee, and I sit next to her. Below us the casino sign goes out, letter by letter. The mountains around are pink, as if they'd been kept under the lamps all night too.

We've been sitting like this for four years. She says it's the best time of day: the players have gone, the sun hasn't come yet, and nobody owes anybody anything.

"Andrei," she says. "I'm twenty-eight. I want to leave."

She says it calmly; she prepared for this a long time ago. She says she knows where I go at night when Shadrin calls. She doesn't know what exactly I do there and doesn't want to know. She knows that I come back, and every time with a face as if I'd left a piece of myself somewhere and can't remember where.

"To Biysk, to my mom," she says. "Or to your place, in Kemerovo. Anywhere they don't shoot at the table. I'm not asking you; I'm telling you. You decide."

## После A
"Let's go," I say. "Now."

Lera sets her coffee down on the balcony floor and looks at me as if I'd said something in a foreign language. Then she gets up and goes to pack a bag, still in her croupier's shirt. We leave at seven, along the Chuysky Trakt, and in Biysk I call Shadrin from a gas station.

Shadrin listens in silence. Then he says, "Very well. I'm letting you go. But you'll call me, Andrei Nikolayevich. You always call." I hang up and throw the SIM card in the trash can by the pump, and Lera laughs like a girl, loudly, for the first time in three years.

In the autumn I buy a house on the Tom near Yagunovsky, on the high bank, overlooking the water, without haggling, for cash from a gym bag. There's enough money for the house and to not work for ten years. Lera walks around the empty rooms counting them out loud, like chips, and loses count twice. She never once asks where the money came from. In October she plants wild roses by the porch, three bushes from Biysk, from her mother's fence, and says it smells here like at her grandmother's in the village, of coal and river.

## После B
I tell her everything, starting with the cold room. The chick in the cowboy hat, the dog on the cable, the roof over Vitka. The basement in Novokuznetsk and Tyoma from Osinniki. The chair with its back to the kitchen. I tell her how many times I've died, and that I remember every time down to the last second.

Lera listens without interrupting. The sun comes out from behind the mountain, and the sign below goes out completely. When I stop, she's silent for a long time, looking at her hand with the scar.

"I knew you weren't lying about luck," she says at last. "I'm a croupier, Andrei. For ten years I've been watching people who are lucky. You're not lucky. You just have somewhere to come back to, and they don't." She takes my hand. "Let's go. Only don't go there anymore. And if you do, come back to me."

She picks the house near Yagunovsky herself, in October, out of the six they show us. She walks through all the rooms and stops in the one with the window onto the Tom. "You can see from here," she says. "If you go there again, at least I'll be looking that way." She counts Shadrin's money like chips from someone else's table and puts it away in a drawer; she doesn't take a ruble for herself. She plants the wild roses by the porch on the very first day, before the furniture, with her bare hands, swearing at the thorns like at players who take too long to bet.

## После C
"Go," I say. "Alone. It'll be better for you."

Lera nods. She was expecting this. She finishes her coffee, sets the cup on the railing and goes inside. An hour later she comes out with a bag, kisses the top of my head, like a little boy's, and says, "I'll leave you my scar. As a keepsake. Kiss whoever you like."

I call Shadrin that same evening and say I'm staying. "I knew it, Andrei Nikolayevich," he says. "You always stay." From that summer on I go in first once a month, though before it was once every three. Shadrin's meetings are in cafés, in bathhouses, in conference rooms with windows onto courtyards. Sometimes I sit down, order tea and manage to finish it. Sometimes I die first, and then tell Shadrin who's standing in the kitchen, and he raises two fingers.

I buy the house on the Tom near Yagunovsky anyway, in November, from a listing Lera once showed me on her phone and then scrolled past. Huge, six bedrooms. I live in one. Nobody plants wild roses by the porch.
