"Come on, driver, forgotten how to use a shovel?" I haven't forgotten. I've just spent two years looking at this hole from above.

::if F_В403 != ВОДИЛА
This is the third summer Vitka and I have been digging this seam. The pit in the birch grove by the stream has become like a garage to us: our own hooks on the props, our own mug on a nail, our own mattress in the niche where we eat lunch. Over the entrance to the drift Vitka nailed a sign from a tram: "Exit on Request." We crack up at it every morning.
::end

Our drift is forty meters long, low; you can only walk bent over. The props are aspen posts with crossbars from the same birch grove, every meter and a half. Vitka is at the very end, at the face, hacking the seam with a pick. His flashlight jumps around down there like a firefly in a jar. I'm by the shaft, shoveling coal into sacks and hooking them onto the bucket. Up top, on the winch, is Shurup. You can't see him, only the rope twitching.

Above my head, between the third and fourth props from the shaft, something clicks. Short, like a snap of the fingers. I raise my lamp. A crack is running along the crossbar, thin, white on black, and fine grit is trickling out of it in a stream, like sand in an hourglass.

::if F_В403 == БАТЯ || F_Т08 == ПАЛЫЧ
It's shooting. First it shoots, then it rustles, then it comes down. They told me that; I just didn't think it would be so fast.
::end

It clicks again, farther in, by the fifth prop. Vitka at the face doesn't hear; he's got the pick. The ladder with the rebar rungs is behind me, and above it a square of white August sky. By the wall at the shaft lies a spare prop, larch; Vitka has been dragging it around since spring "just in case."

## После A
I climb the rebar, the rungs cut my palms, and halfway up I yell down, "Vitka! Up! It's coming down!" There's no answer from below; the pick is knocking down there. Shurup grabs me by the collar and yanks me out onto the grass.

The roof comes down while I'm still lying on the grass. The sound comes from below, from under the earth, as if a huge dog had sighed in its sleep. A cloud of black dust rises out of the pit, slowly, and stands over the birch grove. Shurup says "shit" and sits down next to me.

It takes five hours to dig Vitka out. Shurup and me, two of the Kiselyovsk guys and the men from the next pit over, with shovels, hands, hard hats. He's lying in the niche under the mattress, alive, in an air pocket by the face, legs under the rock. When they bring him up in the bucket, he looks at me with white eyes and says nothing. At the hospital in Kiselyovsk they pin both his shins. We tell the ambulance he fell off a tractor.

> I shouted to him from the ladder that day. Shouting from a ladder is something I've always been good at; I get that from Dad.

## После D
I remember how it goes: first it shoots, then it sighs, and running after the sigh is too late; it'll catch you about twelve meters on. Where I remember it from, I don't know; I've never worked a longwall. But my legs are already running down the drift, bent over, my hard hat knocking against the crossbars.

Vitka raises his lamp at me: "What?" I grab him by the collar of his overalls like a kitten and drag him back toward the shaft; he stumbles, swears, drops his pick. On the ladder I shove him up ahead of me, in the back, in the backside. The roof sighs when we're halfway up, and a cloud of dust catches up with us from below, warm as breath.

Up top Vitka coughs on the grass and looks at me like a man with a third arm. "How'd you know?" he asks. I don't answer. The sky above me is the same as a minute ago, only the birch by the pit seems to be standing a step to the left. I look at it until Shurup starts yelling about the bucket.
