::if F_Т01 == СПОР
Halfway there I pass Vitka, for the first time in my life, and I even have time to feel glad about it. By the pilings the water turns heavy, like a wet blanket, and somebody takes me by the legs, calm as anything, like they own the place, and pulls me down. I kick, and it holds on and takes its time. The last thing I see above me is Vitka's heels. The heels are kicking back toward the shore.
::end

::if F_Т01 == БУБЛИК
Bublik squeals and slaps at the water, claws right across my arms. I grab him by the scruff and shove him toward the shore with both hands, as hard as I can. He scrambles out onto a rock and shakes himself off all over Nika. And the current turns me around and lays me down under the snag, gently, the way Mom puts me to bed.
::end

::if F_Т01 == ЧАСЫ
The shiny thing under the water turns out to be closer than it looked. I reach out, grab something cold and heavy on a metal bracelet, and squeeze my fist shut. And then the snag catches me by the shorts, and I can't get back up. I could push off with both hands, but then I'd have to open my fist, and for some reason I don't want to.
::end

::if F_Т01 == ДОЛГ
I make it about twenty steps along the bank, and then Nika screams in a voice that isn't hers. Vitka the Dolphin has gone in after the puppy, and he's thrashing by the snag, and his head keeps going under. I don't remember how I got into the water. I remember pushing Vitka up from underneath by his wet back, and him grabbing the snag and climbing up right over me, over my head, like I'm a ladder.
::end

The snag rips my palm open from the thumb all the way to the wrist. I feel it even through the cold. The last air comes out of my mouth in big bubbles, and the bubbles run up toward the sun, and I stay down. Then the cold goes away.

Under the water it's dark and warm, and I'm not alone. I can't see anyone, but there are lots of them. It's like the bus in the morning, when you close your eyes and still know there are people all around you and everybody's shoving. Somebody's breathing right in my ear, even though there's nothing to breathe down here.

From above, through the water, somebody is counting in a thin girl's voice: sixty, sixty-one… She loses her place, sniffs, and keeps counting.

> Later the doctors talked to Mom about hypothermia and the diving reflex. Clever words. I don't remember any reflexes down there. I remember it was crowded.

I'm not scared, honest. It's like hide-and-seek, when whoever's it is already counting and you still haven't decided where to run.
