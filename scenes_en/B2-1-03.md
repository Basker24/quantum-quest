Dad died in December of '20, of covid, in the regional hospital in Kemerovo. Site Nine was under quarantine then; the gates were shut, and nobody was allowed out.

A week before the hospital Dad called me himself, the first time in a year. He coughed into the phone and talked about nothing much, the garage, how the honey mushrooms came up late this year. At the end he said, "I sent you a parcel. A storm jacket. It was Grandma's, from her trunk. Must be Granddad's." The parcel arrived in January, after he'd been buried. I never opened it. It sits in the closet in its postal box and smells of somebody else's mail.

Sonka called me every evening. She's in her sixth year of medical school and since November has been working in the red zone, in the same building where Dad was.

"He's waiting for you, Andryukha," she said, her voice hoarse from the mask. "Well, not waiting, he's on a ventilator, unconscious. But I can see it."

The Choir was silent about Dad. Dad isn't me, and none of mine ever lay in that building. And his death had been building for thirty years, breath by breath, in the coal dust of the Glubokaya; covid just finished it. A death like that has no minute you can grab on to.

> They buried Dad without me. That day I was giving Polina the minutes for when a warehouse near Podolsk would catch fire.

## После B
"Stay," I say.

I talk for a long time, until morning, sitting on the floor by the bed. About how without her, inside this fence, I'll stop being able to tell my own voice from the others. How she's the only one here who remembers me the way I was before the Glubokaya. Nika listens, hugging her knees, and toward dawn she nods.

She never unpacks the suitcase. It stays under our bed, packed, with the sweaters' sleeves tucked in. We sleep in separate rooms now but have breakfast together, and at breakfast she sometimes touches my hand, checking whether it's warm.
