I get home on the thirty-first, toward evening, in a storm jacket, with a beard and a backpack, and Mom doesn't recognize me in the doorway. For a second, no more. Then she does and hits me on the shoulder with a dish towel, hard, and cries, and hits me again.

"You could have called," she says. "Just once."

There's nowhere to call from in the taiga. I wrote. I wrote every week, on pages torn from my field log, and gave them to Miron, and once a month, when Miron drove to Tashtagol, he took them to the post office. There were thirty-two letters.

Dad is smoking on the balcony in an undershirt, though it's thirty below. He sees me through the glass, nods and finishes his cigarette. Then he comes in and shakes my hand like a man off a shift, firm and short.

"Alive," he says.

"Alive."

"Well, that's that."

He sits down in front of the TV. All evening he doesn't ask me another thing.

Sonka is eleven. She follows me around like a tail from the door to the bathroom and from the bathroom to the kitchen, talking the whole time. About school, about her friend Karina, about how the muffler fell off Dad's Moskvich right on Shakhtyorov Street. Then she drags me into her room and pulls a shoebox from under the bed, the box from Mom's boots. In the box are my letters, all thirty-two, in order, tied with a bow made of ribbon.

"Mom reads them and cries," says Sonka. "Then I read them. Then I put them in here. And Dad doesn't read them. Mom tells him, 'Kolya, read them, he's your son.' And he says, 'Alive? Well, that's that.' And goes out to smoke."

I look at the box. The letters lie neatly, but their corners are creased and worn, like old cards that have been shuffled a lot. Thirty-two letters read once each don't get that worn.

"Did you find gold?" asks Sonka. "You promised."

I didn't find gold. I reach into my backpack and take out a rock, gray, the size of a fist, and in it are cubes, even, shiny, yellow, as if they'd been cut with a knife. Sonka gasps and grabs it with both hands.

"Pyrite," I say. "Fool's gold. That's what geologists call it."

"You're the fool," says Sonka, and carries the rock off to the kitchen to show Mom under the lamp.

That evening, before the Kremlin chimes, I tap the rock with my hammer, gently, right on the table, between the Olivier salad and the herring. Mom yells that I'll scratch the oilcloth. The rock rings. Sonka listens with her mouth open. Dad turns from the TV for a second and looks at the hammer.

After New Year's I have to decide. Zoya Arkadyevna wrote in a letter that she'll take me up north, to Yakutia, from the spring, as a field hand. She also wrote: "Or go study, you dope. The polytechnic in Tomsk. They'll take you by correspondence; I'll call." Mom wants me to study. Sonka wants to come to the taiga with me.

## После A
I take my exams in Tomsk in January, in the old building with columns that smells of floor wax and wet fur coats. The mineralogy professor, bald, in a knitted vest, lays five rocks in front of me and asks me to name them. I name all five, and then for some reason tap one with his own pencil and say there's a crack in it. He looks at me over his glasses, the way Gosha did.

"Who taught you that?" he asks.

"Kim. Zoya Arkadyevna."

"Ah," he says. "Well, that explains it. She taught us that way too. Top marks."

I'll study by correspondence for six years, two months a year, and the rest in the field. In Tomsk I walk along the embankment and look at the Tom. Here it's the same as at home, only farther downstream and wider. I stand at the parapet and watch the water until my hands freeze.

## После B
In spring I fly to Yakutsk. The first time in my life on a plane, and the whole way I look out the window. Below: taiga, taiga, taiga, then rivers, white, still iced over, winding like cracks in old enamel. Zoya Arkadyevna sleeps in the next seat with her cap pulled over her eyes.

"Study later," she says when we land. "Study later means never. I know. I used to say it too."

In Yakutsk it's twenty below in April, and the air is so dry your lips crack. They put us on a helicopter to the site. In the helicopter, for the first time in a year, I think about Vitka.

## После C
"Honest?" asks Sonka. "Swear."

I swear on the pyrite. Sonka says that doesn't count; you have to swear on the earth. I kneel in the middle of the room and swear on the earth. Mom laughs in the doorway, for the first time that evening.

In July I take Sonka to Mundybash for a week. Zoya Arkadyevna grumbles that this isn't a kindergarten, and then teaches Sonka to tap with the hammer herself. Sonka taps everything: rocks, the porch, Miron. Miron lets her. When she leaves, he gives her Chalkan's old bridle with a brass plate, and Sonka carries it home to Kemerovo like a medal.

> The bridle hung over her bed when she was thirty and when she was fifty. I saw it when I visited. I didn't visit often.
