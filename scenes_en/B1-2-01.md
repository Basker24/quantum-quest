They gave me the white helmet in 2020. Chief of section seven, forty-two men, two shearers, a face a kilometer and a half from the shaft. On my wrist now there's a tablet in a rubber case that shows gas, air, and the men as dots, who's where. They put this system in after the fire in '21, when the belt in the main drift burned and Palych lay down on the rail at meter one hundred. That night I sat at the dispatcher's on the intercom, counting tags.

On the morning of the fourteenth I'm at the face, by the shearer, with the crew. The tablet on my wrist suddenly starts beeping, short and fast, like an alarm clock somebody forgot to turn off. Carbon monoxide at crosscut number three, forty, a minute later sixty. The crosscut is a kilometer and a half from here, toward the shaft. Beyond it is the old longwall five, worked out, closed off behind a stopping, and coal in its goaf must have started to smolder. It happens: coal in a goaf heats up for years and one day catches fire by itself.

The air current runs from the shaft toward us. Smoke from five will come along it, through the crosscut, into our face, and the crew has only one way out, through that smoke.

The intercom at the shearer is dead; the cable's been cut since the night shift, and the dispatcher can't hear us. Twenty-three men at the face pull on their self-rescuers and look at me. Seryoga, Vitka, young Rustam. Next to us the belt hums, carrying coal to the shaft, past the crosscut, at a good trot. Riding it is forbidden. I've docked men's bonuses for it myself.

"Boss," says Vitka through his mouthpiece. "Well?"

## После A
I lie flat on the coal, face forward, and the belt carries me into the dark. The support arches flash by so low over my head that I press my helmet into the coal. Before the crosscut the belt rises to the transfer point, to the drum, and twenty meters out I get up on all fours, push off with both feet and jump sideways into the walkway. My knee hits a rail, my helmet a prop, but I'm on the floor, and the belt goes under the drum without me.

The door is propped with a prop. I kick the prop out, throw my shoulder into the door, and it shuts with a bang that makes my ears pop. The numbers on the tablet freeze at eighty, then slowly creep down. The smoke goes into the return.

Forty minutes later the crew walks out to the crosscut on their own, all twenty-three, in self-rescuers. Vitka sees my smashed knee and says, "Belt rider," and never tells anybody else about it.

## После B
I run along the drift, over the sleepers, in my self-rescuer, counting sleepers so I won't count minutes. At the six hundredth sleeper smoke starts coming toward me, clear at first, then yellow. My lamp hits it and shines no farther. I walk along the belt by feel, holding on to the frame.

I reach the door in the twenty-seventh minute. I kick out the prop and shoulder the door shut. The smoke is still standing in the drift, but no new smoke is coming. The crew walks out through it in single file, holding on to each other, the way I taught them in drills. Rustam and Vitka bring Seryoga out under the arms. He swallowed carbon monoxide while helping a young guy with his self-rescuer.

Seryoga spends two weeks in the hyperbaric chamber at the regional hospital. Nika, who works there in pulmonology, comes out to me in the hallway and doesn't say anything, just looks. Then she says about Seryoga, "Alive." And, already leaving: "You made it, Samokhin. On foot, but you made it." At the wake for longwall five, when it's finally flooded, Vitka drinks to me first.

## После C
I lie down on the coal and ride. The support arches flash overhead, the belt shudders under me on its rollers. I count the stretches the way I remember them: the third stall, the switch, the distribution point. Past the distribution point a red emergency-stop cord runs along the frame. I sit up on the coal, grab it in my fist and yank.

The belt stops with a screech, fifty meters short of the transfer point, and I climb down along the frame to the walkway like off a stove. Coal trickles down my collar. I walk to the door, kick out the prop and shoulder it shut. The numbers on the tablet freeze and creep down.

Past the transfer point there's a spot where the belt goes under the drum, and now my knees know that spot. A minute ago, for me, it wasn't there.
