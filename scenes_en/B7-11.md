I live in Mom's two-room apartment in Rudnichny now, the one I grew up in. Mom's been gone three years. She went at ninety, in her sleep, and that death didn't have a minute to go back to either. I left her china cabinet where it stood, with the crystal and the documents behind the crystal.

::if F_ЖЕНА == УМЕРЛА
My wife has been gone twelve years. Her cup hangs on a hook over the sink, and I don't drink from it, but I don't put it away either.

This year the Tom has been getting shallower since April. By Rudnichny, stones I didn't know about for seventy years have come up out of the water, and on TV they talk about an anticyclone and spread their hands.

For the May holidays Varya sends Liza to me from Novosibirsk, "for the fresh air." Liza is fourteen and tall, like her mother. At school she's been given a project, "My Family Tree." After the Wager, after those two years when the generals with their Second Chance decided dying wasn't frightening, everything was declassified in '58, and half the class is digging for great-grandfathers in the archives now. The other half argues about how many bridges there are over the Tom in Kemerovo, one or three. The teacher doesn't step in; it's like that everywhere now.

"Granddad, look who I found," says Liza, and puts a printout down on the oilcloth. "Your granddad. My great-great-grandfather."

"SAMOKHIN, Tikhon Savelyevich, born 1930, senior research fellow. Head of research topic TUNING FORK, facility Level 640, Glubokaya mine. Killed 28.01.1986 at 04:12 in a sudden gas outburst."

Below it, in small print, a scan from another file is pinned, from '98: "Blood samples of the carrier (Samokhin A. N., born 1989), 2 units, transferred to the Orpheus Foundation under contract." The carrier is me. The two test tubes the doctor from Tomsk took from me in the hospital when I was seven. Orpheus I remember from the ads in '47; they hung at every bus stop then: a gray-haired man in a good coat and the words "Second Chance."

I never saw Granddad Tisha. He died "at the mine," the way all the granddads in Kemerovo died. I read the time of death twice. At four-twelve Dad woke up every night of his life and went out on the balcony to smoke. And at dawn after graduation my Nokia suddenly showed 4:12, though a second before it had said exactly four.

::end

::if F_Т09 == ДВЕРЬ

Under the text there's a photograph. A miners' choir in a House of Culture, men in identical jackets, and in front of them a man in glasses with his hand raised. His face is like Dad's, only older. I saw a face like that in the shed when I was eleven, in a photo by a river: "T. S. and V. P., the Tom, 1981."

"What's he holding?" asks Liza.

"A tuning fork. It gives the choir its note."

The last page of the printout is a scan from a working log, handwritten, with an ink blot. "Experiment 41. 04:12. The choir is clearly audible. There are more of them than of us. They are counting." And under it, in the archivist's typed letters: "In his working notes, T. S. Samokhin referred to the recorded phenomenon as 'the choir.'"

For sixty-odd years the chill behind my ear had no name. It turns out Granddad had one for it.

When they pulled me out of the backwater, Grandma said about that old man in the striped shirt, "Tisha…?" By then Tisha had been lying in the cemetery under Krasnaya Gorka for ten years. Somebody pulled me out. In 2011 somebody picked Varya up where I'd stopped being.

All my life somebody caught my people when I wasn't there. Maybe it runs in the Samokhin family.

In the other room five-year-old Andryusha, Kolka's son, is sitting on the floor building a dam out of blocks. He's so afraid of water that he won't sit in the bath, only the shower, and only with the door open.

## После A
In July the Tom by Rudnichny is waist-deep where as a kid I couldn't touch bottom. Andryusha and I go in every morning, before it gets hot. The first week he hangs on my neck and squeals, the second he lies on his back on the water while I hold my palm under his shoulder blades and lie that I'm not holding him.

I talk to him the way nobody ever talked to me. Water is just water; it won't do anything to you if you're not afraid of it. In August he dog-paddles five meters to me with his eyes open and comes out on the bank as proud as a cosmonaut. Every week the river pulls back another hand's width.

## После B
Liza and I take the bus to Krasnaya Gorka. Past the museum, past the old waste dumps, stands the Glubokaya headframe, rusty, behind a fence with a sign: "Site declassified. No entry." Liza photographs the sign for her project, and I stand looking at my feet. Six hundred and forty meters below them, somewhere down there, Granddad wrote up his forty-first experiment.

::if ДАР >= 40
The ground under my soles is humming, steady, on one note. Liza doesn't hear a thing.
::end

Liza takes my hand like a little girl, though she's fourteen. "Granddad, your hands are freezing," she says, and it's twenty-five degrees out.

## После C
I fold the printout in four and put it in the china cabinet, behind the crystal. There, against the back panel, lies an envelope with a wax seal marked "For Kolya." Mom put it there in 2000, and in fifty-nine years nobody has opened it. Dad has been gone for thirty-four years.

I put the printout next to the envelope, close the glass door and stand next to it for another minute, the way Mom used to.
