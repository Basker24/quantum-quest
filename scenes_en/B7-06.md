This year Varya demanded we put the tree up in her room. She's eight, she's a grown-up now. On the evening of the thirtieth the two of us decorate it, a plastic tree from the top shelf of the closet, and Varya bosses me around about where to hang the ornaments. I bought the lights at Fix Price, the one-price discount chain, for ninety-nine rubles: Chinese, a hundred bulbs. They blink in eight modes, and Varya picks the dumbest one, "wave."

Before bed I go around the apartment myself: vent windows, range hood, stove. My wife laughs that I'm like an old man. I don't explain. Since that banya I've still got the fumes stuck in my throat.
::end

Since the fall Varya has been propping a chair against her door at night so Kolka can't come in after her with his toy cars. Kolka is four; he comes in after everybody, always. At first my wife and I argued about the chair, and then we gave up.

I wake up at 3:40 to a smell. Burning plastic, sweet and acrid, like at the plant last week when a panel shorted out. My wife is asleep, Kolka snuffling against her side. The hallway is dark, except under the door of Varya's room there's a strip of orange light, too bright for Christmas lights, and gray smoke is creeping out from under it.

I yank the handle. The door gives a centimeter and stops against the chair.

"Varya!" I yell. "Varya, open up!"

Behind the door somebody coughs, thin, a child's cough, and then stops coughing. Something is crackling, maybe the tree, maybe the curtains. Behind me my wife screams my name and snatches Kolka up. Out on the landing, I know, there's a red cabinet with a fire extinguisher; I walk past it to the elevator every day.

## После A
On the third kick the door tears off its hinges along with the chair, and I fall into the smoke. The tree is burning top to bottom like a torch, fire is climbing the curtain to the ceiling. Varya is sitting on the floor under the window, hugging her knees. I rip the blanket off her bed, throw it over her head and carry her out, and while I'm carrying her my sleeves are on fire.

My wife is already calling 112 and pounding on the neighbors' doors. The fire crew arrives in nine minutes and puts out the room in five. They take Varya to the Regional Hospital with mild smoke poisoning, and me with her, with second-degree burns on both arms. We see in the New Year in a hospital room, hooked up to IVs, with tangerines a nurse brought us.

"Dad," Varya says hoarsely, "I only put the chair there so Kolka wouldn't come in."

My arms are in bandages until February. The scars on my forearms will stay as white patches that look like a map.

## После C
…and I'm standing in Varya's room at eight in the evening on the thirtieth, with the string of lights in my hands. Varya is bouncing on the bed, yelling, "Dad, come on, plug them in! Wave!" I remember 3:40, the strip of orange under the door, and how behind it the coughing stopped.

I wind the lights back up, put them in the Fix Price box and say they're broken. Varya bawls, calls me a cheapskate, and hangs her candy wrappers on the tree instead of lights. That night I don't sleep. I sit on the hallway floor by her door, with the chair on the other side, until morning. At 3:40 nothing happens. Only the fridge clicks in the kitchen.

In the world where I didn't get to her through the smoke, somebody carried Varya out. A firefighter, a neighbor, somebody. Again I don't know who, and again I wasn't there.
