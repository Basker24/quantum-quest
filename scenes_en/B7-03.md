Varya was born on the seventh of January, Orthodox Christmas, seven pounds even. My mother-in-law thinks it's a sign. A sign of what, she doesn't say.

This sign screams every evening from six to midnight, steady as a shift schedule, knees pulled up to her belly. The pediatrician from the clinic says it's colic, that it'll pass by three months, and that she needs a walk every day, frost or no frost. We're renting a one-room apartment in Rudnichny, in a five-story block two yards over from my parents'. I'm just off a night shift; my wife hasn't slept in two days.

We bought the stroller through *Iz Ruk v Ruki*, the classifieds paper, green and heavy as a Zaporozhets. Varya sleeps in it in a snowsuit bag; you can only see her nose, and frost is building up on the edge of the blanket from her breathing. For a week there was a thaw, and yesterday it dropped to eighteen below, and everything that had melted froze, hanging on the roofs and lying on the ground.

The pharmacy, a Gubernskie Apteki, is across the road by the bus stop. There are three ways to get there, and I've known all of them since I was a kid.

Along our building, on the footpath the whole yard has polished to a shine. It's the shortest way, and it runs right under the wall.

Along the road, on the shoulder. The sidewalk there is buried, the grader has pushed the snow into a waist-high ridge, and behind the grader the cars are moving, slowly, but moving.

Through the garages. Steam drifts over them from the heating main, the snow there is always half-melted and gray, and it smells like a wet rag.

I stop at the corner of the building and fix Varya's blanket. She smacks her lips in her sleep. Somewhere behind the garages the grader is grinding along, and at the edge of the roof above the footpath something long and clear is hanging, and the sun breaks inside it.

I turn the stroller around and haul it back into the stairwell. It smells of cats and somebody's cabbage soup. I stand by the radiator for fifteen minutes, holding the handle with both hands and watching the door through the cloudy window like it's a TV. Varya sleeps. The grader rumbles past the entrance, and behind it, on the turn, a gray Toyota slowly slides sideways and lies down in an empty snowbank. The driver gets out, kicks a tire and swears loud enough for the whole yard to hear.

At home I leave the stroller in the front hall, sit down on the floor next to it without taking off my jacket, and stay like that until my wife comes out to ask what happened. "Nothing," I say. "I got the Espumisan." I've just died, and then I was standing at the corner of the building alive, a minute before. That doesn't happen, but here's the pharmacy receipt in my fist.

At night, when Varya finally falls asleep, I lie there and think, not about myself. Where I stopped being, she was left alone in the cold.

## После E
I turn the stroller away from the wall and push it straight into the deep snow, across the yard, past the buried slide and the iron mushroom over the sandbox. The snow is knee-deep, the wheels sink, and the stroller goes like a tractor through a plowed field. Varya sleeps. The woman on the fifth floor watches me from her vent window like I'm an idiot: there's a path right there.

Behind me, by the wall, there's a short crack, and then a heavy, crunching blow, like somebody dropped a sack of glass off the roof. I don't turn around until I reach the bus stop. An icicle is lying across the footpath, broken into three pieces, each the size of my leg. The woman in the window crosses herself.

At the pharmacy I buy Espumisan and, for some reason, a bottle of green antiseptic. I go back through the deep snow too, in my own tracks. At home I leave the stroller in the front hall, sit down on the floor next to it without taking off my jacket, and stay like that until my wife comes out to ask what happened. "Nothing," I say. "I got the Espumisan." The pharmacy receipt is in my fist.

At night, when Varya finally falls asleep, I lie there and think about who stopped the stroller that was rolling downhill, toward the road. The woman from the bus stop, the driver of the little PAZ bus, a neighbor in track pants? Somebody caught it, and I was lying on the ice and didn't see who.

## После G
I push the stroller through the garages, along the most trampled path, step for step behind somebody in size-twelve felt boots. Steam hangs over the heating main, the snow is gray and soft. I see the smooth white patch from a long way off: round, even, without a single footprint, though everything around it is trampled. I go around it along the garage wall, and my shoulder comes away covered in rust.

On the way back I carry a stick I picked up by the trash bins. I poke it into the smooth patch, and the stick goes into the snow all the way, along with my arm up to the elbow. Warmth and wet-rag smell come up from below. I set an empty diesel drum from the end garage over the hole and go home.

At home I sit down on the floor in the front hall next to the stroller, without taking off my jacket. My wife asks what happened. "Nothing," I say. "I got the Espumisan." The next day the drum is still standing over the manhole, and people have already trampled a path around it.

At night I think about who heard Varya crying by the garages, over the round hole in the snow. I'll never know. Somebody heard her, and I was lying down below, on the pipes, and couldn't answer her.
