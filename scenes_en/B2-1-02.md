In two and a half years I bring them two hundred and ten dates. Shadrin calls them dates, though all I have are minutes: forty, thirty-five, once fifty-two. The Choir never hears further ahead than a little over forty.

Minutes, it turns out, are a commodity too. In the office of the deputy director, Remezov, there's a whiteboard on the wall with columns in marker: number, minute, place, "probability," client. The clients are marked with letters. "I" is insurers; forty minutes is enough for them to do something with their paperwork. "M" is some people with the stock market. The letter "X" Remezov writes with particular pleasure, with a flourish. Once I saw two men in identical jackets with identical haircuts come for that letter.

Warning anybody is not allowed. Remezov explained this to me in the first week, patiently, as if to a freshman. If you warn, the disaster doesn't happen, the forecast isn't confirmed, and the client doesn't pay. "You're a physicist, Andrei Nikolayevich. Measurement changes the system. We simply don't measure anything we don't need to."

Lunyov was retired in '18, with a certificate of merit and a bouquet of carnations. He went back to Tomsk and hasn't called since. I've filled his notebook; I'm keeping a second one now, in the same columns.

> Shadrin signed Lunyov's certificate. Lunyov, they say, hung it at home in the toilet, facing the door.

On Sunday Mom mentions on the phone, in passing, that Dad quit smoking in '98, "when you were nine, don't you remember?" All my life Dad smoked at night on the balcony, at 04:12, and I fell asleep to the smell of his Primas. I tell Mom I remember.
::end

Today the Choir spoke up on its own, in the daytime, while I was standing in line in the canteen for meatballs. One of mine is riding a commuter train into Moscow. In his world I must live in this village and commute to work like everyone else. The train slams into a fuel truck that has stalled on a level crossing. The whole first car burns at once. I stand with my tray listening to him scream, and the meatball on my plate gets cold.

He names the crossing himself. Kilometer forty-seven, by the platform, six kilometers from our fence. There it happened at eleven. Here it will be at eleven-forty.

Remezov hears me out, nods and writes on the board: "211. 11:40. Xing km 47." After a moment's thought he puts an "I" in the last column. Then he picks up the phone and says to me, covering the receiver with his palm:

"Thank you, Andrei Nikolayevich. Get some rest."

The clock over the board says 11:02.

"112, go ahead."

"Kilometer forty-seven, the crossing by the platform. At eleven-forty a fuel truck will stall on the tracks. Stop the train."

"Who's calling?"

I hang up. At eleven-forty nothing happens. In the evening Remezov comes to my room himself, which he's never once done. The train was stopped a kilometer before the crossing, after a call, he says. The fuel truck stalled on the crossing, the driver was pulled out, nobody was hurt. The client is unhappy. Remezov stands in the doorway and looks at me for a long time, like at a thermometer that's lying.

"You've been getting things mixed up lately, Andrei Nikolayevich. Get some rest."

## После B
I go to my room, lie down on top of the blanket and stare at the ceiling. At 11:42 sirens come through the open vent window. They come from the direction of the station one after another, and I count them until I lose track.

In the evening the news reports six dead at the crossing. A bonus lands in my account; I see the text and don't open it. In the notebook I write line number two hundred eleven. I leave the column "what I heard" empty.

## После C
I'm allowed outside the fence until six in the evening; Remezov calls it "a walk." The guard at the checkpoint has a bicycle, an old Ural with no fenders. I say I'll bring it back in an hour, and he waves his hand.

Six kilometers along the forest road I pedal so hard I taste iron in my mouth. I reach the crossing at 11:31. The fuel truck is already crawling up to the barrier, slowly, in jerks, its engine sputtering. I drop the bike and stand in front of the hood with my arms spread. The driver slams on the brakes and leans out the window, red-faced.

"Are you nuts?!" And another word, the one Mom used to slap me on the mouth for when I was little.

The fuel truck's engine dies right there, twenty meters from the rails. At eleven-forty the train goes by, horn blaring. The driver watches it go and silently crosses himself. In the evening Remezov tells me, without raising his eyes from his papers, "You went for a bike ride, Andrei Nikolayevich. Around kilometer forty-seven. Don't take a phone with you anymore. No need."
