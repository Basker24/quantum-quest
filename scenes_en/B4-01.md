Welder sits under a Pepsi beach umbrella in the middle of his scrap yard, in a leather jacket, though it's thirty degrees. He never takes the jacket off; in our yard they say he sleeps in it. Around him are mountains: radiators, pipes, bathtubs, somebody's bed with brass balls on the posts, a length of rail. On the scales a woman in a housecoat is weighing a cart of aluminum saucepans. Welder looks at the saucepans, as if he can't see the woman, and names a price without once glancing at the display.

I stand in front of him in my graduation jacket, because I don't have another one. It's too big for me; the sleeves are rolled up.

::if F_Т04 == ЦВЕТМЕТ
"Samokhin," says Welder. "I remember. You and the freckled one hauled cable for me in '98. Copper, clean. The cops chased you down by the depot."
::end

::if F_Т04 != ЦВЕТМЕТ
"Samokhin," says Welder. "Nikolai's son. From the Glubokaya. Your old man know you're here?"
::end

Dad doesn't know. Dad thinks I'm applying to the mining technical college and that I have a math exam in August. Mom thinks the same, only she's also praying. Sonka is nine; she thinks I've gone to work "for the man with the cars," and on the whole she's right.

::if F_Т09 == ДОЛГ_СВАРНОМУ
Vitka is already here, his second year. He's levering a cast-iron bathtub with a crowbar by the gates and waves his work glove at me. We're working off the debt for his flat feet together, only Vitka is working off his half, and mine is still hanging. Welder remembers that better than we do.

"You've got a debt," he tells me, "and you've got hands. That's good. It's bad when you've got a debt and no hands."
::end

::if F_Т09 != ДОЛГ_СВАРНОМУ
Vitka is serving near Chita and writes that the food's all right. Welder doesn't ask about Vitka. He doesn't ask about many people; he just knows.
::end

He waves me closer, right up to the umbrella. From under the umbrella comes a smell of cologne, iron and sunflower seeds.

"I've got three jobs," says Welder, and counts on his fingers, short and thick. "The scales. There you have to count and not steal: whoever steals from me steals from himself. The yard: hauling. You don't need brains there, you need a back. And the Bychok, with Shurup, round the drop points, collecting iron. There you need a tongue and no chickening out. I pay the same everywhere, not much. Whoever shows what he's made of, I'll pay a lot later."

Shurup, his driver, is smoking on the step of a blue Bychok truck and spitting into the dust. He's about thirty, missing a front tooth, and he looks at me the way you look at a puppy someone has brought home without asking.

> Welder said back then "I'll pay a lot later," and he didn't lie. He rarely lied; he had no need to.

## После A
The booth by the scales is iron; inside it's like an oven. On the table is a graph-paper notebook, a thick one, ninety-six sheets, with columns ruled in: "Date," "Who," "What," "Weight," "How much paid." Welder taps it with his fingernail.

"You write everything down. Even if I said 'don't write it.' Especially then."

By evening there are forty-two lines in my notebook, and in four of them "who" is recorded as "cop," "cop," "old man from Vesennyaya St., Semyonov," "cop." I write and think that this, it turns out, is why Mom made me form my letters nicely in the copybook.

## После B
The first week I haul radiators and think I'm going to die. A cast-iron radiator with eight sections weighs as much as Sonka, only Sonka can be talked into walking by herself. By Friday the blisters on my palms swell up and burst, and the scar from the snag on my right palm shows white among them like an old path.

::if F_Т09 == ДОЛГ_СВАРНОМУ
The work gloves Vitka gave me smell of Vitka.
::end

::if F_Т09 != ДОЛГ_СВАРНОМУ
They issue me secondhand gloves, gone stiff, with a hole at the thumb.
::end

On Friday Welder sticks five hundred rubles in my pocket and says, "There's a back. Let's see if there's a head."

## После C
Shurup and I drive around the private houses beyond Rudnichny, from Krasnaya Gorka to Yagunovsky. I knock on gates and say, "Got any metal? Old iron, radiators, batteries, we pay well." We don't pay well. Old women bring out washtubs, Shurup gives them fifty rubles each and says, "Thanks, mother."

On the third day an old woman brings us a Kama bicycle, rusty, with a basket. "My grandson's," she says. "He doesn't ride anymore." Shurup tosses it into the truck bed without looking. The whole way back I look at the basket and don't ask why her grandson doesn't ride anymore.
