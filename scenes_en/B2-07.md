In March of '15 Gleb didn't come back. One morning he just didn't show up to the seminar, and by lunchtime the police called. I'm not going to say any more about it.

::if F_В206 == ПОМОГ
I taught him to listen. Every night when I can't sleep, I remember him nodding and writing it down.
::end

::if F_В206 == СДАЛ
Lunyov took his keys away and forbade him to work. It didn't stop Gleb, and Lunyov knows that better than I do.
::end

::fate F_В206 == ВРАЧ
He'd stopped the pills a month before. People told me I'd done everything right; it didn't make it any easier.
::end

All that year without Gleb, Lunyov hardly sleeps. His hair has gone white, and he spends nights in the department, on a camp bed behind a cabinet. The kettle in 218 never gets cold anymore.

In March Lunyov takes me to Kemerovo. Under Krasnaya Gorka, at the Glubokaya, they're opening up a mothballed level for him. The money came from some foundation called Siberian Resonance. Its curator, a polite man of nearly seventy in a good coat, shakes my hand and calls me "a young man with an interesting heredity." His hand is dry and warm, and he holds mine a second longer than he needs to, as if taking my pulse. His name is Shadrin.

The cage goes down six hundred and forty meters. My ears pop somewhere around four hundred; a lit landing floats past in the dark, with two men in helmets smoking on it and not looking at us. Down below it's warm as a banya; the rock gives off heat here, and it took six months to pump the water out of the old drift. Granddad's chamber sits on a rise; the water never reached it, only silt dried by the far wall. Lunyov stands over it for half an hour without a word. The chamber is small, lined with lead, and its walls are frosted, because a new refrigeration unit is humming in the corner. On the floor stands a medical cooling bed, still in its plastic, and next to it a heart-lung machine that looks like a Soviet washing machine.

On the wall, in almost-erased chalk: "41. 28.01.86. 04:12." I look at it and hear the crackle of the Svema tape in 218 and that tired voice: "Synchronize watches. Four-twelve." Lunyov runs a finger under the numbers without touching the chalk.

"Forty-one," he says.

::if F_Т01 == ЧАСЫ
I look at my watch. As always it says four-twelve, and here, down below, for the first time in my life it doesn't seem funny.
::end

"Your grandfather failed because he started as an adult," says Lunyov. His voice is even, like a man who has rehearsed this a hundred times. "Your tuning fork was struck in childhood. Here's the protocol. Eighteen degrees, twelve minutes of arrest, start at four-twelve. At that hour the body is at its coldest, and the fork is easiest to hear. That was the only time your grandfather went down. If it works, you'll know everything you only hear now."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then you'll come back." He looks at me over his glasses. "You always come back."

Hospitals do this too, only they cool you for hours, through a machine, with a team of surgeons standing around. Here it will be Lunyov, the machine and me. According to every textbook, you can't do this. Lunyov says Granddad didn't read textbooks; he wrote them.

Somewhere above us, one level up, a shift is working right now, and Dad is there. He doesn't know I'm here. If he did, he wouldn't let me.

## После A
"All right," I say.

Lunyov nods, takes the plastic off the bed and sets a date: the fourteenth of April, four-twelve. For a month I sleep badly and wake up every night at exactly the right time.

## После B
"All right," I say. "But Nika will be at the monitor."

Lunyov winces, but agrees. Nika comes from Novosibirsk a week early, spends half a day silently reading the protocol, and then says, "You're both sick." And stays.

## После C
"No," I say, and walk to the cage.

Lunyov doesn't argue and doesn't even turn around. I go up alone, and all the way, six hundred and forty meters, I hear the refrigeration unit humming down there with him.
