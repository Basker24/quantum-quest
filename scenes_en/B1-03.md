I don't tell anyone about November. Mikhalych keeps quiet too; only at New Year's he brought a bottle of Belochka vodka and a jar of pickled mushrooms to the locker room, set them on the bench and didn't leave for a long time. Seryoga tells everybody that Samokhin has a nose like a rat, and rats are the first to leave a sinking ship. That's his way of praising me.

Apparently I can't manage dying. I remember how I died, and I carry that with me to work. Sometimes at lunch I suddenly feel coal between my teeth and spend a long time washing it down with the sweet stewed-fruit drink.

A little after four the gas detector on my belt, the small box everybody has to carry, starts beeping. It's beeped before, at the Glubokaya it beeps all the time, but now the lights go out at the drive and the conveyor stops. The ventilation stops. The face goes quiet, so quiet you can hear water dripping from the roof. Forty meters behind me Seryoga is swearing, his lamp darting around the walls; he's yanking at something by the drive and can't let go of it.

Methane has no smell and you can't see it. That's the first thing they tell you in training and the only thing I'm sure I remember right now. It's light, and it collects under the roof. It's eight hundred meters to the shaft along the main drift, and the air there has stopped just like here. To the right is the old crosscut, a short passage to the next drift, half filled with fallen rock.

## После A
I run. Eight hundred meters along the drift, over the rail ties, with the self-rescuer in my mouth, and at five hundred my knees start arguing with me for real. I don't argue back, I keep running. My body's strong; it can take this.

At the shaft a mine rescuer in a mask catches me by the shoulders and sits me down on a rail. Half an hour later guys in masks bring Seryoga out, alive, angry and without his helmet. He sits down next to me and says, "You rat, you could've at least yelled for me." I give him my water.

## После B
In the crosscut I find the door in the stopping and remember which way it opens before I have time to get scared. The door is jammed; I hit it with my shoulder, and on the third try it gives. A cold draft comes through the gap, a real fresh current from the next drift, and I sit there until the rescuers come.

They bring Seryoga out the same way, through our crosscut. On the way he trips over my leg, sees me and says, "Oh. You're alive." As if we'd run into each other in the canteen.

## После C
I go into the old incline, where nobody goes. My legs carry me by themselves. After about a hundred meters damp air brushes my face, and I come out at the pump station, where there's a separate pump and its own ventilation pipe, old, Soviet, that nobody ever switched off. You can breathe here.

I sit by the pipe as if by a stove and think that somebody stood here before. Somebody I don't know, who headed for the shaft and didn't make it. This is his chill. The rescuers find Seryoga by the drive; he put on his self-rescuer and lay down like they teach you, and nothing will happen to him except a reprimand for abandoning his helmet.

## После D
I head back toward his lamp. Seryoga is sitting by the drive, ripping the self-rescuer off his face: he thinks there's nothing in it to breathe, and he's yelling that he's suffocating. I sit down next to him and talk, slow and boring, the way Mikhalych tells jokes: "Seryoga. Breathe. There's air in it. Breathe in. Come on." And I hold the mouthpiece to his mouth until he starts breathing on his own.

Then I drag him under the arms to the shaft. He's heavy, but he walks on his own feet; he just keeps sitting down. At the shaft he tells the rescuers, "Samokhin got me out," and then to me, quietly: "I owe you, got it?" I say I've got it.

## После E
Methane is light, it's up under the roof, there's less of it down low. I lie face down on the wet floor, pull my jacket over my head and breathe through it, slowly, as if asleep. After an hour my head starts to buzz; after two I forget how much time has passed. I remember a puddle under my cheek and wanting very badly to sleep.

They bring us up after six hours. I throw up twice right in the cage, my fingers won't work, and Seryoga unties my laces for me in the bathhouse. The doctor at the first-aid post shines a flashlight in my eyes: "Hypoxia. You'll live." For a month afterward my head aches in a particular way, in one spot over my left eyebrow, like there's a nail in it.

## После H
I remember an elevator full of nitrogen, though I've never been stuck in any elevator full of nitrogen. I remember sitting down on the floor because it was more comfortable, and not being scared. There, the cold lay low. Here, at the Glubokaya, methane collects up top, under the roof, and sinks slowly, a centimeter at a time, and the gas detector on my belt beeps faster and faster.

So I go bent double, almost on all fours, face to the floor, holding the gas detector in my hand down by my boots. Down low it beeps less often. I crawl to the crosscut, feel my way to the stopping and the door in it, and hit it with my shoulder, lying down, from below. On the third try it gives, and a cold draft comes through the gap, a real fresh current.

The rescuers find me by the gap an hour later. They bring Seryoga out the same way, through our crosscut, and he asks why I'm lying there like a dog under a fence. I don't know how to explain to him that in another life I died sitting on a floor in Tomsk.
