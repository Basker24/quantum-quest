Vitka is eighteen, and the draft office has remembered him. His notice says the twentieth: two years of service, start to finish. Vitka walks around the yard with his head shaved and acts like it's all a big joke.

That same week Nika comes. It's her last summer before senior year, and her parents let her visit us "one last time, then it's college applications." The braces are gone. She's cut her hair short, and now the old ladies in our yard don't recognize her right away.

And Welder, who runs the scrap yard on Shakhtyorov Street and half of Rudnichny on the side, tells Vitka by the kiosk that the army thing can be "sorted." Not for free, of course. "You'll pay me back later."

These days Dad comes home from his shift and goes straight out to the garage. He sits there till late. Mom says, "Go sit with him. He won't ask you himself."

On Friday Vitka catches me outside our building, already shaved, his head blue from the cold water. "Andryukh," he says. "Maybe screw the army? Welder said he'd sort it." He looks at me like it's my call and not his.

In the evening Nika phones, on our home line. On Saturday she's going out to her aunt's dacha; from the porch there you can watch the sun come up over the Tom, and it's her last sunrise here. "Come with me," she says. "Or don't." And she hangs up without waiting for an answer.

The send-off party is on Saturday. And on Saturday Dad will be sitting in the garage alone, and he won't call me himself. Mom's right about that.

## После A
The whole yard throws the send-off: an accordion, vodka, Vitka's mom wailing out loud. At two in the morning Sanyok, who lives in the next stairwell over, pulls up in his dad's Lada Nine and says let's go for a drive out to Pritomsky while Vitka's still not a soldier. Vitka climbs in front, I open the back door, and the back of my neck goes so cold that I shut it again.

"Chickenshit," says Sanyok, and the three of them drive off.

Half an hour later the Nine is lying in the ditch near Pritomsky with its wheels in the air. All three climb out by themselves, through the windows, covered in blood and glass. Vitka is holding his arm and laughing like a lunatic. The back seat, where I would have been sitting, is crushed flat to the floor.

Toward morning Vitka, drunk, shaved, with his arm bandaged, hugs me and says into my ear, "Listen. Don't leave my mom on her own."

In the morning the bus from the draft office takes him away. I wave until it disappears around the corner.

## После B
Welder sorts out the army in a week: at the draft office they suddenly discover that Vitka has flat feet. We don't have the money, so Welder says, "You'll work it off."

We shake his hand, one after the other. His palm is soft and warm, and for some reason that's the scariest part.

## После C
Nika and I take the commuter train out to her aunt's dacha and watch the sun come up. We talk till morning about everything except the send-off.

In the morning I see her off at the station. Vitka's send-off happened without me, and from the army he doesn't write to me for a long time. The first letter only comes a year later.

## После D
Dad and I sit in the garage until late at night. He drinks vodka, I drink Kolokolchik soda. After the second bottle Dad suddenly says, looking at the wall:

"Your grandpa didn't work at the mine. He worked under it. There was a door down there."

I wait for him to go on. He's quiet for a long time, and then he says, "Go to bed."
