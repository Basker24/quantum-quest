A week before his stroke Dad brings an old canvas storm jacket to my garage, with a patch on the elbow. He says he found it in Grandma's trunk when he was clearing out the top shelves.

"Must be Granddad's," he says, and lays it on the hood. "Grandma kept it all her life. Let it be yours."

Why, he doesn't explain. A week later, on Saturday morning, Mom calls me and is silent on the phone, and I understand everything before she starts talking.

The stroke is on the left side, a massive one. At the regional hospital, in neurology, they give Dad a bed by the window, and from the window you can see the spoil heap of the Glubokaya, which was shut down last year. His right arm doesn't move, the right half of his face hangs like it belongs to someone else. He can hardly speak. The words come out of him one at a time and with effort, as if he were sending them up top from the face, lump by lump.

Sonka flies in from Moscow on the third day, in an expensive coat, and immediately starts fighting with the doctors, because there's nothing else she can do. Mom sits by the bed holding Dad's good hand.

On the fifth night he wakes up at four-twelve, and so do I, in the chair by the bed. He turns his head toward me and beckons me closer with his good hand, two fingers.

"Under Krasnaya Gorka," he says. Each word on its own breath. "Granddad's there." He breathes for a long time. "Don't open it."

Krasnaya Gorka is where the Kuzbass began, on the Tom, the first mine, an open-air museum where they took us in fifth grade to look at the coal carts. I sit and wait for him to say more. He closes his eyes and holds on to my sleeve.

> The doctors said later that with a stroke like that he couldn't have said that many words in a row. Mom didn't argue with them.

I count on my fingers and lose track.

Dad listens with his eyes closed, and then lifts his good hand and shows me on his fingers how many he has. Four. Then he makes a fist and shows four more. Eight. He smiles with half his face, and it comes out like a boy who's beaten his big brother at something.

He dies the next night, at four-twelve. They call me from the nurses' station, but I'm not asleep anyway.

## После B
"Dad, what's there? Under the Gorka?"

He opens his eyes and looks at me, long and angry, the way he did when I was little and went for an outlet.

"Don't," he says. "O-pen. It."

I ask again, and again. He turns away to the window, to the spoil heap, and doesn't say another word, not even to Mom. He dies the next night, at four-twelve, and right to the end he lies turned away.

## После C
The letter has been in my wallet for seventeen years, folded in four, the wax all crumbled off. I put it in Dad's good hand, and his fingers close on it by themselves.

He lifts the sheet to his eyes, though he can't read anymore. His lips move. I know which line. "You're lucky, son, and it isn't luck." Dad knocks twice on the bedside table with his knuckles, very quietly, knock on wood.

He dies the next night, at four-twelve, with the letter in his fist. In the morning the nurse gives it back to me, crumpled and warm. I smooth it out on my knee and put it back in my wallet.

## После D
Tanya comes a little after seven in the morning, straight from her shift in the lamp room, which has been moved to the Severnaya now. She smells of electrolyte. She sits down on the edge of the bed, and Dad looks at her for a long time.

"Grisha," he says. And then, with effort: "Sorry. I. Got out."

Tanya takes his hand, the good one, and holds it. "You got out, Uncle Kolya, and that's good," she says. "Dad would've been glad." Then she sits with him until lunch, and I stand in the hallway by the window looking at the spoil heap. Dad dies the next night, at four-twelve.
