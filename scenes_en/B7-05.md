We got the Lada Granta on credit the spring before last, silver, straight from the factory, and I still wipe the dashboard with my sleeve. Kolka was born in October, nine pounds even, and he hollers in a bass voice, like his grandpa. In January, over the holidays, we're driving him to Novosibirsk to show him to the family.

::if F_ПАРТНЁР == НИКА
We're going to Nika's mother, who's only seen her grandson on Skype and cried every time that he's "the spitting image of a Samokhin." Honestly, that flatters me.
::end

::if F_ПАРТНЁР == ОЛЯ
We're going to Olya's godmother, Aunt Galya, who baptizes, reconciles and matchmakes the whole family, and without her blessing nobody in that family so much as gets a cat.
::end

Kolka is asleep in his car seat in the back, my wife next to him. Varya is five; she sits in her booster seat watching *Smeshariki*, the cartoon, on the tablet with the sound off, because the sound will wake Kolka. We've been on the Novosibirsk highway for two hours, and for the last twenty minutes there's been a semi in front of us.

The semi is long, with a trailer, and snow dust comes up off its tarp at us, white and thick. The wipers can't keep up; there's nothing to see ahead but the tarp with the ad on it.

Sixty kilometers an hour. A straight stretch all the way to Yurga, a dashed center line, no oncoming traffic for a long time.

"Dad, are we gonna pass it?" Varya asks without looking up from the tablet.

::if F_ПАРТНЁР == НИКА

::end

::if F_ПАРТНЁР == ОЛЯ
"Andryush," Olya says from the back, "just don't speed. Aunt Galya can wait. She's been waiting forty years."
::end

## После B
I take my foot off the gas and settle in behind the semi, about fifty meters back. Sixty it is. Varya sighs the way only five-year-olds sigh and buries herself in the tablet again.

A couple of kilometers later a KAMAZ truck bursts out of the white cloud ahead, in the oncoming lane. It's passing somebody of its own, right down the middle of the road, and it roars past us so close that the Granta rocks in the wind. I watch it in the mirror and start counting. The semi's speed, the length of its trailer, how many seconds I would have been short. It comes to about three.

In Yurga we stop at a roadside café. Varya eats dumplings with sour cream, my wife feeds Kolka, and I sit over my tea and can't seem to finish it.

## После C
I put on the turn signal and pull off at the gas station by the turn-off. Varya goes to the restroom with her mom, Kolka wakes up and demands to be fed. I drink coffee from the machine, bitter as diesel, and watch the semi head off down the straight in its cloud.

Twenty minutes later we drive on. Fifteen kilometers on, blue lights are flashing on the shoulder. A KAMAZ is lying on its side in the ditch, and in the oncoming lane stands what used to be somebody's Lada Nine. A traffic cop waves his baton at us: keep moving, don't stop. I drive past slowly.

My wife doesn't say anything all the way to Novosibirsk. Varya asks why the truck is lying on its side, and I tell her it got tired. Somebody else went to pass today instead of me.

## После D
I remember two headlights at the height of my head and the steering wheel in my chest as clearly as Dad's garage. So I slow down to forty and hug the right edge, right up against the snow ridge. The semi pulls away ahead.

A minute later a KAMAZ bursts out of the white cloud in the oncoming lane and roars past us, so close I can see a little icon on the dashboard in the driver's cab. The Granta rocks. Varya looks up from the tablet and asks, "Dad, what's wrong?"

"Nothing," I say. "Cramp in my leg."

At the gas station in Yurga I get out to wipe the headlights. My Granta is cherry red. I picked the silver one myself, the spring before last, at the factory, and argued with my wife, who liked the cherry one. My wife rolls down her window: "Why are you petting it? You'll freeze."

## После E
I remember fog on the R-255, which I've never had in this life, and a gray wall across both lanes, a wet tarp with the letters "…TRANS," and a drop on an iron beam. I remember a crunch inside me, long, like footsteps on a snow crust. My hands on the wheel remember being forty-four and alone in the car, and I'm twenty-six, and in the back Kolka is snuffling in his car seat.

I take my foot off the gas and settle in behind the semi, about fifty meters back. Sixty it is. Varya sighs the way only five-year-olds sigh. A couple of kilometers later a KAMAZ bursts out of the white cloud in the oncoming lane and roars past, so close the Granta rocks.

"Why are you so white?" my wife asks from the back.

"Thinking," I say. In Yurga I buy the kids dumplings and can't eat a single one myself.

## После F
I remember those two headlights at the height of my head down to the second. So I sit on the semi's tail and count. When the KAMAZ roars past in the oncoming lane and the Granta rocks, I pull out from behind the semi right away and floor it. Kolka is asleep, Varya is watching the tablet, my wife is holding the grab handle over the door.

The pass is long; the trailer goes on and on like a fence. The oncoming lane is empty, I know that in advance, and I still don't breathe until the end of the trailer. Then we're ahead, alone on the white straight, and my wife says quietly, so as not to wake Kolka, "You're insane." I say it's faster to Grandma's this way. We get to Novosibirsk an hour early, and all evening I can't get my hands warm.

## После G
Now I remember two of them. The first KAMAZ roars past, the Granta rocks, and I don't move, I sit on the semi's tail and count to forty. At thirty-one the second one comes out of the white, just as rust-orange, with the same little icon on the dashboard, as if the first had come back for me. My wife says from the back, "Lord."

At forty I pull out from behind the semi and floor it. The oncoming lane is empty. I don't trust it until the end of the trailer, and I don't trust it for another ten kilometers after. The world I remembered isn't here anymore, and I only recognize this one by my deaths.

We get to Novosibirsk an hour early, and all evening I can't get my hands warm.
