The mound has grown two meters in a month. The locals call them bulgunnyakh, frost mounds; inside is a core of ice, and they stand on the tundra for hundreds of years. This one stood quietly since the last century, and since July it's started to breathe. That's what I wrote in my report, and in Yakutsk they chewed me out for the word: mounds don't breathe. This one does. Every few minutes it rises a little and sinks, and the turf on it cracks at the seams like an old sweater over a belly.

I'm forty-two. This is my twelfth year working on gas in permafrost. When the tundra thaws, gas that has lain under it for thousands of years looks for a way out, and sometimes it finds one all at once. Five years ago, two hundred kilometers from here, a mound like this blew up and left a crater as deep as a nine-story building. We flew in a week later and measured an empty pit. Now, for the first time, I'm standing beside one while it's still whole.

At the top of the mound there's a fresh crack; water seeps out of it and steam rises, though it's plus eight outside. It smells of rotten eggs and swamp. My sensors on tripods stand in a ring, their cables running to the laptop in front of Aisen, the student. Aisen sits on a crate and calls out numbers every minute, faster and faster.

::body seen_life(✝В3-02.C)
I walk around to the mound over the tussocks, though it's shorter to go straight and the water's only knee-deep there. Standing water on the tundra is black, and I don't like looking into it. Sardaana got used to it long ago and doesn't ask.
::end

"Pressure's rising, Andrei Nikolayevich. Methane forty percent at the crack. Forty-five."

Sardaana stands next to me, the tape measure on her belt, that same one, her mother's, with little flowers. We've been together sixteen years now. She's forty-three; we have no children, and we stopped talking about that long ago. She isn't looking at the mound; she's looking at me.

"You're doing it again," she says. "You're standing and listening. I know you. You always stand like that right before something happens."

The tracked carrier is parked below, in a hollow, about three hundred meters away. The wind blows from the mound toward it, steady, from the north. To the right, upwind, is a small lake and a dry ridge beyond it.

## После A
I know this mound the way you know an old dog's temper. Aisen's numbers are racing, and I'm watching how they race. Pressure up, up, then down for a second, as if the mound were taking a breath. I've seen that in the records from the other one, the empty crater, on the graph a minute before the blast. I take the laptop from Aisen and yell, "Everyone to the carrier! Now!" And I run last myself, with the laptop under my arm like a loaf of bread.

The mound blows when we're at the carrier. The ground jerks underfoot, and over the tundra a column of ice, mud and turf rises, slowly, as in slow motion, and falls back in chunks the size of refrigerators. One chunk lands twenty meters from the track. A cloud stands over the crater, white, and the wind carries it toward us. We drive off choking on the stench, with wet handkerchiefs over our faces.

The recording on my laptop is intact, to the last second. Later they'll show it at a conference in Moscow, and the hall will applaud. Sardaana won't fly to the conference.

## После B
"Drop it! Everyone!" I yell, and Aisen drops the laptop in the moss, and we run downhill to the carrier, over tussocks, through water, in boots that squelch. Sardaana runs beside me, silent, the tape measure bouncing against her hip.

The mound blows when we're halfway. Air hits me in the back like a plank, and I fall face first into the moss. Things come flying down around us: chunks of ice, peat, clods of earth. One hits me in the shoulder, and the shoulder goes numb. Then the cloud comes, white, stinking, and we crawl to the carrier on all fours, coughing, in wet handkerchiefs, and Aisen throws up by the track.

The instruments and the laptop stay where they were. Nothing is left of them. Afterward we measure the empty crater, the way we measured that one five years ago, and I write in the report: "Observation interrupted."

## После C
I drive the hammer into the ice right by the crack, pick down, and lay my palm on the handle. The ringing travels through the wood into my hand, my shoulder, my teeth. Aisen looks at me like I'm crazy. Sardaana doesn't look at all; she knows I do this. The numbers race. The ringing grows, grows, gets thinner, and the voices in it get louder, and I start to make out what they're saying. They're counting. They're counting down.

At three the ringing breaks off. I yank out the hammer and yell, "Go!" We run, and I know we have exactly as much time as we need, not a second more. The mound blows behind us as we jump into the carrier. Aisen manages to grab the laptop.

In the carrier Sardaana looks at my hand. My palm is white, bloodless; it's still ringing inside. "What were they saying?" she asks. I don't remember ever telling her about the voices. I've never told anyone.

## После D
I remember walking along the sleepers through gas, in someone else's mine, where I've never been, and how I lay down on my side, carefully, as if onto a sofa. You don't run in gas. Downwind least of all: the cloud will go the same way, into the hollow, to the carrier, and lie there like water in a pit.

"Not to the carrier," I say. "That way. Walk. Wet your handkerchiefs."

We walk, upwind, around the lake, up to the ridge. Aisen carries the laptop. Sardaana looks at me and asks nothing. The mound blows when we're on the ridge, and from above we watch the white cloud flow down into the hollow and cover our carrier completely, roof and all. It sits there for an hour. Then we go down and start the carrier in wet handkerchiefs.

On the ridge, while we wait, I look at the lake. It's round as a saucer. This morning, when we arrived, I'd have sworn it was long, like a cucumber. I say nothing.
