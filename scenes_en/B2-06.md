Three more years go by on trains between Tomsk and Akademgorodok. I defend my thesis, become Lunyov's grad student, and turn into the strangest grad student in the department. Lunyov now has two "instruments": me and Gleb Rostotsky.

In November Sonka comes to Tomsk for two days, for a chemistry olympiad. She's sixteen, with bangs over her eyes and a backpack full of tangerines from Mom. I give her my bed and sleep on the floor on my jacket. In the evening she sits on the windowsill, looking at the Tom, and says she'll never be a physicist. "I'm going to medical school," she says. "You're always climbing where you shouldn't. Somebody in this family has to know how to resuscitate."

Gleb is twenty-four, from Leninsk-Kuznetsky, thin and sharp as a compass, and about three times smarter than me. At nineteen he was in a crash on the highway near Yurga. His mother was beside him, in the passenger seat. They got him breathing again after six minutes; they couldn't save her. Lunyov says Gleb's grandfather was entered in the "Registry of the Lucky" back in my grandfather's day. Granddad kept a list like that: people who survived where nobody survives. "Your father's in there too," says Lunyov. "Number one hundred seventeen. And in the margin, in Granddad's hand: 'Do not recruit. Mine.'" Gleb doesn't like to hear about it.

He hears the Choir, like me, only in snatches.

::if ДАР < 20
He hears it, to be honest, better than I do. I only hear my Choir clearly when Lunyov presses the tuning fork to my temple, like that time in 218.
::end

He's found his own way to live with it: he's looking for a world where his mother is alive.

In November he shows me an Excel spreadsheet on his laptop. Dates, places, marks: "rewind 1," "rewind 2," forty rows in all. Behind every row is a neighboring world where they buried Gleb.

"I'm getting closer," he says, and smiles in a way that makes me want to take his laptop away. "Yesterday in Kemerovo… well, in Shcheglovsk… oh, never mind. It doesn't matter. I need to go further, Andrei. You know how to go further. Teach me."

My city is called Shcheglovsk only in history textbooks: in 1932 it was renamed Kemerovo.

## После A
I teach him what I learned myself: don't chase the voices away, listen, tell one from another. Gleb writes it all down in a notebook in tiny handwriting and nods. A month later he says he can hear "the one who's driving." I don't ask who he means.

## После B
"You can't bring back the dead, Gleb," I say. "Not in any world."

He closes the laptop, thanks me politely and leaves. The next day he says hello to me as if nothing had happened. That's worse than if he'd been offended.

## После C
I go to Lunyov and tell him about the spreadsheet. That same evening Lunyov takes Gleb's lab keys away and forbids him to work until spring.

Gleb finds out who did this to him and stops talking to me.

## После D
I take him to a psychiatrist at the regional hospital, almost by force. Gleb doesn't resist; he just looks out the window of the minibus and says nothing. The doctor prescribes pills. For six months he takes them faithfully, and for six months I think it's all turned out all right.
