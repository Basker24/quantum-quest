Vitka puts the chopper down on the ridge. I understand that later; right now all I understand is that I'm lying on the wall of the cabin, and the wall has become the floor, and through the windows over my head I can see fog. A minute ago there was a roar, then a shriek, then an impact so hard my teeth clacked and I bit my tongue. There's blood in my mouth.

Vitka has been flying up north for seven years, a captain now, and this is the third season in a row he's flown us, from the base to the site and back. He says he flies us as a favor. In Yakutsk he drops in on Sardaana and me for tea every time, kicks off his regulation tarpaulin boots in the hallway, toe against heel, without bending down, sits on a stool and talks about engines the way other men talk about women. They issued him boots two sizes too big, and he wears them anyway; with a foot wrapping, he says, they fit just right.

::if F_Т09 == ДОЛГ_СВАРНОМУ
He started flying late, at twenty-three, after Welder. "I hauled bathtubs for you," he says every time, "now you ride a jump seat in my cabin. That's fair."
::end

::if F_Т09 != ДОЛГ_СВАРНОМУ
He went into flying straight after the army, and in his very first letter he wrote that the food at flight school was worse than near Chita, but the view from up top made up for it.
::end

It smells of kerosene. Then it starts to smell of something else, burnt rubber and paint. From above, through the cabin ceiling, from under the cowlings where the engines are, smoke is coming, gray, lazy; it creeps along the wiring and gathers in the corner like water in a bucket. Up there something is crackling, quietly, as if someone were crumpling foil in their fist.

Sardaana sits against the opposite wall, holding her left arm with her right and looking at it in surprise. Her arm is bent between the elbow and the wrist, where it doesn't bend. Two of our workers, Timur and Vanya, are stirring among the backpacks and the core boxes. The flight engineer, Lyokha, lies by the cockpit door face down and isn't stirring.

Vitka shouts from the cockpit. He shouts calmly, the way you shout from a balcony that dinner's ready:

"Andryukh! My legs are pinned! Kostya got out through the window, he ran for the first-aid kit! Andryukh, you alive back there?"

On the wall by the cargo door hangs a fire extinguisher, red, in clamps. The cargo door is above us now, like a hatch, and it has to be opened by hand. The cockpit door is next to Lyokha, narrow, warped. The smoke from the ceiling is already coming down to my shoulders.

::if F_В302 == МОЛОТОК
My hammer is on my belt, the one from the golets, with the melted pick. It's humming, barely audible, on one note. I want to rip it off and throw it out the window.
::end

> How many seconds I had, I learned later from the investigation report. At the time I just counted, like when I was a kid jumping off the garage.

## После D
I push Sardaana up and out, then Timur, then Vanya, then climb out myself and drag Lyokha after me by the collar; he's heavy as a sack of core. We run down the slope, over wet reindeer moss, a hundred meters or so. I sit down on a rock, and I'm shaking. Sardaana holds her arm and looks at the chopper; she doesn't look at me.

Kostya, the copilot, runs around the cockpit with the first-aid kit and doesn't know what to do with it. Then he drops the kit, climbs into the blister window feet first and fumbles in there for a long time. The smoke from under the cowlings turns black. Kostya climbs out, hauls Vitka out by the arms, halfway, and Vitka screams so that my ears ring even a hundred meters away. Then the two of them fall into the moss. A minute later the cowlings flare up, yellow, hot, and the chopper burns until evening.

Vitka survives. In Yakutsk they spend four months putting his legs back together, and he'll walk with a cane. He has burns on his arms and neck, up to his chin. When I come to see him in the hospital, he looks at me and turns to the wall. He doesn't say "you abandoned me." He doesn't say anything at all.

> Kostya got a medal afterward. I drank with him once, in '35, in Khatanga. He didn't recognize me, and I didn't remind him.
