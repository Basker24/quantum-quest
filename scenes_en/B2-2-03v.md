Walking the embankment isn't enough. I figure that out by fifty-two, in June, in my twenty-fifth summer at School No. 41. I make it in time to the places I walk to. But people drown where I don't walk: at the Yagunovsky quarry, at the dachas below Metallploshchadka, in the Yaya, where people go on weekends.

::if !F_ТИМУР
At the end of May Timur comes into my classroom with a cake. He's twenty-four, he drives a minibus, and he still calls me "Andrei Nikolayevich" across the desk. The cake is for my birthday, even though my birthday is in February. "I never did learn to swim," he says, and laughs. "What for? You're always around."
::end

::if F_ТИМУР
At the end of May Gennady Ivanovich, the gym teacher, mentions in passing in the smoking room that Timur couldn't swim. The school pool closed in 2010, and his mother wouldn't let him go to the river. I stand there with an unlit cigarette and think that for twelve years I didn't know that.
::end

A week later Dimka Shmelyov catches me by the OSVOD hut at Pritomsky. I taught him in Seventh Grade B; now he watches the boat station and wears a bunch of keys on a cord, the way Mom used to wear her pass. The hut was boarded up while I was already here, back in the thirties, when the last boat was written off.

"This is for it," says Dimka, and puts a key in my palm, rusty, with a tag. "Teach my Yegor, Andrei Nikolaich. My old man never taught me; he was inside."

Yegor is ten. He stands behind his father in shorts and rubber flip-flops and looks at me the way kids look at a teacher in summer, that is, with suspicion.

"So then what'll we be?" he asks. "Vodyanoys?"

> It took me until fifty-two to work it out. Granddad would probably have said that for a Samokhin that's quick.

::if F_ПАРТНЁР == НИКА
In the evening Nika works it out on the calculator on her phone, for a long time, moving her lips. "There are forty thousand kids in this city," she says. "Twenty a summer. That's about fifteen years' work for you, Samokhin. No longer than you've been walking that embankment."
::end

::if F_ПАРТНЁР == ОЛЯ
In the evening Olga takes a class register out of the wardrobe, Soviet, blank, in an oilcloth cover. She's kept it since teacher's college, not knowing what for. On the first page she writes in her neat teacher's hand: "Lists." And says nothing more.
::end

::if F_ПАРТНЁР == НЕТ
In the evening Sonka calls from her ambulance shift; there's a siren wailing on her end. I tell her about the key. "You teach them to swim," she says, "and I'll teach them to bring people back. But properly, Andryusha, on a dummy, not the way you like doing it."
::end

On the fourth day, at five in the morning, Yegor is standing alone by the hut, waiting. He has his flip-flops in his hand.

## После A
The permit for the club comes through two months later. The hut, it turns out, is a capital construction site, the Tom water has to be tested for E. coli, and the lifeguard has to be certified. I find a lifeguard at the boat station, through Dimka, in a red cap with a crossword.

The first thing I teach them isn't swimming but lying. First they lie down on the water and lie there until they believe the water holds them. Many of them don't believe it for a week. Yegor believes it on the second day, and after that he lies on his back in the middle of the backwater by the hut, arms spread, looking at the sky until I call him.

A year later there are thirty-one names in my register. Not one has drowned. I check it every summer, the way I used to check the embankment.

## После B
I open the hut the next morning with a crowbar, because Dimka's key only fits the padlock, and the inside lock has rusted through. At six three kids come. A week later, twelve. A month later there are forty kids by the hut, and mothers sit on the parapet with thermoses.

The city Telegram channel writes that at Pritomsky a retired teacher is drowning children without a license. The local police officer comes, young, with a tablet. He watches for a long time as Yegor swims breaststroke to the buoy and back, and then asks what time we're on tomorrow. His daughter can't swim either.

## После C
::if F_ПАРТНЁР == НИКА
Nika stands on the bank with a stopwatch around her neck and times how long each of them can stay underwater with their eyes open. "I don't let anyone past thirty seconds," she tells me in the evening. "One record holder on this river is enough for me."
::end

::if F_ПАРТНЁР == ОЛЯ
Olga sits on the bank in a hat and writes them down in the register, by surname, with dates of birth and notes on who's afraid of what. Yegor is first on her first page. Next to his name she's written: "Afraid of being laughed at."
::end

The two of us go there every morning, all summer. Me in the water up to my waist, her on the bank. In the evenings we come back along the embankment, and she holds my arm, and for the first time in many years I don't look at the piers.

## После D
Sonka comes in July for two weeks and brings a dummy from Novosibirsk in a gym bag. The dummy's name is Gosha; he has a blue rubber face and a click in his chest when you press right. The kids fight over who gets to bring him back.

Sonka teaches them to count out loud, thirty compressions and two breaths. I stand next to her and listen to ten-year-olds counting to thirty in chorus, and something in my chest clicks, like in Gosha's.

## После E
I never do open the hut. The tagged key hangs on a nail in my hallway. In summer I sleep three hours a night and get up when someone in the Choir starts drowning ahead of time, minutes ahead, on the neighboring forks. Then I take a taxi.

I make it in time to the Yagunovsky quarry, to the Yaya, to the pond in Lesnaya Polyana. The boys I pull out look at me like I'm crazy. Yegor doesn't say hello to me anymore when we meet. Dimka says he calls me "the Vodyanoy who drowns them himself."
