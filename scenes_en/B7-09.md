I have twelve smoke detectors in the apartment. I counted. Two in every room, one in the hallway, one in the kitchen over the stove, one in the bathroom, though there's nothing in there to burn. There's also a carbon monoxide detector, a leak sensor under the washing machine, and a breaker box I can switch off from my phone at the plant. They all blink green, and at night, when I can't sleep, I lie there looking at the green dot on the ceiling.

::if F_Т08 == ДАТЧИКИ
Since I was sixteen I've known that people tape over sensors so they can make quota. Nobody's taping over mine.
::end

"Call me when you get there," I say to everybody, even Gena when he walks home from his shift, three blocks. Gena says, "Andryukha, you're sick," but he calls.

Varya is twenty-two. She's finished university and is leaving for Novosibirsk, for Akademgorodok, the science town, for a lab that took three people from her whole year. She's driving her own car, down that same highway, through Yurga. Her suitcase has been standing in the front hall since last night, with her backpack on top.

::drift F_В706 == ЗНАНИЕ
Last night at the farewell dinner my wife told Varya's boyfriend how she and I met. She told it cheerfully, in detail: the skating rink by the Drama Theater, where I supposedly knocked her off her feet. I don't remember anything like that. I didn't correct her, because everybody at the table was nodding, even Kolka, who wasn't born yet.
::end

Kolka is seventeen; he writes something for neural networks and laughs out loud at my sensors. "Dad, your apartment's like a spaceship, it just doesn't fly." My wife doesn't laugh. Two weeks ago in the kitchen she said to me quietly, so the kids couldn't hear, "You hold on to everybody like a stroller on the ice. With both hands. She's twenty-two, Andrei."

I never told her about the stroller on the ice.

Varya is standing in the front hall in her sneakers, waiting for what I'll say. On my phone I've got an app open that can put a tracker on her phone, and then I'll be able to see her dot on the map the whole way, right up to Akademgorodok, and after.

## После A
I close the app and put the phone in my pocket. I hug Varya, and at first she stands there stiff, waiting for a catch, and then she hugs me back. I don't say "Call me when you get there." I want to say it so badly my jaw aches.

She leaves at eight. I sit down on the stool in the front hall, my wife sits next to me and takes my hand. We sit like that until the phone rings. It's Varya, from Yurga: "Dad, I'm in Yurga, they've got dumplings. Don't worry." I'm not worried, I say, and I'm lying.

## После B
"Either the tracker or you don't go," I say.

Varya goes pale, then red, and yells that she's twenty-two, that she's not a stroller, and that I'm sick, like Gena would say. In the end she installs the tracker herself, out of spite, right in front of me, and slams the door so hard the hallway detector goes off.

That night I lie there watching her dot crawl along the highway through Yurga. My wife sleeps on the couch in the living room. In the morning she says, "You're not saving her, you're locking her up," and leaves for work without breakfast. Varya doesn't call me for two months.

## После C
That night, after Varya leaves, I sit down with my wife in the kitchen and tell her everything. About the corner of the apartment block, about the stroller rolling toward the road, and about how I was standing there alive a minute before. About what came after. I talk and wait for her to call an ambulance.
