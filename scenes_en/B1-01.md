I sat through two years of mining college for one piece of paper, and in September of '09 they hand it to me. With it I go to the Glubokaya mine, to Dad's crew, as a trainee coalface miner. A coalface miner is the man who cuts coal at the longwall face. His trainee hands him the wrench, hauls props and tries to stay out from under his feet. That's exactly what it says in my work record book now, and Mom holds that book in her hands all evening like a diploma.

A morning at the mine starts in the pithead building: first the bathhouse, where you leave your clean clothes and put on overalls stiff with yesterday's dust, then the shift office, where the foreman reads out who goes where, then the lamp room. The lamp room looks like a ticket window at a train station. Behind the window are racks where hundreds of lamps sit charging in their slots, each with its own number. You hand a tin tag through the window and get a cap lamp and a self-rescuer, a metal canister on your belt that gives you half an hour of air when there's nothing around you to breathe. After the shift you hand in the lamp and take back your tag. By the tags on their hooks you can see who's come up top and who hasn't.

The lamp-room girl is called Tanya. She's twenty-two, her bangs are dyed eggplant purple, and to everyone who hands in a lamp she says the same thing: "Brought it back. Good job." On my second day I ask, "And if I don't?" and get smacked on the helmet with the issue log. My tag is number two hundred forty-six; Tanya punched it for me herself and said it's a lucky number, because its last owner retired alive.

"Don't joke with her like that," Dad says later in the smoking room. "Her old man, Grisha Laptev, was down there with us in '05. His lamp sat on the rack for four days."

He finishes his cigarette and doesn't say anything else. Of those twelve, he was one of the last to come out alive.

They pair me up with Seryoga Pakhomov from Beryozovsky, four years older than me. Seryoga eats pork fat with sugar, laughs like a horse, and knows everything about the mine except why he works in it. The boss at the face is old Mikhalych. He's been underground forty years, since '69, his hands look like roots, and he tells his jokes in rotation, the same one every third shift.

The first time, the cage, the iron box of the mine lift, drops so fast my ears pop and my stomach stays up top at the level of the bathhouse. Thirty men stand in it shoulder to shoulder in silence. Down below it smells of wet rock and machine oil. The light from my lamp jumps around the walls, and I can't make it hold still.

Underground you can't be alone; everybody who can be bothered explains that to me in the first week. You have to stick with somebody. Up top too, as it turns out.

> Some nights I still wake up at exactly 04:12. Dad doesn't sleep either, on the other side of the wall; I hear him open the balcony door. We never talked about it, not then and not later.

## После A
I tag along after Dad everywhere, and he doesn't chase me off. Underground he talks more than he does at home in a week, and every word is about the job. "Listen to the roof. Before it comes down, it always talks." I listen to the roof, and it says nothing.

At the end of the month we come up together, and in the bathhouse he scrubs my back hard with a washcloth, like when I was little, without a word. The men laugh. Dad doesn't even look at them.

## После B
Mikhalych takes me under his wing on the third day, after I drop a prop on my own foot. "Listen here, Samokhin. Every safety rule is written in blood, but not always the right blood." What that means he doesn't explain, and I don't ask.

He does teach me the signs that the seam is getting ready to blow: the coal crackles like dry firewood in a stove, fine grit trickles out of the face, and you feel a cold draft. "When you hear it shooting, don't stand there, get out. Don't argue with it."

## После C
I hand in my lamp last, slowly, on purpose. Tanya takes it, wipes the glass with a rag and sets it in its slot carefully, the way Mom puts away the good china.

"What are you standing there for?" she asks.

"Waiting for my tag."

The tag is in her hand. She doesn't give it back right away, and in the second week she also pours me tea from her thermos, strong and sweet like on a train. We drink it in silence, on opposite sides of the window.

## После D
Nika picks up on the second ring, as if she'd been sitting by the phone.

"Well?" she says. "What's it like?"

I tell her about the cage, about my ears, about Mikhalych and his joke. She laughs in the right places, and at the end she says, "Samokhin, you nearly drowned when you were seven. Why would you go somewhere dark?" I don't know what to say, so I tell her another joke. Every call costs me fifty rubles, and by the end of the month my phone is three hundred in the red.

## После E
I ask to transfer to the next crew, to Vitka's, and the foreman looks at me like I'm an idiot: his father is at the face, and he's running off to his buddy. Dad doesn't say anything. In the shift office he stands at the other end and doesn't turn around.

Vitka works fast and sloppy, and I follow him around listening to the roof, though nobody ever taught me to. Once something above us clicks, quietly, like a twig underfoot. I grab him by the collar before I understand why. The roof is silent. Vitka looks at my hand on his collar and asks, "What, again?"

What "again" means, neither of us knows, and we don't talk about it for the rest of the shift.
