The Phuket–Novosibirsk flight takes nine hours, and for nine hours I look out the window at the clouds. The first flight since the Wager, full: old people flying home to bury someone, and young people flying to see whether there's still anyone left. From Novosibirsk to Kemerovo I take the bus, the way I did in '07, only in the other direction. Fifty-four years.

Sonka meets me at the bus station. She's sixty-three, gray, in Mom's headscarf, and she looks at me for a long time, from below, the way she looked at me when she was seven and I came back from the river.

"You came," she says.

She has no other words for me, and these are enough.

::if F_ПАРТНЁР == ПЛОЙ
Ploy stands beside me with two suitcases, looking at Kemerovo as if it were the Moon. July, thirty-four degrees, dust. "Siberia hot," she says, offended. They'd promised her snow.
::end

::if F_МАЛИ
Mali flew with us, took leave from the hospital. She's forty-two; she speaks Russian like a foreigner who learned it from cartoons, and Sonka is crazy about her.
::end

Mom lives in a care home in Rudnichny, in a two-story building that used to be a kindergarten, three streets from our five-story block. She's ninety-five. She sits by the window in a room for two, small, in a flowered headscarf, looking at the street. Sonka says, "Mom, look who's here." Mom looks at me attentively and politely, the way you look at a doctor.

I sit down beside her. She takes my hand, turns it palm up and strokes the scar with her thumb for a long time, from wrist to finger, the way she stroked it in '96, when the stitches came out.

::if F_В512 == ВИДЕО
"You're the one from the video," she says suddenly. "The old man who swims. With the turtle."
::end

::if F_В509 == МАМА
"Were you at the sea?" she asks. "Is it warm there?"
::end

::if F_ПАРТНЁР == ПЛОЙ
Ploy sits down on her other side, and Mom takes her hand too. "And whose are you?" Mom asks. Ploy thinks, and then says in Russian, slowly, "Andryusha's." Mom nods, as if she knew all along.
::end

Outside the sun is setting, and over Rudnichny, low, the comet is already visible. Mom looks at it, then at me, and her fingers on my hand suddenly squeeze, hard, until it hurts. Where she gets so much strength, I don't understand.

"Andryusha," she says, quietly and very clearly. "Don't go to the backwater."

I say nothing. It's been pulling me there ever since the bus. Pulling the way home pulls you. Tonight, they say, the comet's tail will lie along the dry riverbed, right over the backwater, and half the city will go to watch.

"I won't, Mom," I say.

She lets go of my hand and turns to the window. I think she didn't believe me. The canvas storm jacket is in my backpack, folded in four, with the striped shirt under it.

> I lied to Mom about the river all my life, since I was seven and told her I'd never go near water again. This was the last time.

## После A
At the Kuzbass Hotel on Sovetsky I lie on the bed fully dressed, looking at the ceiling. At two in the morning I get up and put on the striped shirt and the storm jacket, the canvas one that hung in my wardrobe half my life on Koh Tao and Phuket. The jacket is heavy and smells of our balcony, though that can't be.

Outside it's hot and dusty, like Pattaya. The comet hangs over the roofs. I walk to the river along Sovetsky, past the bus stop, past the kindergarten where Mom lives now. Her window is dark.

## После B
Ploy isn't asleep. She's sitting on the bed in the hotel, in the dark, watching me put on the striped shirt.

"Water?" she asks.

"Water."

She gets up and puts on her sneakers. "Okay. I come. I don't swim, I watch." We go out onto Sovetsky together, and Ploy takes my arm, the way she held me in the boat at twenty meters.

## После C
I wake Mali at two in the morning. She's sleepy, in Thai pajamas with elephants on them, and at first doesn't understand where we're going.

"To the river," I say. "You wanted to see where I drowned."

She did; she's been asking since she was ten. We go out onto Sovetsky together; Mali carries a flashlight and a thermos that Sonka pushed on her in the evening, "just in case." She says Sonka seems to have known everything. I say Sonka always knew everything.

## После D
I sit by Mom's bed until three. She sleeps, holding my hand, and in her sleep she says something, quietly, with just her lips. Sometimes "Kolya." Sometimes "Andryusha." Once she says distinctly, "Two hundred and fifty-two." That's Nika's, from '96. Nika told the whole hospital back then how far she'd counted, and Mom, it turns out, remembered.

::if lived(В1-01)
She also says, crossly, the way she used to about my hat, "Turn in your lamp, Andryusha. Tanya's waiting." I don't know any Tanya, and all my life I've had only one lamp, a dive light on an elastic strap.
::end

At three I carefully take my hand away. Mom opens her eyes and looks at me more clearly than she has all evening.

"Where are you going?"

"I'll be back soon, Mom."

"Stay away from the water," she says, and falls asleep again. In the corridor I put on the striped shirt and the storm jacket. The nurse at the desk looks at me, at the canvas in the July heat, and asks nothing.
