I don't take the bread. Serafim puts the heel on the windowsill, next to the tuning fork, and leaves.

In the chapel it's dark; one icon lamp is burning. I kneel on the boards. First it's cold, then very cold, then the cold goes away, and it gets nice, warm, like in a bath. I know that's bad. I read somewhere that it happens. But I'm warm, and I can't be bothered to think about it.

The Choir is singing. Quietly, in harmony, all the voices on one note, the way Serafim draws it out before matins. I want to sing along and can't open my mouth; my lips have frozen together. My heart beats rarely, and with every beat more rarely. Between beats I have time to think about salo, about Mom, about Sonka with her purple streaks.

The last beat I hear with my ears, muffled, like a knock on the door of the next apartment. Then it smells of Mom's hair, coal dust, warm and close, as if she'd leaned down to tuck in my blanket.

## Пробуждение
…and I'm standing in the chapel doorway again, and Serafim is holding out the heel of bread in its cloth.

My knees are burning. I look down: felt boots, trousers, ordinary knees under quilted pants. But the skin on them is raw, as if it had just been torn off a frozen board.

The last thing I understood was "warm." The warmth came when I was already dying, and it was pleasant. The bread smells sour and of smoke. Serafim's hand is trembling with cold, and the bread in its cloth trembles with it.
