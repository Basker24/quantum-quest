The boy who was standing off to the side comes closer. A minute ago he was freckled. I blink, and he hasn't a single freckle; his head is shaved bare, he's dark as a little Gypsy, and he looks at me from under his brows. I blink again, and he's ginger and speckled again. The sunlight on the sand jerks too, as if the film had slipped its sprockets: the shadow of the snag lies now to the left, now to the right.

The boy opens his eyes. There's a cut on his palm, fresh, pink from the water, and it's his left palm. I look at my right one, at the scar from thumb to wrist, and then at his left one again, and the cold at the back of my head rises like a column.

Along the bank from the path a woman comes running in a flowered housecoat and flip-flops; one flip-flop flies off, and she keeps running without it.

"Kostya!" she shouts. "Kostenka!"

"Mom," the boy says hoarsely, and reaches out his hand to her, the left one, with the cut.

My mom ran here that day in her work overalls, from the processing plant, with coal dust in her hair. I remember that with my body, the way you remember where the light switch is in your apartment. This woman smells of onions and washing powder. She falls to her knees, grabs Kostya and shouts something at me about thank you, about God, about who I am. I stand up, and my knees crack.

I walk toward the willows, in a wet striped shirt, barefoot. In the shade the seam is still holding, and through the leaves I look for the night of 2061, my own, with the comet and the fog over the dry riverbed. The night is there, and the comet is there, but instead of the backwater, in the middle of the riverbed, lies an empty stone bowl, dry to the bottom, and the white snag sticks out of it like a hand out of a sleeve.

And in the neighboring noon, one step to the side, by the same snag, on the same sand, lies my boy, and nobody is counting over him. I won't get there. The seam stitched me to the world that stood closest, and mine has drifted so far that the thread couldn't reach it.

The Choir moves over, the way people move over on a bathhouse bench when one of their own comes in. There are many voices in it, all of them mine, and not one asks my name. My palms become transparent before I notice. The scar on the right stays visible longest, then it goes too.

I stand in the fog by the dry bowl, and around me stand they, all with my face. We don't touch the water; there isn't any here. Dawn moves down the riverbed slowly, from stone to stone.

Toward morning an old man in a canvas storm jacket comes down over the stones. He sits down by the snag, takes off his boots and sets them toes toward the river. He doesn't see us, or he sees one of us, on the far side, and rubs his eyes. I want to go up and ask him, the way I asked the boy, our way, the Rudnichny way: "Whose kid are you?" I no longer have lips, and my voice comes out as fog.
