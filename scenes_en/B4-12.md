Shadrin is a hundred and one. He lies on the fourth floor of the clinic at Pritomsky, the one with the lyre on its doors, in a ward with windows the width of the wall and the Tom below. The skin on his hands is thin as cigarette paper, but his eyes are clear. The nurses fear him and love him. On Tuesdays they transfuse him with the "second chance."

The second chance is advertised at bus stops now. On Sovetsky there's a screen: a gray-haired smiling man in a good coat, a lyre and the words "Orpheus. Second Chance." The ones who got it in '47 walk around the city with that particular look on their faces: they think they won't die. Some of them have already tested it.

"It's made from your blood, Andrei Nikolayevich," Shadrin told me today, the way you talk about the weather. "Two test tubes, '96, City Hospital No. 2. Your doctor from Tomsk sold them. I'm telling you this because in '61 you're going to drive me to the backwater. I want to dive. You're planning to go there yourself, I know. I worked out back in your grandfather's day where all of you are headed."

I said I'd think about it. He laughed and started coughing, and the nurse threw me out into the corridor.

At ten past four I come out of the clinic onto Sovetsky. The snow is fine and sparse, and the avenue is empty. My car is on the other side, by the main post office. Three hundred meters to the underpass.

I've lived in this city sixty years, and suddenly it seems to me it's always been like this, and I'm the one remembering wrong. ## После A
I look left, Mom's way, and go. The avenue is empty, wide, the snow soft underfoot, not a single car. Halfway across I look back, I don't know why, and look right. Nobody there either, only a snowplow blinking its orange light by Pritomsky.

The car by the post office is buried in snow up to the headlights. I sweep it off with my sleeve, get in, start it. I sit for five minutes until the inside warms up, looking at the screen at the bus stop, at the gray-haired man in the good coat. He smiles at me. I don't smile back.

At home I stand under a hot shower for a long time, and then sleep until lunch, for the first time in six months. I'm sixty, my knees hurt in the mornings, and I climb the stairs slowly now, holding the railing, like Dad.

## После B
I walk along my side to the post office, on the unplowed sidewalk, three hundred meters, and snow packs into my shoes. The underpass is lit and smells of urine and wet concrete. On the steps a man in a good coat is asleep, with that particular face. I step around him.

Up top, by the post office, I stand and look at the avenue. An empty number seven bus goes by, fast, in a spray of snow.

::drift ДРЕЙФ >= 50
The bus is in the left lane, coming from where I wasn't looking. If I'd crossed by the clinic, it would have gone right through me.
::end

My knees are wet and ache all the way to Yagunovsky. I'm sixty, and I climb the stairs slowly now, holding the railing, like Dad.

## После C
I look right. From the right comes a bus, number seven, empty and warm, yellow windows, in the left lane, as if that were how it should be. It passes a meter from me, and I'm sprayed with snow dust and the smell of diesel. The driver looks at me through the glass without any expression.

I cross the avenue, looking right first, then left. My hands are shaking; the key finds the car lock on the third try. I drive to Yagunovsky in the left lane, along with everyone else, and nobody honks at me. I grip the wheel so hard my knuckles go white, and the scar on my palm goes red.

I'm sixty, my knees hurt in the mornings, and my world drives on the left now, and I'm the only one who remembers it used to be different.
