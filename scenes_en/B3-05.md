At the drill site it's fifty-one below. I know that for sure, because a thermometer hangs by the trailer, an alcohol one, down to sixty below, and every morning the foreman, Petrovich, taps it with his fingernail and says, "Well, you can live with it." The diesel hasn't been switched off since November. If you switch it off, you won't start it again until April.

Today I'm carrying core samples from the rig to camp. Four kilometers along the creek, on the ice, because you can't get through along the bank; the dwarf pine is waist-high. A pack of sample boxes on my back, felt boots with rubber overshoes, quilted trousers, a scarf over my face, and in ten minutes the scarf goes stiff as cardboard from my breath. My eyelashes stick together, and I have to pull them apart with my mitten.

This winter Zoya Arkadyevna is in Yakutsk, at the office, with her ankles. Before she left she said, "On the creek, watch where there's steam. Where there's steam, there's water. Overflow ice, Samokhin, is alive underneath."

I watch. The creek lies like a white ribbon between the larches, flat as a road, and nothing is steaming anywhere. I walk and count my steps so as not to think about how my fingers are freezing. On step one thousand two hundred forty-six the ice under me sags, softly, as if I'd stepped on a sofa.

My leg goes down to the knee, into water. The water under the crust is warm, or so it seems for the first second, because it's zero and the air is fifty-one below. Then it stops being warm. I yank my leg out. The felt boot is heavy, soaked through, and steam rises off it, thick, like off a saucepan. Half a minute later the steam stops. The overshoe turns white. The cloth at my knee seizes up in a crust of ice, and the knee creaks inside it when it bends.

I can't feel the toes of my right foot anymore. Two thousand steps to camp. On the bank stands a dead larch, gray, barkless, and under it a snowdrift, loose, dry as flour.

## После A
I run. Running in a wet felt boot at fifty below is like running with a bucket on your foot. The boot knocks against the ice, wooden, ringing. My lungs burn with every breath; I breathe through the scarf, and in one minute the scarf becomes a crust of ice. At step eight hundred I stop feeling my leg up to the knee. At step one thousand I stop counting.

In camp Petrovich sees me from the trailer door, grabs me by the collar and sits me down on the bunk. The boot has to be cut off with a knife; it won't come off. The foot under it is white, waxy, like a candle. Petrovich rubs it with vodka, swears and rubs it again. My toes come back by night, with such pain that I bite the sleeve of my quilted jacket. Later the nails come off two of the toes. Petrovich says, "You'll live a hundred years. An idiot, but a healthy one."

## После B
I drop into the drift under the larch, tear off the overshoe and start rolling the boot in the snow, rubbing it in, the way you rub in soap. The snow is dry and powdery; it sticks to the wet felt and immediately darkens and gets heavier. I shake it off and scoop up more. Rub, shake off, scoop. After three minutes the boot is white and dry on the outside, in a crust of snow, and inside it's damp but no longer wet.

Then I get up and run along the creek right by the bank, through the dwarf pine, where the ice can't be alive. My toes ache and prickle as if with needles, and that's good; it means they're still mine. In camp Petrovich takes off my boot, feels my foot and grunts. "Who taught you?"

## После C
The larch is dry as gunpowder. I chop off the lower branches with the hatchet, strip birch bark from a neighboring birch, and the matches break in my fingers one after another; my fingers won't bend. It catches on the seventh. I take off the boot right there on the snow, prop it on a stick by the fire, hold my foot over the flame and look at it, white, someone else's, like a foot from an anatomy atlas.

The boot takes two hours to dry. For two hours I sit by the fire on my pack, and the sun goes down behind the hill, and the frost drops another three degrees. The ice on the creek sings. I didn't know it sang. It hums low and long, as if under the ice someone were drawing a bow across a thick string, and the sound comes from the whole creek at once, bank to bank. It makes the back of my head go cold, and the frost doesn't chill like that; this cold sits inside. I listen until the boot is dry.

I reach camp in the dark. Two toes on my right foot never come back. In spring, in Yakutsk, they cut them off, the little toe and the next one. I won't limp, the surgeon says. Won't dance either.

> Afterward I looked for those toes in every one of my deaths. They were never there. They were cut off in this life, and so this is the life I live in.
