They sent me up the golets alone, because it's already my second season and because Zoya Arkadyevna's ankle is swollen. "Bedrock outcrop on the saddle," she said this morning, and jabbed a pencil at the map. "Twelve samples, top to bottom, one every meter. Lunch up there. Back by six." Miron walked me to the tree line and went no farther. He said the horses had no business up there.

Up top there's not a tree, not a bush, only rock. The boulder field creeps down the slope toward the forest, gray blocks in rust-colored lichen, like a frozen river. In the middle of the saddle stands one single cedar, old, crooked, leaning all the way to the east, with dry needles under it. To the right is a little hollow, and in it, between the rocks, runs a stream, thin as a finger. Beyond the stream is a huge boulder with a flat top; two people could stand on it.

The cloud comes over the ridge in ten minutes. At first I see it out of the corner of my eye and think it's the shadow of another mountain. Then it rolls over the crest, low, black, with white rags hanging beneath, and the sun goes out as if someone had switched off the light in a stairwell. It turns cold. The wind brings a smell I don't know, fresh and sharp, like a socket when you plug a broken iron into it.

The first lightning hits the crest a kilometer from me. The thunder comes three seconds later, and the boulder field underfoot answers it with a faint tremor.

Then the hammer starts to hum. At first I think it's a bumblebee. The sound comes from behind my shoulder, thin and steady, like the transformer box behind our garage. I turn my head. The pick of the hammer above my shoulder is glowing pale blue, barely, the way a watch dial glows in the dark. The hairs on my arms are standing on end, every one.

The rain comes down all at once, like a wall. The rocks turn black and slippery. The second lightning strikes closer, on the neighboring crown, and the thunder comes with it.

## После D
The cedar stands alone on the saddle, but its roots, I remember, spread across the rocks in every direction, under the moss, under the lichen, ten meters out. I walk away from it across the boulder field counting steps, and at twenty I drop my pack, pull out the foam pad and squat on it, hugging my knees, feet together, head down. That's how drunks sit on the commuter train when they feel sick.

The strike comes a minute later. I don't see it, my eyes are shut; I see only white through my eyelids, and then a sound as if the sky right beside me has been torn like a bedsheet. It smells of scorched resin. When I raise my head, the cedar is standing, but down its trunk from top to bottom runs a fresh white stripe, stripped to the living wood, with steam pouring out of it. Around the roots the moss is smoking in a ring. The ring ends three steps from my foam pad.

I sit like that for another half hour, until the cloud moves off behind the ridge. Then I get up, take my hammer and, not knowing why, tap the trunk of the cedar with it, on the white stripe. The cedar answers short and dull. Inside it there's a crack, all the way to the roots, and outside it stands just as it stood.

## После E
The hammer is humming, and I remember how this ended, even though it hasn't happened yet. I yank off the pack, throw it on the rocks along with the hammer, the pot and the hatchet, run fifteen paces away and squat right on the wet lichen, feet together, head down. The foam pad is still in the pack, and to hell with it.

The strike hits the pack. Through my fingers I see a white thread from the sky, and my pack jumps on the rock as if alive. The thunder punches me in the chest. I'm shaking, and I don't know if it's from the cold or from something else.

The cloud moves off forty minutes later. I go over to the pack. The canvas is burned clean through, the stew can has swollen, and the hammer's pick has melted; a drop of metal has frozen on the tip, like on a candle. I pick it up. The handle is warm. I tap the boulder with it, and the boulder rings, long, and the ringing in my head doesn't stop for another half minute after it's gone quiet. I won't give this hammer back to Zoya Arkadyevna later, and she won't ask.

## После F
The hollow underfoot has become a chute, and down it, from the crest, runs brown water, and something knocks inside it. I remember what's knocking in it. I climb out of the chute onto the little ridge, a stony hump about five meters off, throw the foam pad on it and squat, curled up, feet together, head between my knees.

A minute later a mudflow comes down the chute. I hear it, and then I see a slurry of water, gravel and blocks the size of a TV crawling past me downhill toward the forest, the rocks in it knocking together with a sound like someone chopping firewood down below. Lightning hits the crest, then the cedar. I don't raise my head.

When the cloud moves off, the hollow is gone. In its place is a fresh gray furrow, bare rocks without lichen. I go down to the forest along the edge, skirting it, and at the tree line Miron is waiting for me. He's standing under a fir with two horses, and it looks like he's been standing there a long time. He looks at me, then at the mountain, and says, "They called?" I don't know what to answer. He nods, as if I had.
