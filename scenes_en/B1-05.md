The six men from the first squad are buried on the same day, at the Kedrovka cemetery, with a band playing so slowly it seems the trumpeters are falling asleep. The governor gives a speech. Palych stands in the front row in dress uniform and never once lifts his eyes from the ground.

After the funerals the Glubokaya mine is shut for six months. Then it opens again, with new ventilation, a new director and the same old men. Dad goes back to the face on the very first day. Now, sometimes, in the middle of a shift, he switches off his hammer, listens, and says to the whole crew, "Let's get out of here." And the crew goes, nobody argues. Twice, half an hour later, the roof came down at the face.

In June Palych catches me outside the bathhouse.

"I need men," he says. "Six of them. You'll do, Samokhin. You know when not to go in. That's rare. The rest only know when to go in."

Working in the rescue unit pays less than working at the face, but you only go down to places everybody else is running up from. Training takes six months, and they teach you everything, down to how to pull a man out from under rock without finishing him off. Then it's twenty-four-hour duty every fourth day, and every alarm in the region is yours.

In the evening it's Vitka's birthday, twenty-three, at the garages, with shashlik on a grill made from an old fridge shelf. Nika comes. She's finishing medical school in Novosibirsk and in two years will do her internship at the regional hospital, here, in Kemerovo. Her hair is long, below her shoulder blades, Vitka's jacket is over her shoulders, and she still counts everything: the skewers, the bottles, how many times Vitka has said "basically."

Seryoga is telling her a mine story, how in March he walked out of a gas-filled face "all by himself." Nika laughs with her head thrown back, sitting on the hood of Vitka's Lada Nine and swinging her legs.

## После A
For six months I train at the unit's base in the Kirovsky district. I carry a stretcher with a sack of sand instead of a man up seven flights of stairs, run in a breathing set through a smoke-filled training tunnel, learn to listen to the roof through a prop with my ear pressed against it. The straps of the set rub my shoulders raw, and by December the raw flesh turns to skin.

At the swearing-in Palych shakes my hand and holds it in his. "Seventh," he says quietly. He recruited six men to replace the ones he lost, and I'm the extra one, the seventh. At first I don't understand what he means by it. Then I understand that he doesn't know himself.

Dad doesn't come to the swearing-in. In the evening he knocks on my door, puts a bottle and two glasses on the table and says, "Well, rescuer." He pours and drinks his without clinking.

## После B
"I'm staying," I tell Palych.

He shrugs, puts out his cigarette against the garage and leaves without saying goodbye. There's no offense in it. He has six vacancies and not a minute for people who aren't coming.

At the face Dad puts me next to him, in the same team. We work in silence, like two pumps, and I learn from him how to listen to the quiet. That's when the face goes quiet in the wrong way. He can't explain how exactly. He just says, "When you feel it, run." I'll feel it, I say. I already do.

## После C
"I'm going to study," I tell Palych. "By correspondence. For foreman."

Palych looks at me for a long time, the way you look at the roof when it hasn't yet decided whether to come down. "Management, then," he says without malice, and puts out his cigarette against the garage. Then he slaps me on the shoulder, heavily, with his palm. "Well, at least one manager will know when not to go in."

In September I enroll in the mining department at the polytechnic as a correspondence student. For four years I go into Kemerovo for exams, sleep on commuter trains, write term papers on the backs of work orders in the assignment office between shifts. Dad looks at my textbooks like somebody else's boots in the front hall. "Paperwork," he says. But when I bring home the diploma, he takes it in his hands and reads the name for a long time, moving his lips.
