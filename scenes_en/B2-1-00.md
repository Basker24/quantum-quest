The machine warms me for three hours, and my heart restarts in the eleventh minute, on the first shock. I open my eyes and know that Lunyov is about to drop his glasses, and he drops them. I know that in four seconds the bulb over the door will flicker. In four seconds it flickers.

Lunyov gropes around the floor for a long time, finds his glasses, puts them on and stares at the monitor for a long time. On the monitor a steady note runs on, forty hertz, and it isn't going to fade.

"It's ringing," he says in a whisper. "Tikhon Savelyevich, it's ringing."

He isn't saying it to me.

They bring me up in the cage wrapped in a wool blanket, like a fire victim. My ears pop at four hundred, the same as on the way down. Through the hum of the cable the Choir keeps talking, and one voice I hear separately. This me, right now, this very minute, is behind the wheel somewhere in fog, on a highway, and shouting one word: "Truck!" Then he stops shouting.

Shadrin is waiting for me at the cage. He takes my palm in both of his, like a politician meeting voters, and holds it until I stop shaking.

"What are you hearing right now, Andrei Nikolayevich?"

"One of me just crashed his car," I say. My tongue moves badly after the cold. "Just now. There's fog."

Shadrin takes a leather-bound notebook and a gold pen out of his inside pocket. He looks at his watch and writes down the time. He finds nothing strange in what I've said.

"Where?"

"I don't know. He didn't have time to look."

"Next time let him have time," says Shadrin, and puts the notebook away. "There's a place outside Moscow where you're expected, Andrei Nikolayevich. A lab, an apartment, a salary your father never saw in his whole life. Vadim Petrovich is coming with you as your supervisor. The plane is at nine tomorrow. You're going to have a very interesting life now."

The words "let him have time" only reach me later, in the bathhouse by the pithead, under a hot shower, when my teeth stop chattering.

> Shadrin was sixty-eight then. That was how he held the hand of everyone he bought. I found that out later.

## После A
In the morning I'm sitting in the back seat of Shadrin's black car, and through the tinted glass the Glubokaya headframe and the garages on Shakhtyorov Street float away. At the airport Shadrin buys me coffee and a copy of the *Kuzbass*, like an uncle seeing his nephew off to the army.

On the plane I fall asleep, for the first time in months without dreams, and wake up over Moscow because one of mine in the Choir is going through the April ice on the Ob Sea. Lunyov sits across the aisle and doesn't sleep at all. Every half hour he touches the inside pocket of his jacket, the way you touch a ticket.

I call Mom from Moscow. She asks when I'll be back. I say soon, and that day I believe it myself.

## После C
"First to my father," I say. "Then wherever you say."

"A week," Shadrin answers, and smiles. "Family is sacred."

Dad sits in the kitchen in Rudnichny, smoking out the vent window and listening to me without interrupting. When I mention the man in the good coat who holds your hand longer than he needs to, he stubs out his cigarette on a saucer, though it's only half smoked.

"Dry sort of fellow?" he asks. "He used to come see your granddad, in '80, in '85. Grandma gave him tea. He didn't have gray hair then." Dad looks out the vent window at the headframe for a long time. "Well, if they're paying, go. Only call your mother, Andryukha. Call every week."
