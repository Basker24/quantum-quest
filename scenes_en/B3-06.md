Sardaana is measuring a mammoth with a tape measure. All you can see of the mammoth is one leg; it sticks out of the bluff at the height of my head, brown, in reddish wool, with skin, and Sardaana stands under it on a crate and pulls the tape from the knee to the hoof. The tape is a dressmaker's tape, her mother's, in centimeters, with little flowers on it.

"Forty-three," she says. "Samokhin, write it down. Come on, write it down, what are you staring at?"

I write it down. I've known her four days. She was brought to our base from Yakutsk on a passing helicopter, with a backpack, a rifle and the tape, because tusk hunters on the next channel had found a mammoth in the bluff, and her institute had neither a boat nor money. We had a boat. Zoya Arkadyevna took one look at her and said to me, "You'll take her. Just don't fall in love, you idiot." I took her.

The bluff here is forty meters high, gray, striped, all frozen silt and ice. Yedoma, Sardaana says; it's thirty thousand years old. The tusk hunters wash it out with motor pumps, with river water, two hoses like fire hoses, and that leaves tunnels in the bluff, black, round, twenty meters deep. Liquid mud flows out of the tunnels, and it smells so bad my eyes water: thirty thousand years ago everything here rotted and froze, and now it's thawing back out.

The crew boss is called Gena; he's from Belgorod, with a gold tooth and camouflage, and he wants us to leave. In the next tunnel they have a tusk, a second one, still in the wall, and they're going to saw it, because there's no getting it out whole. Sardaana wants them not to saw it. She wants to take out the whole skeleton, photograph it, measure it. That's three days. Gena says he doesn't have three days; he has a buyer from China and a boat on Friday.

A shotgun leans against the diesel barrel. Next to the shotgun sits Gena's nephew, Slavik, about twenty, drinking something from a mug that isn't tea. Since morning he's been looking at Sardaana's backpack, at the rifle on her back, at my field log.

"Inspection," says Slavik for the third time today. "Uncle Gen, I'm telling you, it's an inspection. They're writing stuff down."

The tunnel with the tusk is behind me. It's dripping inside. Its roof has slumped over the day, I can see it: in the morning it was round, and now it sags in the middle like an old mattress.

## После A
"Gena," I say. "What are you selling the tusk for? Per kilo?"

Gena names a price. I shake my head the way Mom used to shake it over her frying pans when the woman in the next row priced hers ten rubles lower. I say that the Chinese buyer will pay a third more for a whole one with a document saying it's a mammoth, and that only this woman with the tape measure can write him that document, and that it'll take her three hours to write it while we measure. Gena looks at Sardaana. Sardaana looks at me as if I'd sold her into slavery.

For three hours we measure, photograph and write. Then Gena saws the tusk in half; I turn away, Sardaana doesn't. She writes Gena a paper, on institute letterhead, with the stamp she carries in her backpack. The paper is genuine; it's just that it says the tusk was sawn illegally.

"He won't read it anyway," she says in the boat, and laughs so loudly that geese take off from the sandbar. "Samokhin, where did you learn to lie like that?"

"At the market. In '98. I was nine."

She stops laughing and looks at me for a long time.

## После B
I sit down next to Gena on a log and ask for a smoke, though I don't smoke. I tell him I'm from Kemerovo, Dad's a miner, Mom works at the factory, that I'm out here digging permafrost for twenty thousand a month and couldn't care less about their tusk. That she's no inspection either, just a crazy woman with a tape measure, and that if they let her measure the skeleton, she'll leave happy, and if they don't, she'll sit here until Friday singing in Yakut. Sardaana hears the "crazy woman" part and doesn't object.

Gena roars with laughter. Slavik doesn't. Gena tells him, "Put the gun in the tent, you moron, he's a miner's kid." Slavik carries the gun away.

We excavate the skeleton until morning, by the white night, and neither Gena nor Slavik bothers us. Toward morning Gena brings two mugs of tea and silently sets them on a crate. Sardaana drinks hers and says to me without looking, "You told them the truth about yourself. All of it." I nod. "Strange method," she says.

## После C
"Let's go," I say.

"Go where? There's a mammoth here!"

"The mammoth's waited thirty thousand years. It'll wait a bit longer. Slavik won't."

She swears in Yakut, for a long time, and I don't understand a word, but I understand everything. She gets into the boat on her own, the tape clenched in her fist, and the whole way back to base she sits with her back to me. Behind us, by the bluff, the motor pumps start up.

That evening at the base she comes to my tent with a bottle and two mugs. She pours, drinks, pours again.

"You're a coward, Samokhin," she says. "But you're a live coward. That's rare. Usually with me it's the other way around."

## После D
I go up to the tunnel and tap the roof with my hammer, the gray frozen wall, at the entrance, then a step deeper, then another. Near the entrance the roof answers solidly, tuk-tuk, like a good stump. At the fifth step the sound changes, hollow, like a crate, and afterward the ringing doesn't end in my head but trembles, thin and cold, and in it I hear water running above, beyond a meter of ice.

"Gena," I say. "Get your people out. In twenty minutes or so it'll come down."

Gena looks at me, at the hammer. Then, for no reason anyone knows, he believes me and yells into the tunnel. Two men come out, with a saw and a crowbar. Forty minutes later the roof comes down. It comes down softly, without a crash, the whole tunnel at once, the way an underbaked pie sinks in the oven, and the tusk stays in the wall under three meters of mud and ice. On Friday the Chinese buyer leaves without a tusk.

Sardaana stands beside me and looks at me. "How did you hear it?" she asks. I don't know. I say Zoya Arkadyevna taught me. Sardaana says nobody teaches that. In the evening she measures my palm with her tape, from the scar to the wrist, and writes it down in her notebook.
