Since '22 I've been allowed to live outside the fence, in the village. Remezov calls it "the trust regime." I rent a one-room apartment on the third floor of a five-story block by the station. The landlady, Tamara Petrovna, has moved in with her daughter in Moscow and left me a rug on the wall, a china cabinet full of crystal, and a Neva gas water heater in the kitchen. The village has no central heating; the water heater warms both the water and the radiators, and it hums all night behind the wall, steady, on one note.

In '22 half the young people left Site Nine. Polina and her husband live in Yerevan now and send me photos of mountains. I look at them for a long time. On those mountains none of mine ever dies.

After sessions I always lie in a hot bath. After thirty-two degrees the cold sits in my bones until lunch, and only boiling water drives it out.

Yesterday, while I was at Site Nine, two men from the gas service came by for a routine inspection. The downstairs neighbor let them in; she has a key the landlady left her. On the phone Tamara Petrovna was very surprised. She hadn't called anybody.

Today I lay in the bath for an hour and a half. It's half past one in the morning now, and my head aches as if a wedge had been driven into my temple, and I'm a little sick to my stomach. The tiles swim when I look at them. That happens after sessions; I'm used to it. I'm sitting on the edge of the couch in my bathrobe, and the pillow seems like the softest thing in the world.

## После B
I throw open the kitchen window, and November tumbles into the apartment, damp, smelling of fuel oil from the station. I shut the valve on the pipe by the water heater with both hands; it's stiff. The little flame in the blue window goes out. Then I climb to the fifth floor, holding the railing, and ring the doorbell of Uncle Gena, a retired warrant officer I sometimes smoke with on the stairs.

Uncle Gena opens the door in his undershirt with a hammer in his hand, just in case. He doesn't ask anything, makes up the couch for me and covers me with an army blanket. I sleep until nine.

::drift ДРЕЙФ >= 5
In the morning Uncle Gena is frying eggs and calls into the other room, "Valya, we're out of bread!" He's been a widower for six years; we've talked about it on the stairs maybe twenty times. A woman in a bathrobe comes out of the room, puts bread on the table and says to me, "Andryusha, eat, eat." She calls me by my name, though I've never seen her before.
::end

In the morning the gas service, the real one, called by Uncle Gena, finds something in the flue that shouldn't be there and neatly writes up a report. And at Site Nine, in the hallway of building two, Shadrin is walking toward me. He stops, which he's never once done in eight years.

"Sleep well, Andrei Nikolayevich?"

## После C
"Andryukha, you awake?" Vitka is sober today, and that makes me uneasy right away. "Listen. There's a guy coming up in our reports, a former gas fitter, two prison terms. Yesterday a camera caught him outside your building, with a tool case. There were two of them. I checked: the gas service has no work order for your address. You've got a water heater there, right?"

"Yeah," I say. My tongue moves like after a session.

"What's wrong with you? Andryukha! Open the window. Open the window, shut off the gas and go out on the stairs. I'm not hanging up."

I do everything he says and sit on the cold steps in my bathrobe, and Vitka talks to me for an hour about fishing on the Pakhra, just so I won't go quiet. In the morning he comes himself, in a police car, looks at the water heater, at the flue, and smokes outside the building for a long time.

"It wasn't the neighbors," he says. "That's a clean job. It's your people, Andryukha, from behind the fence. Get out of there."

"Nobody gets out of there, Vitya."

## После D
I remember lying down on this couch on top of the bedspread. I remember a hand on the blanket that wouldn't lift, and the blue reflections of the water heater on the ceiling. Just looking at the pillow makes me sick.

I shut off the gas, open every window and sit on the stairs until morning, in a jacket over my bathrobe. At eight I go to Site Nine and walk into Shadrin's office without knocking.

Shadrin hears me out to the end, hands folded on the desk, and smiles.

"So you can hear yourself too," he says. "I confess I had my doubts. An instrument that can't sense its own failure is no use to us. Sit down, Andrei Nikolayevich. Tea?"
