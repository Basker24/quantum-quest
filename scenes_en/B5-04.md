The water came to Bangkok from the north, slowly, the way spring comes to Kemerovo, only the other way around. First on TV, then at Don Mueang airport, then in the suburbs. Ploy's mother lives in Nonthaburi, right by the Chao Phraya, in a two-story house where there used to be a noodle shop downstairs, and upstairs live she, her chickens and a Buddha in gold foil. She refused to leave. She said on the phone that the Buddha won't leave, so she won't either.

::if СВЯЗЬ_ПАРТНЁР >= 15
Ploy calls me at four in the morning and says one word, "Andy." An hour later we're on the ferry, and by evening, on a passing army truck, we drive into a city standing knee-deep in water.
::end

::if СВЯЗЬ_ПАРТНЁР < 15
At the center Ploy asks who'll go with her to Bangkok to get her mother. Everyone is suddenly very busy. Ploy looks at me; I'm the only trainee with no girlfriend and no ticket home for Christmas. An hour later we're on the ferry.
::end

The last two kilometers we go by long-tail boat, so narrow you can only sit in single file. The boatman steers with a pole that has the motor on it, like an oar. The streets have become canals. Plastic chairs float along them, slippers, banana leaves, and once a dead dog, bloated like a ball. It smells of slime, gasoline and fried garlic; someone on a second floor is frying rice right in the middle of the flood.

By the bridge the boat stops. There's only half a meter of air under the bridge, and the boatman waves his arms: no farther. It's two hundred meters to the mother's house down the soi, a narrow lane between buildings. The water there is waist-deep. The mother stands on her balcony waving to us, a chicken cage beside her.

::if ДАР >= 30
I lower my leg into the water, and the filling in one of my lower teeth starts to ache. Thin and sour, like when you were a kid and bit into chocolate with the foil still on. I pull my leg out, and the filling goes quiet. I put it back in, and it aches again.
::end

::if РАЗ >= 45
Across the street, above the water, a green 7-Eleven sign is lit. So the power on this block hasn't been cut. The pole at the corner stands crooked in the water, and black wires from it sag right down to the surface. And by the bridge the roof of a market sticks out of the water, all tin awnings, stall to stall, almost all the way to the house.
::end

"Andy, come," says Ploy, already swinging a leg over the side.

## После A
I take two steps waist-deep in the water, and the filling shrieks so that I grab my cheek. Ploy is already beside me, in the water. I grab her by the tank top and pull her back to the boat; she slaps my hands and shouts in Thai.

"Teeth," I tell her. "Current. Electric. I don't know how you say it."

She looks at me like I'm crazy, and then at the pole at the corner, at the wires in the water. Then she climbs back into the boat herself and sits for a long time hugging her knees. The boatman takes us the long way around, down the next soi, where there are no poles, and we reach the house from the back, half an hour later. Ploy's mother lowers a ladder to us from the balcony and doesn't ask a single question.

A week later the papers write about two people electrocuted in that very soi, by that very pole. Ploy reads me the article aloud, translating, and her voice doesn't tremble.

## После B
I point Ploy to the green sign. She understands before I've finished in English, and goes so pale it shows even under her tan.

We cross the market roofs on all fours, over hot tin. The tin booms under our knees like a drum, and in one place it sags, but holds. From the last awning to the mother's balcony is a meter and a half. I jump first, Ploy second, and I catch her by the hand.

Ploy's mother feeds us rice with egg right on the balcony and talks to her daughter about me for a long time, pointing. Ploy doesn't translate. She just blushes.

> Afterward the Thais called that flood "the big water." How many people that autumn were electrocuted in their own streets, I learned from the papers. More than drowned.

## После C
"No," I say, holding Ploy's hand. "We're not going."

She pulls away and shouts at me. I don't let go. The boatman leaves us at the bridge; we sit on the concrete all night, ankle-deep in water, and the mosquitoes eat me alive like they never did even at the dacha in Yagunovka. Ploy's mother waves to us from the balcony, then waves less often, then blows out her candle and sleeps.

The truck comes at six in the morning. Soldiers in waist-high rubber boots bring it up to the house and lower the mother from the balcony, chickens and all. She carries the Buddha herself. By evening I have a fever of thirty-nine, and all the way back to Koh Tao Ploy doesn't speak to me. Only on the ferry, when I'm shaking under three towels, does she silently push a pill and a bottle of water into my hands.

## После E
"Stop," I say, and catch Ploy by the tank top when she's already got a leg over. "There's current in there. I know. It cramps your legs."

She looks at me, at the pole, at the wires. I don't know what my face looks like, but she doesn't argue. She stands up in the boat to her full height and shouts something up at the bridge, where a policeman in rubber boots is standing. They shout at each other for five minutes. Then the policeman leaves, and twenty minutes later the 7-Eleven sign goes dark, and the whole block with it.

We wade down the dark soi waist-deep, holding on to each other. The filling is silent. Ploy's mother lowers the ladder to us from the balcony and, while Ploy climbs up first, looks down at me very closely. That's how my grandmother looked at me when I came home from the hospital at seven.
