"Andryukh," he says, very calmly. "My leg's pinned. Give it a yank, huh?"

Right here, on the right, is an old raise, a narrow passage up along the seam, caved in halfway. From up top, at the shaft, Shurup is yelling something about the bucket.

## После A
I lift the prop with my shoulder, Vitka yanks his leg out and screams so that my ear rings. His shin is bent where it shouldn't bend. I take him under the arms and drag him backward along the drift, over the coal, and he helps with his good leg, pushing off like a swimmer off the side of the pool.

::if F_СТОЙКА
The rustling gets louder and louder, and by the shaft, past my larch, something lies down heavily and softly, once, then again. The larch stands. It shrieks under the load like a saw, and stands, and we pass under it on all fours, and the last five meters I don't remember.
::end

::if !F_СТОЙКА
The rustling gets louder and louder, and dust comes at us from the shaft, though we're crawling toward it. We pass the third prop from the shaft, and I hear it break behind me, dry, like a stick broken over a knee.

Shurup yanks Vitka out onto the grass by the collar, then me. The roof comes down while we're lying in the birch grove. A cloud comes out of the pit, slow and black. Vitka lies on his back looking at the sky, laughing, and crying, and laughing again.

"You're sick," he says. "You're sick, Andryukh. I'm heavy."

## После B
"Shurup!" I yell. "The bucket! All the way down! Crank!"

I get under the sagging crossbar and prop it up with my shoulder. It's heavy, as if the whole birch grove were standing on me, along with Shurup and the Bychok. My knees shake. Grit pours down into my ears, down my collar. Meanwhile Vitka pulls his leg out from under the broken prop himself, with his hands, hissing through his teeth.

The bucket comes down, scraping the walls of the shaft, just as my knees start to buckle. Vitka tumbles into it sideways; I jump onto the rim and hold on to the rope. Shurup cranks the winch, wheezing like a steam engine. We ride up into the light together in one iron barrel, and the roof comes down beneath us when the bucket is already above the grass. Dust punches up into its bottom.

My shoulder is blue for a month afterward. Vitka says I look like the Atlas from the picture in our history textbook, only with snot.

## После C
The sandstone holds. I remember it the way you remember an address: by the shaft it came down, by the face it didn't. I pull Vitka out from under the prop and drag him back, into the old raise, into the narrow hole up along the seam. He screams; his leg drags. We cram ourselves into the raise like into a burrow, heads up, and I brace my feet against the wall.

The roof comes down between us and the shaft, all of it, from the second prop to the ninth. The sound is such that I feel it in my teeth. Then quiet, only sand trickling off Vitka's hard hat, and one lamp between the two of us.

It takes six hours to dig us out, Shurup and the men from the next pit. For six hours we sit in the raise.

::if F_Т09 != ДОЛГ_СВАРНОМУ
Vitka tells me about the army, all of it, including what he's never told anyone.
::end

::if F_Т09 == ДОЛГ_СВАРНОМУ
Vitka tells me how for two years he hauled bathtubs for Welder for both of us and never once threw it in my face, though he wanted to.
::end

Then he asks, "How'd you know about the raise?" I say I guessed. He nods and doesn't ask again, but from that day he looks at me differently, a little sideways.

## После D
I turn around and run, bent over, toward the shaft, the ladder, the white square of sky. Behind me Vitka shouts my name, once, and then just shouts, without a name, and through the shouting the roof rustles. I climb the rebar, the rungs cut my palms, and Shurup yanks me out onto the grass by the collar.

The roof comes down beneath us. A black cloud comes out of the pit and stands over the birch grove.

It takes five hours to dig Vitka out. He's lying under a prop in an air pocket by the face, alive, legs under the rock. When they bring him up, he looks at me and says nothing, not then, not at the hospital in Kiselyovsk, where they pin both his shins. We tell the ambulance he fell off a tractor.

## После E
I remember a cockpit that never existed, its window pointing up at the sky, and a tarpaulin boot under a pedal. Back then I pulled by the shoulders at something that was holding on by the boot. I drop to my knees by the prop and look. The prop lies across Vitka's shin, and the shin is in a rubber boot, and the boot is too big for him; Vitka always buys a size up, "so the foot wrapping fits."

My knife is in the pocket of my overalls, a folding one. I cut the boot shaft top to bottom along the seam, the rubber parts, and Vitka's foot in its wrapping slides out of the boot by itself, whole. Vitka looks at it as if it were someone else's. "Run!" I yell, and we run for the shaft, bent over, both on our own feet, him in one boot. Behind us it rustles, and then comes down, at our backs, when we're already on the ladder.

Up top Vitka sits on the grass in one boot, laughing. The birch by the pit stands where it stood, but the stream, it seems to me, always used to run on the other side of the grove. I look at it until Shurup asks where the other boot is.
