I leave for Altai on my own, in the spring of '08. In the city people drown them out, but in the mountains there are no people, and there you hear each voice separately, as in an empty stadium. Mom sees me off to the bus for Biysk and presses five hundred rubles and a bag of cutlets into my hand.

For the second year I've been living in Multa, a village by the lakes, with seekers of Belovodye. There are about twenty of them, from Novosibirsk, Moscow and Barnaul, in linen shirts. They're looking for the land where nobody dies, and they say "namaste." I chop their firewood, set up beehives and keep quiet at their evening circles. Nobody asks me who I am. Nobody here asks anybody.

I can tell the voices apart now. Each one is me, only in another life, and each has his own death. The one under water, he's seven. The one who was buried during his apprenticeship. The one who coughs. Since winter there's a new one, the one who laughed in the elevator, a student from Tomsk; he wasn't afraid. And in spring one more, the one people stepped over on a beach. He sometimes speaks English. The dead all speak in my voice, and they all lie a little. Each thinks he was the only one who had a life.

Today Matvei is leading the three of us over the pass to the Poperechnye lakes. Matvei is a former programmer from Akademgorodok, red-haired, bearded, swears every other word and doesn't believe in Belovodye, I think. He's here for the honey. Near the pass the fog covers us. First low down, then knee-deep, then up to the throat, and five minutes later I can't see my own hands.

"Stop!" Matvei yells from somewhere. "Everybody stop!"

I stop. The other two are somewhere behind; I can hear one of them praying in Tibetan. Underfoot is wet scree; it rustles and slides a little.

Then I'm called from two sides.

From the left, close, ten steps or so: "Andryukha! Over here! There's a gap between the rocks, squeeze through, the trail's there!" From the right, farther off: "Andryukha, where are you? Say something!"

The voices are alike. Both male, both calling me by name. The fog muffles sound, and I can't tell which of them is closer to the trail.

## После A
I turn to the right and walk, feeling every rock with my foot. The left voice calls louder at first, then sounds offended, then goes silent, and that silence sends a chill down my back. Twenty steps on I bump into a backpack.

"Holy cow," says Matvei. "Andryukha. How'd you find me?"

I say I followed his voice. He nods, ties a rope from his belt to mine, and one at a time we gather up the other two. We spend the night right on the pass, under a rock, four of us in one tent. In the morning the fog drops away below, and I see where the left one was calling me. Ten steps from where I stood last night the scree ends, and below it, far down, lies the Upper Lake, small and green as a button.

## После B
"Matvei!" I yell. "Swear!"

From the right, after a pause, the fog answers with a curlicue of a curse I wouldn't repeat even at the mine. On the left, silence. I go right, toward the swearing, and twenty steps on I run into a red beard.

"What are you yelling for?" says Matvei.

"Checking you were alive."

He looks at me for a long time and then guffaws, loudly, into the whole fog. That night in the tent he tells everyone I've invented a new mantra. Later, at the village circles, they repeat it with serious faces, and nobody but Matvei and me knows what it means.

## После C
I sit down on the wet rocks, hug my knees and sit. They call me from the left and from the right, then only from the left, then from the left they start coaxing, gently, in my voice, the way Mom used to coax me to take my medicine. I don't get up.

By midnight I can't feel my fingers. By morning I can't feel my legs. The fog lifts at dawn, and I see I'm sitting ten steps from a drop. Below, two hundred meters down, lies the Upper Lake, small and green. Matvei finds me an hour later and rubs my hands for a long time with snow from the gully. Afterward two nails on my left hand come off, and until autumn I can't hold an axe.

## После D
I listen to the left one while he calls, and remember how he called last time. A gap, a trail, squeeze through. My legs remember the scree going out from under them, and I want to sit down.

I turn my back on him and go right, toward the far voice. The left one calls louder and louder and suddenly breaks into a shout, a child's, offended, and goes silent. Twenty steps on I run into Matvei.

"Why are you so white?" he asks.

I don't know what to say. In the morning, when the fog lifts, I don't look to the left. Matvei looks, and is silent for a long time, and then takes off his hat and stands bareheaded, though it's cold.
