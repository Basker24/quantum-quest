Up top, on the reindeer moss, Vitka lies on his back looking into the fog. His right foot has no boot; the boot stayed down there under the pedal, and his shin is bent at an angle. The cowlings flare up when we're already down the slope, yellow, hot, and the chopper burns until evening. Vitka watches it burn and says, "Good machine. Twenty years. Don't feel sorry for it."

## После B
I shove the pry bar under the pedal assembly and throw my whole weight on it. The metal creaks and won't give. I reposition, lean on it again, and the pedal moves two fingers' width, then a palm's. Vitka pulls his right leg out himself, holding the handle over his head. The left one I work free from under the panel for another minute, centimeter by centimeter, and sweat pours off me, though it's cold in the cockpit. The smoke already hangs thick under the ceiling, and I breathe through my sleeve.

Then I take him on my back like a sack and crawl through the cabin toward the light. He helps with his arms. Vanya takes him by the collar from above, Timur by the belt, and the four of us tumble out onto the moss in a heap. The cowlings flare up when we're halfway down the slope.

Vitka's legs are intact, both of them, just crushed, and in Yakutsk they pin them back together. He'll walk with a cane. A month later in the ward he tells me, "What took you so long back there, huh? I figured you were stripping the pedals for scrap."

## После C
I remember pulling him by the shoulders while something below wouldn't let go, and how it burned at my back afterward. It wasn't the pedal holding him. His foot is in a boot, a size 44 tarpaulin boot, wide in the shaft, and the pedal has pinned the boot, but the foot sits loose inside it. I dive under the panel with my knife, slit the shaft top to bottom along the seam, the way you slit the skin off a sausage, and Vitka's foot in its wool sock slides out of the boot by itself.

The left one he yanks out himself, with his hands, swearing. I drag him through the cabin toward the light, and we tumble out onto the moss before I can count to a hundred. The cowlings flare up when we're on the slope. The boot stays down there under the pedal and burns with the chopper.

Vitka lies in the moss in one boot, laughing. "Tarpaulin," he says. "Andryukh. You murdered my boot. Government issue. They'll dock my pay."

## После D
I back out of the cockpit. Vitka looks at me, and his smile doesn't fade; it stays right there on his face, only his eyes change.

"Go on, then," he says. "Go on, Andryukh. Kostya's coming."

I climb out through the cabin, through the hatch, and fall onto the moss. Kostya runs up with the first-aid kit, drops it and climbs into the blister window feet first. I sit on the slope next to Sardaana. She holds her arm and doesn't look at me. Black smoke pours from under the cowlings, then Kostya hauls Vitka halfway out of the window, and Vitka screams, and they both fall down onto the rocks. A minute later the chopper goes up in flames.

Vitka survives. Burns on his arms, his neck and his cheek; four months to put his legs back together; he'll walk with a cane. In the hospital he doesn't talk to me. Later, a year later, on the phone, he says just one thing: "I don't blame you. Maybe I'd have done the same." I don't know whether that's true.

## После E
I remember a drift that never existed, low, black, and how I dragged him backward toward a white square of sky, and how I fell six meters short. Not backward, not to the exit, not to the cabin, where the smoke is going. The exit is here, above us, in the window, with the sky right overhead.

I plant my feet on the armrest of the second seat, grab Vitka under the arms and pull up, not back. He comes out from under the panel more easily than I thought, with a terrible sound down below, and I shove him into the window, head first into the fog, into the hands of Kostya, who's just run up with the first-aid kit. Then I climb out myself. The smoke follows me out like a cork from a bottle.

We lie on the moss. Vitka holds his leg and looks at me, at my hands, and says, "How'd you know I'd fit through?" I don't know. The fog over the ridge is lifting, and the hill opposite isn't standing where it stood when we came down. That's how it seems to me. I look at it for a long time, until the chopper goes up behind us.

## После F
I lie on my side under the instrument panel, cheek on the floor, wet with oil, and find his right foot by touch. The tarpaulin boot is pinned by the pedal right at the sole, but the shaft stands loose, with a foot wrapping flapping inside it. Up above, Vitka says it tickles.

My knife is a folding one, a field knife. I cut the shaft down from the knee along the seam, and the tarpaulin parts like cardboard. Then I pull Vitka's leg up by the trouser leg, and it comes out of the boot along with the wrapping, bare, white. The boot stays under the pedal.

The left one I get out the same way, by touch. I haul him into the cabin by his harness straps. Smoke is already flowing over the panel, and behind us, at the tail, the crackling is louder.
