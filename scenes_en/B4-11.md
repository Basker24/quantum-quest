Sonka calls me at nine in the evening. She's twenty-three, in her sixth year of medical school and her second year working as a nurse in the red zone at the Regional hospital. She talks to me the way she probably talks to relatives in the corridor: evenly and fast.

"Dad's bad. Lungs, heart, everything at once. He might not make it till morning. Come. Only there's a gray car parked by the main entrance, third day now. Two men in it. They asked me about you."

I know about the gray car. A week ago Shadrin's lawyer called me and said Vitka had given a statement. Fourteen years of my life, year by year, with addresses: the bootleg pits, the basement in Novokuznetsk, the meetings. "A source who worked at the pits," only now with a surname and on the record.

::if F_В409 == СКАЗАЛ
And the Uyut café near Topki. Two in the kitchen. That night Vitka was behind the wheel of Shadrin's car in the parking lot and saw me lean down to the window.
::end

::if F_В404 == БРОСИЛ
Vitka has walked with a cane for eleven years. They never did put his legs back together right. The lawyer said that at his interrogation the first thing Vitka talked about was the pit. "He got out, and I stayed." Word for word.
::end

::if F_В404 != БРОСИЛ

::end

Dad is on the fifth floor, in intensive care, where they let you in one at a time. He hasn't spoken to me in seven years, since that eighth of March. Two weeks ago Sonka told him I'd come, and he said, "Let him come without the Welder." The Welder's long gone. Dad has probably forgotten.

I'm sitting in my car in the far parking lot. The snow is wet; the wipers are freezing to the glass. A hundred meters to the main entrance. The gray car is parked right by the steps with its engine running, steam from the exhaust spreading over the asphalt.

## После A
I walk across the parking lot, through the wet snow, past the gray car. Its window rolls down, and the man inside looks at me without any expression, the way you look at a bus that isn't yours. The window rolls up. I go in.

Dad is small. I remember him huge, in his hard hat, with his lamp, but he's lying under a sheet small as Sonka was as a child, with tubes in his nose. He opens his eyes, sees me and looks for a long time. Then he points a finger at the nightstand. On the nightstand is a bundle in a Magnit supermarket bag. In the bag is a canvas storm jacket, old, faded, with a patch on the elbow.

"Mother kept it in the trunk till she died," he says hoarsely, one word per breath. "Granddad's, I think. For you. You're one of ours too… under the mine."

That's all he says to me. On the way out the two from the gray car take me by the elbows, politely, and put me in the back seat. Dad dies two days later, at 04:12 in the morning; Sonka says afterward he was looking at the door before it happened. They bury him without me. I'm released four months later: Vitka withdraws his statement, and Shadrin's lawyers find forty errors in the records.

## После B
"Move the car at the Regional," I tell Shadrin. "For an hour."

"We'll move it," says Shadrin. His voice at seventy-three is the same as at sixty-eight, when he poured me Krasnodar tea. "And we'll move your Vitka too, if you like. Not in that sense, Andrei Nikolayevich, come now. He'll just forget what he saw."

Twenty minutes later the gray car leaves the steps. I go up to the fifth floor. Dad is small, in tubes, and points at the nightstand: there's a Magnit bag, and in the bag a canvas storm jacket with a patch on the elbow. "Granddad's, I think," he says. "Mother kept it in the trunk. For you." Dad dies two days later, at 04:12 in the morning. A week later Vitka tells the court he doesn't remember anything, and the case is closed. Vitka isn't at Dad's funeral. He isn't anywhere until spring, and in spring he calls drunk and stays silent on the line for half an hour.

## После C
"Sonya," I say into the phone, "tell him I came. Tell him I stood by the entrance."

Sonka is silent. Then she says "okay" and hangs up. I sit in my car in the far parking lot until midnight, looking at the fifth floor. The windows there are all the same.

Dad dies two days later, at 04:12 in the morning. After the funeral, which I don't come to, Sonka brings me a bundle in a Magnit bag at Yagunovsky. In the bag is a canvas storm jacket, old, with a patch on the elbow.

"Dad said to give it to you in person," says Sonka. "He said, 'Granddad's, I think.' Grandma kept it in the trunk. He was waiting for you, Andrei. He watched the door to the very end."

## После D
Vitka lives on Tereshkova, on the ground floor, his windows facing the tire shop. He opens the door to me in an undershirt, sees me and isn't surprised.

"You wanted it to my face?" he says. "Go on, look."

We sit in his kitchen until three in the morning. He tells me how they picked him up with the truck, how the investigator put a photo of his son in front of him. I tell him Dad is dying. Vitka is silent for a long time, then says, "I've already signed, Andryukh. You can't play it back." Then he adds, "In court I'll mumble. That much I can do."

Dad dies that same night, at 04:12, while we're sitting in Vitka's kitchen. Afterward Sonka brings me a Magnit bag, and in the bag a canvas storm jacket with a patch on the elbow. "Dad said to give it to you in person. He said, 'Granddad's, I think.'" In court Vitka mumbles so badly the judge twice asks him to speak up. The case falls apart by summer.
