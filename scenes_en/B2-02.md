The associate professor didn't lie: after the first exams half our year was gone. I'm in second year, I've held on, and I even work part-time as a lab assistant in the department. I wash flasks, haul instruments and listen to the grad students swear.

Zoya Mikhailovna, our senior lab technician, asks me to bring a Dewar flask of liquid nitrogen, about forty liters, up from the basement to the third floor. The stairs are being renovated, the railings removed, the steps covered in cement. That leaves the freight elevator, which has NO PASSENGERS stenciled on its doors. Everybody ignores the sign, and so do I. I wheel the flask inside on a cart; it's heavy, pot-bellied, and hisses quietly under its lid.

Between the second and third floors the elevator jerks and stops. The bulb in the ceiling flickers and stays on at half strength. I press "3," then "2," then every button in a row, like when I was a kid in our stairwell. Nothing. My Nokia has no bars; there's no signal in this iron box.

The flask's lid hisses louder. White mist creeps across the floor around my sneakers, like from a fog machine at a Mumiy Troll concert, only cold. The cold gets into my sneakers, through my socks, to my ankles. But up to my waist the cabin is warm, even a little stuffy, and smells of machine oil. I stand there watching the mist cover the floor and think it's actually kind of beautiful.

## После D
I stand on the handrail, press the top of my head against the cabin ceiling and put my face to the grille. It's warm there and smells of dust. With my free hand I bang the lab key against the wall, evenly, once a second, and don't stop even when my arm goes numb.

Twenty minutes later somebody outside shouts, "Who's in there?!" They open the elevator with a crowbar. Zoya Mikhailovna sees the mist on the floor, goes pale, first swears and then bursts into tears.

They pour tea into me in the department. My hands are shaking so hard I spill it. Everybody thinks it's the cold.

## После E
The panel by the door is old, with buttons in two rows, and under them a red mushroom button painted over along with the panel, as if nobody had ever pressed it. I push it with my palm until the paint cracks. The bulb in the ceiling goes out completely, and somewhere up in the shaft a relay clicks. Quiet. Now the elevator is dead as a wardrobe, and for some reason that makes me calmer.

I pry the doors apart with the lab key and my fingers; my nails break off to the quick. The gap is half a meter, and beyond it is the third-floor landing and a pair of brown shoes, those same ones. The shoes belong to Uncle Vitya, the watchman; he's smoking by the window and drops his cigarette when a student comes crawling up at him out of the floor. I climb out to the waist, then all the way, and scrape my stomach on the sill. My sweater catches on the edge and tears. The mist doesn't follow me through the gap; it stays lying on the cabin floor.

"Where the hell did you come from?" asks Uncle Vitya.

Ten minutes later Zoya Mikhailovna comes running with a mechanic. The mechanic shines a flashlight down at the white lake on the cabin floor, whistles, and says the elevator probably wouldn't have moved by itself, and with the Stop pressed nothing at all could have happened. I nod. My hands are shaking, and everybody thinks it's the cold.
