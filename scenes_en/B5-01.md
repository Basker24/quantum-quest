"Anywhere" turned out to be two hundred forty kilometers from home. I came to Novosibirsk on the commuter train with a single knockoff Adidas bag from the market, and for six months I've been delivering other people's envelopes around the city for Sibexpress. Metro, trolleybus, on foot, metro again. Envelopes come thin and thick, and for the thick ones you sometimes get a tip. I live in a dorm on Nemirovich-Danchenko Street, in a room with two correspondence students from Kupino who play Counter-Strike all night and yell "Rush B." By evening my legs hum like power lines.

::if F_Т09 == НИКА
I came here because of Nika; I only realize that on the commuter train, when it's too late. Nika is in her first year of medical school and lives with her mom on Gogol Street. Until December we saw each other every Saturday. In December, outside Krasny Prospekt metro station, she said, "Samokhin, you're not here for me. You're here so you're not there." I argued all the way to Gagarinskaya, and then stopped. She was right, and we both knew it.
::end

::if F_Т09 != НИКА
Nika is here too; it's her city, she's at medical school. We've seen each other twice: once at the dumpling place on Krasny, once by chance on the metro. Both times she asked what had possessed me to come here. Both times I didn't know what to say.
::end

On Sundays I call Mom from the pay phone at the post office, with a phone card. Mom talks about Sonka, about Dad's back, about prices. At the end, every time, the same thing:

"Don't you go near the river there. They say the Ob is cold."

I promise. I go to the Ob every evening, to the Kommunalny Bridge, and look at the ice. It's wider here than ours and grayer, and under it, they say, flows water from Altai itself.

In March I deliver a thick envelope to the Sibir-Exotic travel agency on Krasny Prospekt. The office is in a half-basement, with palm trees on the wall, and under the palm trees the plaster is peeling. The owner, Alla Borisovna, red bob and gold rings on every finger, signs my delivery slip and looks at me for a long time.

"Whose are you? From Kemerovo?" she asks. "Listen, Kemerovo. I need a guide in Pattaya. Not a smart one; I've got plenty of smart ones. One with an honest face. So our guys from Prokopyevsk will believe him that the crocodiles are real and the Alcazar show isn't a con. Thirty dollars a day plus commission. We'll give you a place to live. Warm, the sea. Will you go?"

"Is the water warm there?" I ask, for some reason.

"Like a bathtub," says Alla Borisovna. "What, can't you swim?"

::if F_Т06 == ПЕРЕПЛЫЛ
I can swim. The last time I swam for real I was thirteen.
::end

::if F_Т06 != ПЕРЕПЛЫЛ
I can swim, I think. The last time I swam I was seven, and it ended badly.
::end

::if F_Т03 == ВОДОБОЯЗНЬ
I haven't talked about water since I was seven, and in Novosibirsk nobody has asked me about it. But here they're asking, and my chest goes tight at once. I say "I can," as if I'd been dared.
::end

I fill out the passport application right there on her desk. Three weeks later the passport is ready; four weeks later I have a ticket, Novosibirsk to U-Tapao, on a charter, overnight, one way. The evening before the flight I sit on my bed in the dorm, the correspondence students are playing Counter-Strike, and Mom back in Kemerovo doesn't know anything yet.

> How I chose "anywhere" back then, I don't remember well. I only remember that I badly wanted to go where it was warm, and that none of us thought of it as running away.

## После A
The pay phone at Tolmachevo eats two of my cards in a row. Mom doesn't pick up right away; she's on the night shift.

"Mom, I'm flying to Thailand. For work. As a guide."

It's so quiet on the line that I can hear the flotation machine humming behind her at the processing plant. Then Mom says, "At least there's no water there?" I say there's the sea. She's silent again, and then asks whether I took a warm sweater. I didn't. At the end she says what I already knew she'd say: "Stay away from the water, Andryusha." I promise for the second time that week.

## После B
I spend twenty minutes writing the text, erase it and write it again. It comes to a handful of words and a period. I send it at the boarding stairs, when the flight attendant asks everyone to switch off their phones.

The answer comes as the plane is taxiing. One line, no commas, in capitals, the way Mom writes when she can't find the lowercase on her Nokia: "STAY AWAY FROM THE WATER." How she knows about water, I never figured out. I switch off the phone and don't look at it all the way to U-Tapao.

## После C
Nika arrives at Tolmachevo on the last bus, in her father's sheepskin coat down to her heels. We sit on metal chairs by the departures board drinking tea from a machine that tastes of the paper cup.

::if F_Т09 == НИКА
"I told you," she says. "You're not here for me. You're here for 'not there.'"
::end

::if F_Т09 != НИКА
"You're like Vitka, Samokhin," she says. "He went into the army, you're going to Thailand. Anything to get far away."
::end

At boarding she suddenly takes me by the sleeve and holds on. "Two hundred and fifty-two," she says. "Remember? I counted all the way to the end back then." Then she lets go and walks to the bus without looking back. I call Mom from the plane, right before takeoff, and only manage to say, "Mom, everything's fine." Mom manages to say, "Stay away from the water."

## После D
I bought the phrasebook in an underpass for forty rubles; on the cover is a palm tree and a smiling Thai woman. Until three in the morning I sit on the windowsill in the corridor whispering, "How much? Too expensive. Where is the hospital?" One of the correspondence students from Kupino comes out for a smoke and listens to me for a long time.

"What are you, a spy?" he says.

By morning I have forty phrases in my head, and one of them, "Help me, please," for some reason sticks harder than the rest. I text Mom from the airport: "Flying south for work, will call." Mom answers at once, as if she hadn't been asleep: "Stay away from the water."
