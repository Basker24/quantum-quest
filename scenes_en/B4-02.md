Outside it's thirty-eight below, and in the cold room it's eighteen below. It's warmer in here than outside, and that's the first thing I understand about working for the Welder. Inside his business it's always warmer than outside, and outside somebody is always standing.

A warehouse by the market, the night from Friday to Saturday. Someone sold the Welder the key to the gates, and the watchmen were bought too: "Petrovich is asleep tonight, it's all smooth." Our job is sixty boxes of chicken leg quarters from the cold room into Shurup's Gazelle van. Bush legs, everyone calls them. Every box has a smiling chick in a cowboy hat on it and the words "PRAIRIE GOLD · ARKANSAS." Shurup says the Americans' chickens are the size of dogs, that's why the legs are like that.

On the forty-second box a flashlight comes on in the yard. A yellow circle crawls across the ice from the guardhouse to the loading dock. Behind the flashlight walks a man in a sheepskin coat with a shotgun on his shoulder, barrel up. That isn't Petrovich. Petrovich may really be asleep, just not here.

Shurup sees the flashlight before I do. The Gazelle jumps forward with no headlights, swings sideways on the ice and goes out through the gates. The boxes I didn't get loaded fall out of the open back onto the snow. From the nearest box the chick in the hat smiles at me.

I'm standing in the doorway of the cold room with the forty-second box in my arms. Behind me the compressor hums. To the right, along the fence, a ring slides along a stretched cable, and behind the ring runs a dog, not barking, just its chain jingling. To the left, by the dock, stands a refrigerated truck, its back doors wide open, dark inside and boxes in there too. The flashlight is already halfway across the yard.

## После D
I break a chicken leg off from the box, yellow, rock-hard, covered in frost, and back into the cold room. The door is thick, clad in tin, and it pulls itself inward on its spring, heavily and steadily, the way water pulls you to the bottom. I shove the chicken leg into the gap, right at the floor. The door settles on it, crunches and stops. A gap two fingers wide. Street cold comes through it, thirty-eight below, and I breathe it the way you breathe at an open window.

The flashlight reaches the dock, shines on the abandoned boxes, on the Gazelle's tracks. The man in the sheepskin swears under his breath, at length, like he knows what he's doing. Then his footsteps go along the cold room, past the door, and I don't breathe. The beam passes over the gap, over the chicken leg, and moves on. He looked at the door and saw a warehouse door ajar, one of a hundred he has.

I sit on the boxes for forty minutes, until he goes back into the guardhouse and a TV starts mumbling in there. Then I come out, put the chicken leg back in its box, I don't know why, and walk home across the whole of Rudnichny. I can't feel my hands all the way home. In the kitchen Mom puts the kettle on and asks nothing; she has a shift at six in the morning, she's not sleeping anyway.

## После E
The dog runs along the fence behind the ring on the cable, and I know where the cable ends: at the lamppost, five meters from the corner of the warehouse. Past that it can't go. I throw the box into the snow and walk right along the wall, away from the fence, under the dock, bent over, on black ice where the light doesn't reach. The dog sees me and lunges. The ring hits the post and jerks her back so hard she sits down.

The man with the flashlight turns toward the dog; he doesn't see me. "What is it, Naida, a cat?" By then I'm already at the gates. The gates are open; the Gazelle left them that way.

I walk home through the private houses, across vegetable plots, knee-deep in snow. By the garages on Shakhtyorov stands the Gazelle with its headlights off. Shurup is smoking in the cab. He sees me and doesn't even pretend to be ashamed. "Alive?" he says. "Well, that's that. Don't tell the Welder I bolted; tell him we bolted together." I get into the cab, because my fingers are white and I can't feel them.

## После F
"Kuzmich!" I yell from the dark, from behind the boxes, without sticking my head out. "Don't shoot! It's Samokhin! Nikolai Samokhin's son, from the Glubokaya!"

The flashlight freezes. Then it lowers. The man's voice is hoarse, like Dad's; all of them have that voice, the ones who've spent twenty years at the coal face.

"Andryukha? Kolka's boy? What are you doing here, huh? What the hell are you doing here?"

Kuzmich was Dad's crew leader until they pensioned him off for his lungs. He takes me into the guardhouse, pours me tea from a thermos and keeps the shotgun on his knees while I drink. Then he says he won't call the cops, but he'll tell Dad. I ask him not to. He says, "I'll tell him." And on Sunday he does. After that Dad doesn't speak to me until summer, except once, in the kitchen, when he asks the ceiling, "So were the chicken legs at least any good?"
