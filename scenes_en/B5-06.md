In December the ruble crashed by half, and the Russians stopped coming. By spring the Blue Crab had four instructors left out of twelve, and I was the odd fifth. Ploy said, "Go to temple." In Thailand nearly every man does it at least once in his life: shaves his head and goes into a monastery for the rainy season, three months. His mother is happy; it earns her merit. I said I was baptized Orthodox. Ploy said the Buddha doesn't care.

It's a forest monastery near Ubon, at the edge of Isan, twenty kilometers from Laos. A wooden hall with no walls, cells on stilts, a pond and the forest. Up at four in the morning, the gong, chanting in Pali, which I don't understand, the alms round through the village barefoot with the bowl. One meal, at eight in the morning, and whatever they put in, you eat. You're supposed to talk little. The rain falls for three days at a stretch, steady, like on the garage roof in Rudnichny.

I wake before the gong. At four-twelve, every night, like when I was a kid, and I go out to the pond. After a week the abbot is waiting for me at the pond, Luang Pho Thong. He's over eighty, thin and dark as dried fish, and before the monastery he spent forty years catching squid off Trang. He speaks Thai to me slowly and in simple words, as to a child.

"You don't sleep," he says. "Why?"

And I tell him. For the first time in my life, in broken Thai, to an old man in an orange sheet. About the jellyfish at Phangan and how people stepped over me. How I died and am standing alive a minute before. That my hands and my chest remember it, though nothing happened. He listens without interrupting and scratches his heel.

"You think you're the one who dies," he says at last. "You're not the one who dies. You're the one who remembers."

"Then who dies?"

Luang Pho Thong shrugs, and his robe slips off one shoulder.

"Another one. He left you the memory. A good gift. Don't waste it."

The rain rustles on the pond. I ask him about one more thing. About the old man who pulled me out of the river when I was seven and disappeared. I've been looking for him all my life. Luang Pho looks at me for a long time, and then laughs, toothless and quiet.

"Don't look outside," he says. "You'll get old, then you'll see."

> I'll never have any other explanation. Not that I went looking for one.

## После A
I stay. I write Ploy a letter, a paper one, because phones aren't allowed here. The letter comes out short: "One more season. Sorry." The answer comes a month later, also on paper, one line in English: "Okay. Don't be monk forever." I reread it every evening.

The second rainy season by the pond is quiet as first snow. The whispering I've heard under water since I was seven can be heard here without water, at night, when the rain stops. It doesn't frighten me. Luang Pho Thong says that's normal: when it's quiet, you hear everything. At four-twelve the two of us sit by the pond in silence. A year later he gives me his old alms bowl, copper, with a dent.

## После B
I take off the robe at dawn on the last day of the season and give it to Luang Pho. He folds it in four and says, "Good. Go dive."

I buy the ring in Ubon, at the market, silver, for eight hundred baht. On the train to Surat Thani I hold it in my fist for sixteen hours. On the island Ploy stands on the pier in her white helmet and watches me come down the gangway, bald, skinny, with my fist clenched.

"What?" she says.

I open my fist. Ploy looks at the ring, then at me, then at the ring again. "Blin," she says, in Russian. We hold the wedding a month later, at the temple near her mother's, with monks, string on our wrists and chickens under the table. I send Mom a photo on Odnoklassniki. Mom answers: "Pretty. Skinny. Feed her."

## После C
"Under water there's whispering," I say. "Since I was seven. Many voices, close. Who is it?"

Luang Pho doesn't answer for a long time. Then he picks up a stick and draws circles in the wet earth by the pond, one inside another, like from a thrown stone.

"The ones who didn't come back," he says. "You remember in their place. They're near because you remember. Don't be afraid of them. And don't call them."

He says nothing more about it, not then, not later. I leave at the end of the season and think about the circles all the way to the island. Since then, under water, I listen to the whispering differently. The way you listen to neighbors through a wall. You know they're there, and what they're talking about is none of your business.

## После D
I take off the robe on the last day of the season and get on the night bus to Chumphon. As we part, Luang Pho puts a Thai coin in my palm, five baht, with a hole in the middle. "For the water," he says. What for, he doesn't explain.

There are no tourists on the island, but there are Chinese, Koreans and one Dutch family that pays for a course in cash. I take them all. Ploy takes the ones I don't. I wear the coin with the hole on a cord around my neck, and a year later I notice that under water I touch it with my fingers, the way Mom used to touch her cross.
