I run. The boot knocks against the ice, wooden, ringing, and with every step it gets heavier, as if someone were pouring lead into it. My lungs burn. The scarf on my face is a crust of ice; I rip it off, and the frost grabs my cheeks so that they stop being mine at once.

At step six hundred I fall. My leg won't bend, the knee is in a crust of ice like a cast, and I get up off one knee like an old man. At step one thousand I fall again and sit on the ice for a long time, because I can't get up on the first try, or the second. Then I get warm. First in my leg, then everywhere. I know that's wrong, they told me, but I feel good, and I take off my mitten because it's hot.

The last thing I see is the creek ahead, white, flat as a road, and the sun over the hill, small and red. The larches around me stand in hoarfrost and are silent. It smells of coal dust, of Mom's hair when she comes home from a shift.

## Пробуждение
…and I'm standing on the creek again, knee-deep in the overflow, and steam is rising off my wet boot.

I'm hot. I remember how it got hot, how I took off my mitten. I remember which step I fell on the second time: one thousand, halfway. It was two thousand steps to camp.

My foot in the boot is wet and doesn't hurt anymore. The felt on it crunches when I shift my weight, still softly, for now.
