After Epiphany I went higher. First to the upper reaches of the Mras-Su, then over the ridge to the source of the Tom, right under the bald peaks of the Abakan Range. Here there's a cabin the hunters built for me, a spring where the river begins, and three days on horseback to the nearest village. I've lived here fifteen years. In winter the snow comes up to the roof; in summer the midges are so thick you can't see the sky.

::if F_В608 == УШЁЛ
No one comes here but hunters. The channel about me went silent long ago, and people probably think I'm dead. That suits me.
::end

::if F_В608 != УШЁЛ
People find me even here. Once a summer, in twos and threes, with a guide. I give them tea, listen, and send them back. The summer before last a father and son from Osinniki built a little dam of stones on the creek below the cabin. "A font for you, Grandpa," said the son, and since then the water in the font has been waist-deep. No one calls me a saint anymore. They call me "the old man at the source."
::end

Stepan the hunter brings salt, matches, flour and letters. Letters from Sonka, once every six months. From them I know that Dad died in '26, at four-twelve in the morning, on the balcony, with an unlit cigarette, of his heart. Sonka found him in the morning; he was sitting on a stool as if alive. A death like that has no fork. The Choir is silent about Dad; Dad wasn't one of mine.

::if СВЯЗЬ_НИКА >= 40
Nika comes once a year, in August, when the midges are over. With a guide, on horseback, and she's nearly sixty. She stays a week, treats Stepan's kids for bronchitis, sleeps on the bunk by the stove. Every time she says I've lost my mind. And every time, leaving, she says I'm the most normal person she knows, I just sleep badly.
::end

Tonight the Choir wakes me at four-twelve, as always. Only tonight there are new voices in it.

They sound in my pitch. My voice, my way of drawing out "we-ell," my cough. Only they speak in someone else's words. One with a Moscow accent, one in English, the third a woman. My own I've been able to tell apart since I was twenty; each has his own death. These have no death. One crashed his car, and right away, within a breath, he's driving again, down the same road, and changes lanes. They don't die. They rewind.

::if ДАР >= 60
I can make out the words. A man in Moscow says "rebooted, thank God," as if about a computer. The woman is counting money, a lot of money. And in each of their heads, somewhere at the very bottom, there's one place that sounds like me. Like me at seven, under water.
::end

::if ДАР < 60
I can't make out the words. They hum like power lines in a frost, and they hum on my note.
::end

::if ДАР >= 70
Under them, very quietly, a child is crying. This voice isn't mine. A child's, with a whistle in it, about nine, and he's not from my worlds. He's crying out of the same water as my seven-year-old, only earlier, before me.
::end

In the morning Stepan comes. He brings salt and his tablet, which has his books and the weather on it. He shows me an ad he sees everywhere now, even in his village: a calm face, a white coat, the words: "Second Chance. You don't have to die. The Orpheus clinic, Novosibirsk, Krasny Avenue."

"What's this?" asks Stepan. "This is your sort of thing."

## После A
For three nights I listen to them. They don't know I hear them. They don't even know they're hearing anyone; they think it's their own luck. The man in Moscow rewound four times in a year. The woman in Dubai twice. There are hundreds of them, and more every month.

On the third night I find a doctor among them. He gave himself the injection, the same one he gives them. In his head, like a stamp on a can of stew, lies one line from a document he's read many times: "Source matrix S-96. Carrier blood sample, two test tubes, Tomsk, 1998." Ninety-six. Two test tubes. I'm holding a mug, and the mug rattles against the table.

I tell Stepan, "That's my blood." He looks at me, then at his tablet, and switches it off.

## После B
I don't listen to my own. I listen underneath, where the little one is crying.

"Who are you?" I ask out loud, and behind the stove Stepan stops snoring.

"Mitya," says the child's voice. "Lunyov. From 12 Shakhtyorskaya Street. I've been in your water since '88. Papa waited for me there every July. And then he found you. He took your blood at the hospital, two test tubes, thought it would bring me back. It didn't. And in '98 he had no money, and he sold them. Now they have your voice."

"Where are you, Mitya?"

"Under Krasnaya Gorka. Where the old adit goes under the riverbed. The water dragged me there. Papa doesn't know. Tell him."

In the morning I stand for a long time by the spring where the Tom begins, watching the water go downhill. Vadim Petrovich Lunyov, Granddad's graduate student. The doctor from Tomsk who rubbed his eyes. He'd be over ninety now, if he's alive.

## После C
I spend all night writing the letter to Nika, three pages, with the indelible pencil I've had since Altai. About the voices in my pitch. About how they don't die. About Stepan's ad. Stepan will take the letter to the village, and from there someone will photograph it and send it.

The answer comes a month later, with Stepan, as a printout. "Samokhin. I went to their place on Krasny. There's a line of black cars. My head of department says they have a method based on cold-induced clinical death, four minutes, and that their source material is from Siberia, from the nineties. I told him I knew whose. He asked me not to make jokes like that again. Take care of yourself. Sleep. N."

## После D
I chop wood for two days. Stepan watches me chop and then leaves without saying anything. The woodpile grows taller than the window. By the evening of the second day my hands won't close into fists.

The voices in my pitch don't go away because of it. They hum at night, steadily, on my note, and keep rewinding: someone in Moscow, someone by the sea, someone in Novosibirsk. I lie down on the bunk and think that maybe this is what people have said about me all my life. That I've lost my mind. And then I remember Stepan's tablet and the white coat, and I know I haven't.
