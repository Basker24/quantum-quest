They shut down the bootleg pits a month after our roof came down. One of the Kiselyovsk guys talked, someone wrote it down, and one of the cops suddenly stopped taking money. For six months Welder sat under his umbrella and didn't smile. Then he went to Novokuznetsk to win it back and lost more than he had.

Casinos were banned here three years ago, back in '09. People didn't gamble any less; they just moved into basements. This basement used to be a bowling alley. The lanes are still there in the dark, and at the far end stand the pins, white as teeth. In the middle they've put a green table, and on it two glasses of water and a revolver, an old Nagant with the bluing worn off. Around it stand men in suits, holding glasses, twenty or so. The owner is called Baldy, though he has beautiful gray hair down to his shoulders.

The game is called the Drum. Two players, one cartridge; you spin the cylinder, put it to your temple, pull. Then the other one. The onlookers bet on who'll get up from the table. Whoever gets up takes the pot.

"You're lucky," Welder told me in the car. "Got out of the cold room, got out of the pit. Sit at the table for a bit, they write off my debt and you get half a million. You won't die, Andryukh. I know your type."

He doesn't know. He senses it. I've never told anyone that I come back.

Across from me sits Tyoma from Osinniki. He's twenty, in a sweater with reindeer on it and a little cross on a string, and he keeps touching the cross. He owes Baldy for his brother, who smashed somebody's car. Tyoma smiles at me the way you smile at the person next to you in line at the dentist. His hands are wet; he wipes them on his knees.

Baldy lays the Nagant on the felt, grip toward me.

"Guest goes first," he says.

A chill runs up my back, into the back of my head, behind my ears, like in an elevator when it starts moving. The onlookers stop clinking their glasses.

## После B
The Nagant is heavy and warm from someone else's hand. I spin the cylinder; it clicks like a bicycle wheel. I raise it to my temple. Somewhere in another world, one fork away from here, I'm already lying face down on the felt, I remember that, and the one who's lying there stays there, and I pull. Click. The onlookers all breathe out at once, and someone claps.

I lay the Nagant on the table, grip toward Tyoma. Tyoma picks it up, crosses himself, spins the cylinder for a long time, listening to it the way you listen to a watermelon. Raises it to his temple. Looks at me. Says, "Well, God help me."

The shot is louder in the basement than I expected. Tyoma lies with his cheek on the felt, eyes open. The onlookers pay each other. Baldy wipes his hands with a napkin and says to someone, "Clean that up." They hand me a Pyaterochka supermarket bag with half a million in five-thousand notes. In the car Welder hugs me and cries, real tears, and says, "I knew it, I knew it." I hold the bag on my knees all the way to Kemerovo. Tyoma's sweater had reindeer on it.

> That was the first death I sold. It wasn't mine.

## После C
I stand up. The chair scrapes on the concrete. Baldy looks at me with interest, without anger, the way you look at a dog that's refused to jump over the stick.

"Sit down," says Welder.

I walk to the stairs. They whistle at my back, first one, then all of them, whistling like at a stadium, and someone throws a glass at me. Tyoma stays at the table alone, looking from me to the Nagant and back.

In the parking lot two of Baldy's men are waiting for me. They beat me competently, ribs and kidneys, leaving the face alone. Then one squats down and says, "The debt's on your Welder now. And you, take a walk." I lie in the snow by the wheel of someone's Mercedes looking at the streetlight. Tyoma comes out of the basement twenty minutes later, alive, and walks past without looking. They don't have a game without a second player.

## После D
I stand up, go around the table and take Tyoma by the hand. His hand is wet and ice-cold. He doesn't resist; he gets up as if in a dream and follows me, the little cross bouncing on his chest.

"Hey, hey," says Baldy. "Boys."

They beat the two of us in the parking lot for a long time. Tyoma less, me more, because I'm covering him. Then one of them says, "His debt's on you now, got it?" I've got it. Tyoma cries into the snow and says his brother will kill him. I take him to the bus for Osinniki and give him all the money I have, three hundred rubles and some metro tokens for a metro we don't have; somebody gave them to me in Novosibirsk.

## После E
I spin the cylinder and pull, and the click comes out dry, like a light switch. Tyoma pulls after me, for a long time, eyes closed. I spin a second time without waiting for Baldy to nod. One fork away from here I'm lying with my cheek on the felt, two forks away I'm lying there too, and every time the one who gets up from the table is the one who stayed here. On the third round Tyoma crosses himself, pulls, and the basement gets loud.

"Clean that up," says Baldy, wiping his hands with a napkin. Then he looks at me. "Welder owes me the same again. Sit with the next one, and I'll write off the lot, to the last kopeck."

They seat the next one ten minutes later, a man of about forty in a leather jacket, with a gold signet ring. I spin and pull twice in a row and look at him. He picks up the Nagant, holds it on his palm, puts it back down on the felt and stands up. He says one short word and walks to the stairs, and nobody whistles at him. Baldy tears Welder's IOU in half and gives me both halves and the Pyaterochka bag.
