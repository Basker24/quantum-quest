I passed the instructor exam in January, on the third try. Theory, the pool, "rescuing a tired diver," "rescuing a panicked diver." On the "panicked diver" the examiner, a bearded Australian, drowned me twice, and both times I was so angry afterward I couldn't eat. Both times I swam at him from the front and got a fin in the mask. The third time, out of sheer anger, I dived under him from below and grabbed his tank valve. Ploy said, "Good. Angry is good. Scared is bad." The third time the Australian said, "Okay, mate," and that was the best thing I'd ever heard in my life.

::body seen_life(✝В5-02.A)
Before every dive I check my air three times, though the gauges show the same thing all three times. Underwater I breathe evenly and count my breaths, but on the surface my hands shake for another minute.
::end

Now I have students of my own. This morning it's Vika from Barnaul, twenty-six, a manager at a cell phone store, here for ten days and wanting "the deep one." That means thirty meters, by the rock at Chumphon Pinnacle, where the big fish go. All the way out on the boat Vika laughs, chatters, checks her depth gauge, checks her depth gauge again, and her fingers tremble slightly. Mom would say she has wind in her head. Ploy says one word to me in Thai and points at Vika's fingers.

At twenty meters everything is fine. At twenty-five Vika waves at me about a fish, huge, gray, the size of a man. At thirty she suddenly stops looking at the fish.

She's looking up. Up there, far away, the surface glows like a window in a basement ceiling. Vika takes the regulator out of her mouth, looks at it as if she doesn't understand what it is, and kicks her fins.

You hold a panicked diver from behind. By the tank valve, so they can't reach you with their hands, and right away the spare regulator in their mouth, the yellow one, from my shoulder. Don't go at them from the front; they'll drown you. The Australian drummed that into me twice, through the mouth and through the nose.

The water at thirty meters holds me the same way the Black Backwater held me when Nika and I swam across it at thirteen. It's my own water, and in it you don't have to hurry.

::if ЭМП >= 55
Behind the glass of her mask I see her eyes, white all around the pupils. She doesn't see me; she sees only the window above. That's how Tolik from Prokopyevsk looked on the sand at Phangan when his leg got stung. That's how everyone looks who thinks they're about to die.

You can't go up from thirty meters; that's the first thing they tell you on any course. You'll shoot up like a cork, and your blood will fizz like soda from a bottle.

## После A
I catch up to her in two kicks, come around behind her and grab the tank valve. She thrashes like a fish on a stringer, but she can't reach me with her hands. I shove the yellow regulator into her mouth; she bites it and takes a breath. Bubbles stream upward, lots of them, and that means she's breathing.

We hang at twenty-eight meters, me holding her, her holding the regulator in her teeth. After a minute she stops thrashing. After another minute she raises a hand and makes a ring with her fingers: "okay." The ascent takes eighteen minutes, with stops, and the whole time I hold her by the vest.

On the boat Vika cries, then laughs, then cries again. "I thought there was air up there," she says. Ploy gives her sweet tea, and gives me nothing, only lays her palm on my wet neck and keeps it there a little longer than she needs to.

## После B
I let her go. Vika shoots up like a cork, feet down, and melts into the light. I go up on my own, slowly, by the computer, with a stop at five meters, three minutes. I count those three minutes out loud, into the regulator, and the bubbles carry my count up.

On the boat Vika throws up over the side. Half an hour later her left leg goes numb; an hour later a speedboat takes her to Samui, to the hyperbaric chamber. They pull her through there. For the rest of her life her leg will go numb when the weather changes, like old women's knees before rain.

"You did right," Ploy says that evening. "By the book."

I nod. I know that book by heart. That night I dream I'm holding Vika by the tank valve, and I wake up with my fingers clenched into a fist.

## После C
I don't grab her. I swim up in front of her, at arm's length, and catch her eye through two panes of glass. I push the spare regulator at her and show her with my palm, down and up, slowly: in, out. In. Out. The way Ploy once did for me at twenty meters.

She looks at my hand, then at me. She takes the regulator and breathes in. Then again. I hold my palm in front of her mask, up and down, up and down, until the bubbles come evenly.

On the boat Vika says, "You were looking at me like that. Like my mom when I was sick as a kid." Ploy hears this and turns away, and I see that she's smiling.

## После D
She kicks her fins, and I remember where her heel will land if I come from the front. In the mask, in the glass, so the mask flies off. I move to the side and up, come around behind her and grab the tank valve.

She bites the yellow regulator like a bit. Bubbles stream upward, lots of them. After two minutes she raises a hand in a ring. The ascent takes eighteen minutes, and the whole time my chest burns, deep under the ribs, where another time something burst.

On the boat I sit down on the bench and cough. Vika asks what's wrong with me. I say I swallowed some water. Ploy looks at me silently for a long time, and then gives me her tea.

## После E
I drop down, a meter under her fins, and they beat the water above me, missing. From below everything looks different: her knees, the buckle of her weight belt, the yellow hose. I come up behind her and take the tank by the valve, the way the Australian showed me on the third try, with that anger after which you aren't afraid.

She jerks, but can't reach me with her hands. I put my spare regulator in her mouth and hold her head with the back of it toward me until the bubbles come evenly. Then the two of us hang at thirty meters, and I count her breaths on my fingers in front of her mask.

We take forty minutes to go up, with stops. On the boat Vika sits hugging her knees and doesn't say a word, and in the evening she brings me a can of beer and sets it on the dive center steps without looking at me.
