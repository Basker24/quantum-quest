::if !F_ЗАКЛЕИЛ

Seryoga, at the shearer, takes off a glove and shows me his phone. On the screen is a fat baby in a knitted bobble hat: Mishka, ten months old.

"Six teeth," says Seryoga. "At night he screams like the siren at the pithead. Nika carries him around and counts her steps. Says she got to a thousand and he's still screaming."

## После A
In the top corner I measure the gas. Point eight, normal; the sensor shows the same. I write the number in my notebook next to the date and stand there by the sensor a long time, I don't know why. Then I go back to the shearer, and the shift ends the way hundreds of shifts end, with the bathhouse and the canteen.

There's nobody alive at the face. We carry out nine men on stretchers, one at a time. At the junction I shine my lamp on the fixed sensor and see a bag. White, with handles, wrapped in electrical tape. I take it off and put it in the evidence sack, and at that moment my hands aren't shaking at all.

## После C
I go to the shearer's control panel and hit the emergency stop. The shearer goes quiet, and Seryoga cusses me out. I go up to the top corner and rip the bag off the sensor, tape and all, and the sensor starts howling right away: three point eight, four, four point two. There's enough gas at the face for a good explosion, and it's been building up here for a year under the bag I put on myself.

I drive everyone off the face, some with swearing, some by the collar. Bondarenko comes running down the drift toward me, red-faced, and stops when he sees the bag in my hand. He doesn't say anything. The degassing units run at the face for three days, and the shearer is only switched on again on the fourth.

The men I drove out don't talk to me for a week; their shift got cut. Seryoga does. That evening he catches up with me at the bus stop and without a word stuffs an Alyonka chocolate bar, the one with the girl in the headscarf, into my jacket pocket; he'd bought it for Mishka. I never did eat it. It's still lying around somewhere.
