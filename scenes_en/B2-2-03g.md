In spring there's a new craze in the city: jumping off the Kuznetsky Bridge feet first and posting the video. The boys stand outside the railings one at a time, count to three and drop twelve meters. Some get a thousand views, some a hundred thousand. On TV those same days they talk about rich old men to whom some foundation sells a "second chance" after death, and the boys in the smoking spot crack up that they've got no use for that.

In the evening, as always, I walk along the embankment to Pritomsky and back, and then climb up onto the Kuznetsky Bridge. A hundred and twelve steps up from the water; I counted them long ago. On the third span there's a knot of boys with phones, all looking down over the railings.

Yegor is standing outside the railings, on a ledge as wide as a palm, holding the handrail with one hand. Under him, straight down, is the bridge pier with its icebreaker, gray, wet, sharp. Yegor isn't planning to jump down but out, to clear the pier and hit the water beyond it.

## После B
I stop three steps from the railings and tell Yegor that the pier under him sticks out two meters farther than it looks from above. That the water beyond the pier is deep, but the current there pins you against the pillar. That feet first means going in legs down, and the way he's standing, he'll go in sideways. I say it evenly, the way I explain Archimedes' principle on the board.

::if ЭМП >= 45
Yegor listens, then looks down, then at me. The boys stop filming. He climbs back over the railings, slowly, and sits down on the asphalt with his back to them. "Sideways, huh," he says, and spits. We walk home together. He doesn't say a word all the way to Krasnaya Gorka.
::end

## После C
Dimka comes running four minutes later in rubber boots and pulls Yegor off the ledge by the scruff, like a kitten. The boys scatter. Yegor doesn't cry or argue, just looks at me over his father's shoulder.

He doesn't come to me anymore. On the embankment I hear from the boys that the Vodyanoy ratted. A week later Dimka gives me back the key to the hut, without a word.

## После D
I lie down on the bridge, on the warm asphalt, and push my arm between the railing posts. Yegor's ankle is thin and wet with sweat. I grip it hard, the way you grip an oar, and tell him to crouch down now, slowly.

He jerks when he realizes he's being held. My shoulder cracks, and my arm goes numb to the elbow. But the bridge holds me, and I hold him. The boys help drag him back, and he sits down next to me on the asphalt, white, his lips trembling.

They set my shoulder at the emergency clinic on Shakhtyorov. Ever since, it aches before rain, and I don't treat it.

## После E
The Choir answers at once, with a hum. In the neighboring worlds Yegor jumps off this ledge many times, and almost always it goes fine. Only when he lets go of the handrail to wave does he fall not out but down. Under the third span, two meters underwater, lies a concrete slab that was dumped there from Pritomsky in '39 and never pulled out.

I tell him about the slab, loudly, in front of everyone. Yegor doesn't believe me, but he stops smiling. Then he climbs back over, and a week later he dives by the pier with a mask and comes to show me a photo: the slab is there, with weed growing on it.
