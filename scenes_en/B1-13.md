They call me after eleven at night. It's Mishka Pakhomov, Seryoga's son, platoon commander in the mine rescue unit, forty-six years old, and on the phone his voice sounds like Seryoga's in 2010, when he lost his helmet.

"Andrei Nikolaich," he says. "Some kids from Rudnichny crawled into the adit under the Gorka. Three of them. Two came out, Pashka didn't. Eight years old. You know that adit; you've seen it on the plan."

I know the adit. 1939, from Krasnaya Gorka under the riverbed to the old incline under Rudnichny. In '44 I followed its plan to Granddad's door. Back then its mouth was underwater, but for three summers now, since the Tom went, it's been sticking out of the bluff as a brick arch, and kids run in there to scare each other.

I put on a striped sailor's undershirt and Granddad's canvas storm jacket, the one with the patch on the elbow. For thirty-four years it hung in my front hall, and I never once wore it underground. At the mouth of the adit Mishka's guys look at my storm jacket and are politely silent. They're in new suits with built-in lights, with breathing sets the size of a thermos. Half of them went to the Wager in '55; that's what they called the war where everybody was sure he wouldn't die, because somebody had bought him a Second Chance. Not all of them came back. Mishka did.

The Choir at the back of my head hums low and steady and pulls me into the arch, under the riverbed, in the direction where, under the left bank, Black Backwater is waiting. It pulls the way home pulls after a long trip away.

> Can you get everyone out? I asked myself that all my life, since '09. That night they gave me a short answer.

## После A
I go first, the guys behind me, Mishka at the rear. The adit is low, a brick vault, a thin stream runs along the floor, and the farther we go, the deeper it gets. At the fork, where an old working branches off the adit, I say, "Right," though there's nothing there on the plan. The back of my neck tells me.

We find Pashka at half past two, in a dead end a hundred meters past the fork. He's sitting on a rock hugging his knees, and his flashlight has died. He isn't crying. When the light falls on him, he says, "I was counting. I got to a thousand, and then I lost count." Mishka wraps him in a foil blanket and carries him up top himself, in his arms.

"Andrei Nikolaich, come on," says Mishka.

"Go ahead," I say. "I'll take a look farther on, in case there's a second one."

There isn't a second one, and Mishka knows it. But he doesn't know how to argue with me; Seryoga raised him that way. I go on down the stream, and the adit dips under the riverbed, and the water rises to my knees, my waist, my chest. The vault goes down into the water. I take a breath, like at seven by the snag, and dive. The gas detector says 04:08. Up above, through the black, the water starts to grow light, though at four in the morning there's nothing to light it.

## После B
I leave the guys at the arch, tie a rope around my waist and go alone. Mishka curses at my back, quietly, in Seryoga's words. The adit is low, a brick vault, a stream runs along the floor. At the fork I turn right, though there's nothing there on the plan. The back of my neck tells me.

I find Pashka at half past two, in a dead end. His flashlight has died, and he's sitting on a rock hugging his knees. "I was counting," he says when the light falls on him. "To a thousand, and then I lost count." I give him my lamp, number two hundred forty-six, and put him on the rope. "Follow it up, hand over hand. There are men up there. Give the lamp to Auntie Tanya, got it?" He nods and goes, shining it at his feet. The rope jerks twice, then twice more. He made it.

I untie the rope from my waist. Without a lamp the adit is as dark as it was in '86 behind Granddad's door. I go on down the stream by feel, and the water rises to my knees, my waist, my chest. The vault goes down into the water. I take a breath, like at seven by the snag, and dive. Up above, through the black, the water starts to grow light, though at four in the morning there's nothing to light it.

## После C
"Mishka," I say. "Right at the fork, about a hundred meters. There's a dead end. He's there."

Mishka looks at me, at my storm jacket, nods and leads the guys into the arch. I sit down on a rock at the edge of the bluff. Tanya sits next to me and pours me tea from her thermos, strong and sweet, like on a train, like in the lamp room.

At half past two the radio at Tanya's ear crackles in Mishka's voice: "Found him. Alive. Says he counted to a thousand." Tanya switches off the radio and says, "There." She doesn't say anything else.

It still pulls at me. Tanya sees it; she's seen everything since '09. She takes my arm, and we climb down the bluff onto the dry riverbed and walk over the stones to the backwater, slowly, the way old people walk. The comet hangs over us. Tanya carries the lamp herself, shining it at my feet.
