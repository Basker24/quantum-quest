The last forty kilometers I walk along the dry riverbed. Below Kuzedeevo the Tom still runs as a stream, but by Kemerovo it disappears entirely. Underfoot are stones, shells, rusty saucepans, cow bones, a Lada upside down. I walk along the channel, the very place where in '96 I was afraid to swim past the buoys, and hundreds of people are going the same way, downstream, with folding chairs, children and dogs. For the comet.

Sonka meets me at Krasnaya Gorka. She's sixty-three, gray, in Mom's flowered headscarf. The hunters passed her my note back in May, through Stepan, and since the twentieth of July she's come here every evening, to the foot of the hill, to wait.

"You came," she says.

I nod. She looks up at me for a long time, the way she looked at nine when I came home from the hospital, and suddenly laughs.

"What a beard. Mom would say you look like Karl Marx."

Mom died in '50, in her sleep, in winter. Sonka wrote to me then, three pages, and Stepan carried the letter for two months. I found out when the snow had already melted.

"Right to the end she set a plate for you," says Sonka. "At New Year's. At the end of the table. And wouldn't let anyone sit there."

Over Rudnichny, low, the comet is already visible. The Choir in my head is silent. For the first time in fifty-four years they say nothing, not a single word, and they're silent the way people on a platform are silent when the train has already been announced.

## После A
"I'll go down," I tell Sonka. "Take a look at the comet."

"Go," she says. She says nothing more, only straightens the collar of my storm jacket, the way Mom used to before school.

I go down onto the dry riverbed. People all around are setting up chairs, pouring tea from thermoses; children run over the stones with flashlights. I walk past them toward the backwater, and the crowd thins little by little. People don't go near the water. They say a cold comes off it.

## После B
"Nika," I say. "Will you come with me?"

She gets up, leaning on her cane, and gives the cane to Sonka.

"Twenty years I've waited for you to ask," she says. "You're an idiot, Samokhin. Let's go."

Sonka watches us go. We go down onto the riverbed together, Nika holding my elbow, and I carry the thermos. Halfway there she says, "If anything happens, I'll count. I'm good at counting, if you remember." I remember.

## После C
Sonka lives in our five-story block in Rudnichny, in the same apartment. The kitchen has the same oilcloth, only a different color, and the same view from the window. We sit until three drinking tea. Sonka tells me about her grandchildren, about Mom, about Dad on the balcony. I tell her about Serafim, about Stepan, about Granny Nyura with her vegetable patch in the middle of an empty city.

At three I get up. Sonka doesn't ask where I'm going. She sees me to the door and suddenly says:

"Andryukh. Am I really not in there?"

"You're not in there, Sonya."

"Good, then," she says, like at fourteen, and closes the door behind me.
