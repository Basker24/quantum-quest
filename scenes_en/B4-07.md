We celebrate the eighth of March in Mom's kitchen, as always: Olivier salad, herring under a fur coat, a Napoleon cake from layers she bakes the night before. Mimosa in a pickle jar. Sonka has her headphones on; she's sixteen, studying biology right at the table and eating Olivier without looking at her plate.

::if F_В406 == ВЫИГРАЛ
Sonka is aiming for medical school. I pay for her chemistry and biology tutors, two thousand an hour, in cash, from the bag in my wardrobe under the sweaters. Mom thinks I earn money driving cars over from Vladivostok. Sonka doesn't think about any of that; her exams are in two years. The new Bosch fridge hums in the corner; Mom still strokes it when she walks past.
::end

::if F_В406 != ВЫИГРАЛ
I have no money. Welder's debt is on me now, fifteen thousand a month to Baldy, and I drive coal in the Bychok two shifts a day. Mom sees it and keeps quiet. My ribs healed wrong, and when I laugh, it stabs on the right.
::end

::drift ДРЕЙФ >= 10
Mom sets a plate in front of me and says, "Eat, Seryozha." Then she hears herself and laughs, covering her mouth with her hand. "Oh, what am I saying." There's no Seryozha in our family and never has been, not among relatives, not among neighbors. Sonka takes off one headphone and looks at Mom. Mom laughs again and goes for bread, though the bread is on the table.
::end

Dad sits at the head of the table in the shirt he wears only on holidays and keeps silent until the third shot. At the third he sets his glass down and says, looking at the mimosa:

"You're no son of mine while you're with Welder. If you want to be my son, come without him."

Mom says, "Kolya." Dad gets up and goes out to the balcony to smoke. I stay sitting. Sonka takes off her other headphone.

Nika catches me in the yard when I go out to my car. I haven't seen her in about eight years. Short hair, a puffer jacket, a voice recorder in her hand. She works in Novosibirsk now, for a website, writing about what the newspapers don't write about.

::if F_Т09 == НИКА
The last time we saw each other was at the station, in June '06, after her aunt's dacha. She waved to me from the commuter train window. Then there was one text on graduation night, and that was all.
::end

::if F_Т06 == ТАЙНА_НИКИ
"When you were thirteen you told me about the darkness under the water," she says instead of hello. "I believed you back then, the only one in the whole yard. And now here I am writing about what's under the ground."
::end

"I'm writing about the bootleg pits outside Kiselyovsk," she says. "About the one where the roof came down in 2010, with Vitka down below, my brother, in case you forgot. And about a certain basement in Novokuznetsk. I was told you'd been there. Tell me the truth, Andrei. You, at least."

> With Nika, in this life, I never once managed to just talk. Somebody always had a recorder running.

## После A
We sit in my car in the yard for three hours, until her recorder's battery dies. I tell her about the cold room with the chicks, the birch grove, aspen and larch. I tell her about the basement the way it was.

::if F_В406 == ВЫИГРАЛ
She knows more about Tyoma from Osinniki than I do. He was found a week later in a ditch off the highway, near Myski: "traffic accident, driver fled the scene." Nika silently shows me his photo on her phone, from his social media page, in the reindeer sweater. I look at the photo until the screen goes dark by itself.
::end

::if F_В406 != ВЫИГРАЛ
I tell her about Tyoma too. Nika says that a month after that night they sat another kid down at that table, from Prokopyevsk, and buried him in a closed coffin. I didn't know. I thought if you got up, that was the end of it.
::end

The article comes out in April. My name isn't in it; there's "a source who worked at the pits." Welder reads the article on his phone under his umbrella and looks at me over his glasses for a long time.

## После B
"I don't know anything, Nik," I say. "I haul coal. It's rumors."

She looks at me the way she used to look at Vitka when we were kids and he lied about getting top marks. She switches off the recorder. I drive her to the station for the last commuter train to Novosibirsk; we're silent the whole way, and only on the platform does she say, "You used to be a worse liar, Samokhin." The train leaves. I stand there until the red lights go out.

The article comes out in April, without me. It talks about "a local businessman nicknamed Welder." Welder pins up a printout in his booth and writes on it in marker: "Star."

## После C
I drive her to the scrap yard, saying the whole way that Welder will tell her himself, he's got nothing to hide. Welder welcomes her like family, pours her tea from a thermos, and then two of his guys take her recorder and phone and walk her to a car. They don't hit her. They just put her in the car and drive her out to Yurga, to the highway, and drop her off in the snow.

Nika calls me at midnight from some traffic police post, on someone else's phone.

::if !one_life()
"I've known you since you were seven," she says. "I counted two hundred and fifty-two seconds for you. I won't do it again."
::end

::if one_life()
"I've known you since you were seven," she says. "I counted forty seconds for you. I won't do it again."
::end

She hangs up. The article comes out in May, twice as angry as it might have been. My name is in it.

## После D
The notebook has been lying in my garage for seven years, in a box from a Sega. A thick one, ninety-six sheets, columns ruled in. "Date," "Who," "What," "Weight," "How much paid." In the "Who" column is half the city: cops, guys from the city administration, the section chief from the Glubokaya who sells off cable from the mine.

Nika leafs through the notebook in the car under the dome light and stops breathing on the third page. "Andrei," she says. "Do you understand what you've given me?" I do.

The article comes out in May. They arrest Welder in July, right under his umbrella, in front of witnesses. Nobody touches me. Nobody looks for the notebook, because I'm the only one who knows about it, and now Nika.
