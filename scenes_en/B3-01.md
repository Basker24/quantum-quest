From Tashtagol to Mundybash I ride in a "loaf" van full of crates, sitting on the crates, because the van has no seats. The crates hold rocks. Every crate has a number in marker, and the whole way I read those numbers the way I used to read railcars at the crossing as a kid: zero-forty-seven slash twelve, zero-forty-seven slash thirteen. The driver smokes out the window and says the rocks go to Novosibirsk for analysis, and from there they write back that there's nothing in them. "Twenty years we've been hauling them like that," he says, and laughs.

The party's base is in an old school at the edge of the settlement. Tarpaulin is drying on the porch, two saddled horses stand by the fence, and the yard smells of horse manure, diesel and damp foot wrappings. Above the roof are the mountains, dark, in shaggy forest, and at the very top of one of them a bare gray crown, like Dad's bald spot.

"That's a golets, a bald peak," says the woman on the porch, without saying hello. "You'll go up there next year, if you live that long. Zoya Arkadyevna. Senior geologist. Show me your hands."

I show her. She looks at my palms, looks for a long time at the scar from the snag, and doesn't ask anything about it. She's about fifty-five, with short gray hair, a Belomor in the corner of her mouth and a canvas jacket bleached almost white at the shoulders. Her surname is Kim, and everyone at the base just calls her Kim, even the boss.

Zoya Arkadyevna takes a hammer out of a crate by the porch. It doesn't look like the one we have at home: a long birch handle, polished yellow by palms, a blunt face on one end and a sharp pick on the other. She gives it to me.

"Yours. Lose it, and I'll take it out of your pay, and your pay is such that you'll still end up owing."

Then she picks up a gray rock from the step and strikes it with her own hammer, not hard, a glancing blow. The rock rings, thin and long, like a glass when you run a wet finger round the rim.

"Sound," she says. "And this one?" She strikes another. That one answers dull and short: tuk. "Crack inside. You can't see it from outside, but it told you itself. A rock doesn't lie, Samokhin. The one who lies is the one who didn't listen to it."

In the corner of the yard, by the horses, an old Shor man squats on his heels mending a bridle with an awl. He hasn't once raised his head while we've been talking. Zoya Arkadyevna nods at him:

"Miron Todyshev. The horses are his, the mountains are his too; we're guests here. Our boss is Gusev, but Gusev is in Novosibirsk, and we're here. Choose where you'll go. One job, three places."

## После A
By evening the sample pack weighs as much as Sonka, and by the end of the route as much as Sonka with her school bag. Zoya Arkadyevna walks ahead, traveling light, with just her hammer, and every two hundred steps she stops, chips a piece off the rock face, looks at it through a hand lens, wraps it in paper and labels it. Then she hands it to me. I put it in the pack. Eight hours of that.

On the third day I have bloody stripes on my shoulders from the straps. Zoya Arkadyevna looks at them, takes two pieces of felt out of her jacket pocket and silently shoves them under the straps. The felt smells of her Belomors.

"Rock is heavy because there's time in it," she says that evening by the fire. "A million years per centimeter. Today you hauled forty million. Don't whine."

I don't whine. I lie in my sleeping bag and think that forty million years weigh exactly as much as my back hurts.

## После B
She teaches me for a week, and all week I tap. On cliffs, on boulders in the stream, on the school's threshold, on the stove. Granite rings high, limestone dull, shale rustles and flakes like an old book. Sandstone answers as if it can't be bothered.

"You're listening, but you need to look," Zoya Arkadyevna snaps. "Ringing is for fools."

But she listens too, I can see it. Before she strikes, she always freezes for a second, like Dad before opening the stairwell door after a shift. On the seventh day she gives me a box of twenty rocks without labels and tells me to name them all. I name sixteen. Four I get wrong, but about one I say there's a crack in it, and she splits it on the corner of a crate, and the crack is there. Zoya Arkadyevna looks at the halves for a long time, then at me, and writes something in her field log.

## После C
Miron Todyshev doesn't say a word to me for three days. I groom the horses, take them to water at the Kondoma, haul oats. On the fourth day he points his awl at a gelding, a chestnut with a white star on its forehead.

"Chalkan," says Miron. "He's afraid of thunderstorms. You're afraid too. You'll get along."

I'm not afraid of thunderstorms, I'm afraid of water, but you can't argue with Miron; I understand that right away. In the evening he sits by the fire smoking his pipe and looking at the mountain, at the bare crown where the forest ends. Then he says, to no one in particular, that on the golets live the ones who didn't make it. They aren't evil. They just call, because they're lonely. I ask who didn't make it. He shrugs and knocks his pipe out against his heel.
