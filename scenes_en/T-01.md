In the summer of ninety-six our yard smells of tar, poplar fluff and coal, because here everything smells of coal. Even Mom's hair smells of coal when she gets home from her shift at the coal-washing plant. I'm seven. I own half a bike (a Kama with one pedal missing; the other half is Vitka's), a Turbo bubblegum wrapper with a red Lamborghini on it, and the whole of August.

Nobody's allowed to go to Black Backwater. The old woman on the ground floor says a boy from Shakhtyorskaya Street drowned there in nineteen-eighty-something and they never found him, because there are old piles there, and a vodyanoy, the old man who lives under the water and grabs kids by the ankles. Vitka says there's no such thing as a vodyanoy, but the piles are real, and they suck you under. So that's exactly where we go.

There are three of us. Vitka is a year older, ginger and freckled all over, the loudest kid in the yard, and he lies that he swims like a dolphin. He's seen dolphins on TV. Nika is his cousin from Novosibirsk; her parents sent her to us for the whole summer "for the fresh air." Mom says there's nothing here to breathe but dust and chemicals, but Nika likes it. She has a panama hat with cherries on it, a face as serious as the vice principal's, and a ginger puppy called Bublik who doesn't know the word "no" yet. And Nika counts everything in the world: the steps in our stairwell, even how long Vitka can stay under the water in the bathtub. Vitka lasts forty seconds and then tells everybody it was a hundred.

The water in the backwater is black, like the tea Dad drinks after his shift. Wade in up to your knees and your legs are gone, just your swim shorts sticking up. At the far edge, by the old piles, a white snag climbs out of the water with its branches spread out, exactly like a hand. The sun is smacking me right in the eyes. I squint and see something under the water by the snag, shining and winking. I nudge Vitka. He looks and says, "Wha'? There's nothin' there." So it's only winking at me.

"Bet you can't make it to the snag." Vitka is pulling his T-shirt off over his head and gets stuck in it. "Last one there's a girl."

"I am a girl," says Nika. "And I'm not stupid enough to swim out there."

Vitka comes out of his T-shirt red in the face and, so he can beat at least somebody, chucks a stick into the water. Bublik yelps and flies off the rock after it. At first it's funny. He snorts and paddles with all four legs and even grabs the stick in his teeth. Then the water turns him sideways. The stick floats off by itself, and Bublik gets pulled toward the snag, not fast, just smooth and steady, like a sled when somebody's pulling it by the rope. Only his ears are left above the water.

Nika screams so loud that crows fly up off the far bank.

> The old woman downstairs was right about that backwater. In almost every world where I went into the water that day, they never found me.

Vitka just stands there with his T-shirt in his fist and doesn't say anything, for the first time all summer. Nika has stopped screaming. She's looking at me, and the ears by the snag keep getting smaller.
