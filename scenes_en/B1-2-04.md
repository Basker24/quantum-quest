These days the boys from Rudnichny walk to Krasnaya Gorka straight across the Tom, in sandals, with jars, over the white stones where the river markers used to stand. On TV scientists throw up their hands at the backwater: all the water is gone, but this one is still there.

::if F_В123 == ОТКАЗ
The Glubokaya spoil heap is gone. We took it apart over seven years, until '40, cooled it and leveled it, and in its place there's a gentle slope with birches now, cold, the way earth should be. After that I spent another ten years closing mines, one after another, sealing shafts with concrete and letting water into the workings. I closed the last one in Kemerovo in '48. Dad opened them and I closed them, and there's some kind of order in that, though what kind I never figured out.
::end

::if F_В123 == ТАНЯ
The Glubokaya spoil heap has been under clay for twenty-eight years now, green, with birches that go yellow in circles. In winter the snow won't lie on it. I see it from Tanya's window every morning, and every morning I put my palm to the glass as if it were a stove. The glass is cold, but my palm still knows it's warm. Somebody after me will have to put it out.
::end

Under Krasnaya Gorka, Dad said, was Granddad. I never did open it. The door he was talking about is somewhere down there, under the riverbed, under my feet, and these last three summers, since the Tom ran dry, I haven't been drawn to it. I'm drawn to the backwater.

It's been pulling at me all year, and since the comet, more than ever. The comet hangs over the riverbed in the evenings, its tail lying exactly on the water. "It'll come back on the twenty-eighth of July, sixty-one," Galina Ivanovna said at graduation, and we cracked up: seventy-two was never. The whisper behind my ear, which since '09 I've heard before every disaster, has turned this year into a steady hum, like in the shift office when forty men are waiting for the shift.

Vitka calls me a little after nine. He's seventy-three; he says his knee is acting up again, and Yegor wants him to come live in Novosibirsk, and he won't go. About the Wager, the war of '55, where the generals with their purchased Second Chance decided dying wasn't frightening, and about the two men who never came back from it to his open pit, we haven't talked in a long time. He asks if I'm going out at first light tomorrow. "Pulling at you?" he says. I say it is.

::if F_ПАРТНЁР == ТАНЯ
Tanya is asleep in the living room on the couch, with the TV on, the way people sleep when they're retired. My lamp, number two hundred forty-six, sits on her shelf. She carried it out of the lamp room when they closed the Glubokaya, and all these years she's charged it once a month without knowing why.
::end

::if F_ПАРТНЁР != ТАНЯ
It's quiet at home. The fridge clicks, and on my shelf sits my miner's lamp, number two hundred forty-six. Tanya gave it to me when they closed the Glubokaya. I charge it once a month. I don't know why.
::end

Granddad's storm jacket hangs in the front hall. Dad brought it to my garage a week before his stroke, canvas, with a patch on the elbow, and said, "Must be Granddad's." I've never worn it since.

> I made a decent section chief. I still remember those twenty-three names in a column, in order, and where every one of them is now. What would happen to me tomorrow, I didn't know.

## После A
At three I get up, put on a striped sailor's undershirt and Granddad's storm jacket, and take the lamp off the shelf. The lamp shines yellow and steady, the way it did in the lamp room when Tanya put it in my hands. I turn the lock once.

The yard smells of dust and dry wormwood. The comet hangs over the roofs. I go down to the riverbed by the old fishermen's stairs; the steps end on white stones now, and I walk over the stones as if they were railroad ties, counting. I lose count at three hundred and start over.

## После B
I sit down on the edge of the couch and touch Tanya's shoulder. She opens her eyes at once, no sleep in them, like in the lamp room when somebody knocked on the window at four in the morning. She looks at me, at the storm jacket, at the clock.

"To the river," she says, and is already swinging her legs off the couch.

She gets up, takes the lamp off the shelf, checks the charge and hands it to me, and then takes it back. "I'll carry it myself," she says. "I issue it, I take it back." We walk to the riverbed together, slowly, her in front with the lamp, and the stones in its light are white as sugar.

## После C
Sonka answers on the third ring, though it's past eleven at night in Moscow and after three in the morning here. She always answers on the third ring.

"Andryush, what is it?" she says. "Did something happen?"

"Nothing," I say. "I'm going to the river. Tell Mom…" and I stumble, because Mom has been gone eight years. Sonka is silent on the phone. Then she says, "I'll tell her." And: "Go on already, you silly thing." That's what Mom used to say when I ran off to the Tom at seven. I put the phone in the pocket of the storm jacket and walk to the riverbed, with the lamp, by the old fishermen's stairs.
