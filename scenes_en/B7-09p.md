::if F_РАЗВЕДЧИК
Over two years I drive every one of their roads ahead of them. Varya's highway to Akademgorodok when she comes home for the holidays. Kolka's route to college, through the crosswalk by the Lapland mall. My wife's way to the dacha over the old bridge. I'm always a minute ahead, and on every road I die where I need to, wake up, and get the call in in time. On the kitchen wall hangs a map of the region, covered in my little crosses. My wife thinks I'm marking the potholes.
::end

::if !F_РАЗВЕДЧИК
Since that morning on the highway I've driven ahead of my family twice more. Once ahead of Kolka, when he started driving, and once ahead of my wife, when she went to see her sister in Belovo. Both times everybody got there, and both times my wife asked why I'd followed.
::end

In April Kolka is flying to Moscow for a programming olympiad, his first time on a plane. He bounces around the kitchen, knocks over a stool and says there'll be people from Yandex there.

::drift F_РАЗВЕДЧИК
Varya calls on Sundays, like before. Only now she calls me "Daddy" instead of "Dad" and laughs differently, short, through her nose. I ask my wife how long Varya's been talking like that. My wife looks at me like I'm sick: "Always, Andrei." Kolka writes with his left hand. I try to remember teaching him to hold a spoon and can't remember which hand.
::end

My wife sits across from me with her tea, watching me watch Kolka.

"You're going to go ahead of him again," she says. It isn't a question. "Why do you always go first, Andrei? I'm not jealous. I'm scared."

You can't drive ahead of a plane. But you can buy a ticket for a flight an hour earlier and walk his route in Moscow first. And wake up, if it comes to that.

## После B
In the evening, when everyone's asleep, I take the map off the wall and fold it in four, then in four again, until it won't fold anymore. I put it in the toolbox, under the drill. The crosses on it are where I lay dead, and I keep my hand on it for a long time before I close the box.

Kolka flies to Moscow in April. I don't see him off at the airport, because I'm afraid I won't be able to stand it and I'll buy a second ticket. He calls from Sheremetyevo: "Dad, landed, it's huge here." I sit by the phone for another hour after he hangs up.

## После C
I tell her everything: the highway, the moose by Taiga, the Niva in Yurga, the minute I always keep between us. The map on the wall and what the crosses mean. I talk and wait for her to cry or laugh.

She's quiet for a long time. Then she gets up, goes to the map and touches one cross, by the old bridge, on her own road to the dacha.

"Was that for me?" she asks.

"For you."

She takes the map down herself, carefully, pin by pin, and carries it into the bedroom. In the morning the map is in her closet, under her underwear. "Let it stay with me," she says. "And you don't drive anymore." I don't.
