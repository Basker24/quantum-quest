I go up and squat down beside him. He starts and looks at my bare feet.

"Under Krasnaya Gorka there's an old working," I say. "Flooded. Water from the backwater drains into it through the adit when the Tom rises in spring. Your Mitya is there. Not in another world, but here, a kilometer from you. He needs to be buried."

He's silent for a long time. Then he asks how I know. I say I've seen him, and I'm not lying: last night on the bank, among my own, he was looking down at his feet.

In September Lunyov brings divers from Tomsk. They dive into the adit for three days. On the fourth they bring up what's left of a nine-year-old boy, with a sandal on one foot. They bury him at the Kirov cemetery, next to Lunyov's mother. Lunyov puts up a light-blue railing and paints it himself.

Then he takes me to his dacha near Tomsk, as a watchman. We live there together: me, an old man, and him, forty, who has aged more this autumn than I did in my whole life. In the evenings he asks nothing. I tell him nothing.

In August '98, during the default, he doesn't sleep at the dacha for a week. He comes back with an envelope, puts it on the table between us and looks at me.

"You knew I would do this," he says.

"I knew."

"And you didn't stop me."

"You have a family, Vadim Petrovich. And six months without pay."

He cries, for the first time in front of me, quietly, into his palms. The world will go on the way it was going: toward Orpheus, the "second chance," the dry Tom. I did change one thing in it after all. Now, when Lunyov thinks about Mitya, he thinks about a light-blue railing, not about another world where Mitya is alive somewhere.

I die that same winter, at the dacha, at four-twelve in the morning. Lunyov buries me next to Mitya. On the marker he writes "T. S." and nothing more, because he knows nothing more.

! Ending "Mitya." You gave a father back his son, but couldn't change the world. My Life shows what other roads led to this morning.
