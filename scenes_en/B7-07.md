Gena Shubin is fifty-five, and they won't let him retire because there's nobody to do the work, so now he smokes right in the operator room and nobody says a word to him. In the evenings people talk quietly in their kitchens, and the TV talks loudly.

On Sunday Sonka comes over after a night shift and falls asleep on our couch in her jacket, right in front of Varya. She's twenty-four, in her second year working in the ER on Vesennyaya Street. When she wakes up, she finds me in the hallway with a hammer drill and watches me drill the wall for a new outlet. "You're the only brother I've got," she says. "Stop doing everything yourself. Call somebody for once." I say it's faster this way.

## После B
"The breaker, you idiot!" Vitka yells into the phone so loud Varya can hear. "Don't touch anything, I'm coming."

He gets there in forty minutes, with a tool bag and a bobble hat his daughter knitted him. He flips the breaker on the landing, shines a flashlight into the wall, and curses me, the developer, and every Chinese outlet manufacturer by name. In ten minutes the outlet is sitting in the wall like it grew there.

Afterward we drink tea in the kitchen. Vitka stirs his sugar for a long time and says, without looking at me:

"You're supposed to be smart, Andryukha. But all your life somebody's been pulling you out. First some old man out of the river, then me."

## После C
Before screwing in a light bulb, Dad always went out on the landing and unscrewed the fuses, even when Mom laughed at him. I go out on the landing, open the iron door and flip the breaker. The lights in the apartment go out, Kolka yells "Hey!" from the living room, and my wife laughs. I change the outlet by the light of my phone. Before I touch the wire, I test it with the back of my hand, the way Dad taught me, so that if anything happens my hand won't clench.

Then I call Dad. He coughs into the phone for a long time, a miner's cough, and finally says, "Good man. The breaker comes first." He doesn't say anything else, but I can hear he's pleased.

> That was one of the last times he praised me. Two years later he'd slip at a bus stop, and I wouldn't be there.

## После D
The electrician from the housing office comes a week later, smelling of last night and without any tools, and borrows my screwdriver. All week the hole in the wall is taped over with an X of electrical tape, and Kolka pushes a toy soldier into it, and I fish it out with tweezers.

The outlet in the living room is on the same circuit, though, so the TV doesn't work, and for a week we play Monopoly in the evenings. Varya beats everybody and demands to be paid in real money. My wife laughs, and I feel like a man who can't change an outlet in his own home, and I don't like that feeling.
