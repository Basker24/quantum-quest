I'm forty-nine, my twenty-second summer at School No. 41. I know how many steps it is from the staff room to the physics classroom and where on the embankment the ice breaks first in March. In summer I get up at five and walk along the Tom to Pritomsky and back. The boys on the piers know me by now and don't dive while I'm around; they wait until I've passed.

> Nobody asked me to. One day I just realized I didn't want divers bringing anyone else up.

It's true, I've never seen the sea. The water I know can be black, can be icy, and can be warm and close, with a whisper inside. It's never salty. At night I lie awake and listen to the Choir rustling on one note, steady, like a kettle just before it clicks off. Late July is the worst time for the Tom. Last year at Pritomsky they pulled out two in one week.

In the morning I stand in the kitchen holding a ticket.

## После A
On the train I sleep for two days straight. I haven't slept like that since I was nineteen, no dreams, no four-twelve, as if someone had pulled out a wire. Near Tuapse she wakes me and points out the window. Out there it's blue, all the way to the edge, and the edge doesn't end.

The sea is warm and smells of iodine and chebureki. I walk in up to my knees and stand like that for ten minutes. She doesn't hurry me. Then she takes my hand, and we go in up to the waist, then up to the chest, and it holds me up. Salt water holds you by itself; you don't have to talk it into it.

For two weeks the Choir is silent. On the Tom, as I later learn from the news, nobody drowned in those two weeks. The boys on the piers dived without me.

## После B
I put the ticket back on the oilcloth and sit down across from her.

I tell her about five in the morning, about the embankment, the piers, about how in March I walk the ice with my eyes and count children, the way Nika once counted. About how after every summer I cross off in my head the ones they didn't pull out in time, and how that list is longer than a class roster. For twenty-two years nobody knew about it.

She listens to the end. Then she picks up the tickets, goes to the window and looks at the river for a long time.

"We'll go in September," she says. "In September it's too cold to swim here. You said so yourself. Until then, walk your embankment. I'll walk it with you."

We walk it together, every morning, right up to September. And in September we go to the sea, and there, for the first time since I was seven, I swim on my own.

## После C
Novosibirsk greets me with a traffic jam on the Dimitrovsky Bridge. Sonka goes gray beautifully, like Mom, and at the party she seats me next to her instead of with her husband's relatives. Mom sits across from us, small, in her best dress, and all evening looks at the two of us as if she's counting.

"Two," she tells me in the kitchen, when everyone's already having tea. "Back then, in the hospital, I kept thinking: let there be two. So there'd be someone to live."

I don't ask what she means. I think I understand.

## После D
I return the ticket at the station ticket office myself, while she's at work. The cashier asks if I'm sure, and I say I'm sure.

On the twenty-ninth of July at five in the morning I walk along the embankment. Nobody at Pritomsky. Nobody on the thirtieth either. On the thirty-first, by the pier, a boy of about nine dives off a concrete slab and doesn't come up for a long time, and I'm already taking off my shoes when he shoots up to the surface and yells something filthy about the cold.
