For a year I've been taking Siberians around Pattaya. Crocodile farm in the morning, elephants in the afternoon, the Alcazar in the evening, where guys from Prokopyevsk spend half an hour arguing whether the women on stage are women or not, and then pose for photos with them for a hundred baht and hide the photos from their wives. I speak Russian slowly and loudly, as if to the deaf, and they believe me. Alla Borisovna says on the phone that my face really is honest, and in March she sends me to the islands with a group of nine for the "Full Moon for $199" excursion.

At night Haad Rin looks like a disco at a House of Culture, if the House of Culture stood in the sea. Sand, speakers the size of garages, strobe lights, glow paint on bare backs. Thai boys sell cocktails in plastic sandbox buckets: a bottle of local rum, Coke, Red Bull, ice and six straws. I've been holding my bucket for two hours. My group has scattered across the beach, and I count them by the glowing bracelets I handed out at the pier. Seven bracelets, sometimes eight.

Right by the water stands a wooden sign, handwritten: "Jellyfish! Vinegar at bar." The sign has half fallen over, and two Swedish girls are sitting on it.

::if F_Т03 == ВОДОБОЯЗНЬ
In a whole year I haven't once gone into the sea. I tell my group that guides aren't allowed, it's in the rules. There are no rules. Tonight I'm on my second bucket, and it seems to me it's time. Go in up to my chest, stand there, come out. Just once.
::end

::if F_Т03 != ВОДОБОЯЗНЬ
It's so hot my T-shirt sticks to my back, and the rum from the bucket warms me from the inside. The sea is right here, warm, black and smooth, not a wave. Girls in glowing swimsuits run into it shrieking and run back out.
::end

Tolik from Prokopyevsk, a tunneler from the Koksovaya mine, who's spent the whole evening telling me "we've seen worse than this underground," is standing at the water's edge in nothing but his shorts, waving his bucket at me.

"Andryukha!" he yells over the beat. "Let's go swimming! The water's like fresh milk!"

The rest of my people are all over the place. Two ladies from Belovo are tired and want to go back to the bungalow, and our bungalow is past the headland, over the rocks, along a path, twenty minutes in the dark. The songthaew truck won't come until six. The moon hangs over the sea, huge and white, and from it a path of light runs across the water right to my feet.

I set my bucket down on the sand and look at the water.

## После D
I yell "Get out!" at Tolik so loudly that the Swedish girls fall off the sign, and run to the bar. The bartender, a kid of about fifteen, understands at once, reaches under the counter and shoves a plastic bottle of vinegar at me. Clearly people ask him every night.

When I get back, Tolik is already on all fours at the water's edge, howling. Down his shin, from knee to ankle, run red welts, swollen, as if from a whip. He only got in up to his knees. I pour vinegar over his leg, the whole bottle, and don't touch it with a finger. Tolik swears in words I've never heard even in Kemerovo by the beer kiosk. Then he stops swearing and cries. That means it's letting go.

At six in the morning I take the ladies from Belovo to the bungalow in the songthaew, along the road around the headland. In the back Tolik sleeps on my shoulder, his leg bandaged. He doesn't thank me; he doesn't remember anything.

I remember everything, and I shake until lunchtime. Hands, shoulders, jaw. None of it happened to me, but my hands shake as if it had.
