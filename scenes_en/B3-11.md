It's a night train; there's no open sleeper car on it anymore, only compartments. The neighbors on the upper berths have been asleep since evening, two young people with that particular calm look on their faces; there are a lot of them now. Across from me sits a woman. She's about sixty, gray-haired, cropped short, glasses on a cord, and she isn't sleeping, like me. We get talking at Bolotnoye because of the tea. The attendant brought one glass for the two of us, and after that we don't stop until morning.

She's a doctor. A hematologist, in Novosibirsk, thirty-five years. Blood, she says, is a river: everything that's happened to you floats in it, you just have to know how to read it. I say rock is a river too, only slow. She laughs and says I'm the first geologist who hasn't bored her. I tell her about the bald peaks, about Miron, about the mammoth with reddish wool, about the mound that breathed. She tells me about her father, who drank, and her husband, who left, and her son, who's in Canada. She says little about her work. She used to work at a certain clinic, then she left. Why she left she doesn't say; she just turns the spoon from the glass holder in her fingers.

::if СВЯЗЬ_НИКА >= 30
The longer we talk, the more it seems to me that I know her: the voice, and the way she squints when she doesn't believe you. I can't remember where from.
::end

Outside it's getting light. Birches, swamp, fog over the swamp. The train stands in Yurga for two minutes, and the attendant calls down the corridor for smokers not to wander far. The woman looks out at the fog and says, somewhere into the glass:

"You know, as a kid I used to count. In summer, at the river, outside Kemerovo. A boy drowned, and I stood on the bank and counted how long he'd been gone. My mom used to tell me: count when you're scared, count out loud. I got to two hundred and fifty-two. They pulled him out. All my life since, when I'm scared, I count. To two hundred and fifty-two. No need to go further; after that I know it'll be all right."

She turns to me and smiles apologetically.

"Silly, isn't it? A woman of sixty."

The tea in my glass has gone cold. The spoon in the glass holder clinks softly against the glass at the rail joints. I'm holding the glass with both hands, and my hands, it turns out, are shaking.

> I was sixty, and for forty of those years I'd listened to rock. In all that time it never once occurred to me to listen to a person.

## После A
"It was me you were counting," I say. "Nika. It's Samokhin. Andrei. The Vodyanoy."

She looks at me for a long time, over her glasses. Then she takes her glasses off, and her face becomes different, very young and angry, like in '96 on the bank, when she counted and yelled at the grown-ups to hurry up.

"Samokhin," she says. "You idiot. Where have you been for forty years?"

"In the field."

"In the field," she repeats, and suddenly covers her face with her hands and laughs into her palms, or cries, I can't tell. The train starts moving. Up above, the young neighbor wakes up and looks at us, two gray-haired old people sitting across from each other holding hands over cold tea, and turns back to the wall. In Kemerovo she doesn't head for a taxi. She rides with me to Rudnichny, to Mom, and Mom opens the door, looks at the two of us and says, "Well, finally."

She looks out the window. I sit there not breathing. My seconds, on a label, in someone else's archive, and now my blood flows in generals' veins.

"I knew him as a kid," she says. "Summers, in my brother's yard. Then I lost track of him. But it's his blood, you see? They're selling his blood to generals. Maybe he's living somewhere and doesn't even know."

I say nothing. In Kemerovo she gets off first, waves to me from the platform and walks off into the fog, small, gray-haired, with a rolling suitcase. I never do tell her my name.

## После C
"I'll go have a smoke," I say, though I don't smoke.

I step out onto the platform in Yurga and stand by the car in the fog. The attendant calls that we're departing. I don't get back on. The train starts moving, slowly, and in the compartment window I see her. She looks at me through the glass, not understanding, and raises her hand as if to wave, and then lowers it.
