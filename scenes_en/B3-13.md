They summoned me by telegram. Nobody has sent telegrams for thirty years, but the commission apparently decided I wouldn't answer otherwise, and decided right. "To A. N. Samokhin. Request your presence as an expert on anomalous bodies of water in permafrost and non-permafrost zones. Kemerovo, Krasnaya Gorka. Urgent." I run my finger over the word "non-permafrost" and laugh. I know of exactly one anomalous body of water in a non-permafrost zone, and I became an expert on it at seven.

At the backwater below Krasnaya Gorka I'm met by floodlights, tents, drones, people in identical jackets. The sonar can't see the bottom. Three different people tell me this, each with a face as if it were my fault.

The chairman of the commission is young, about thirty, with that particular calm face, like everyone who's had the second chance. The Wager ended last year, but the faces stayed. He shows me the sonar images on his tablet: black, black, black, nothing.

"Andrei Nikolayevich," he says. "You worked with permafrost collapses for forty years. You've seen craters where sonar couldn't see the bottom either. What is this?"

I don't answer him. I take a rope out of my backpack.

The rope is my own, old, nylon, fifty meters, with a knot every meter. For forty years I measured pits, cracks, ice holes and craters with it. On the end is a lead sounding weight, covered in notches. I step onto the flat rock by the snag, white, like a hand, and lower the weight into the water.

A knot. A knot. Another knot. At the fifth the rope goes slack. The weight has touched bottom. Five meters, not quite. The chairman looks at the rope, then at his tablet, where it's black, and says nothing.

I pull up the weight. There's sand on it, yellow, fine, warm. I pour it into my palm, and it warms my palm the way sand on a beach warms at noon, when you walk on it barefoot and hop. I put a thermometer in the sand. Twenty-four degrees. The water up top is cold as a root cellar, but the sand from the bottom is summer sand. A freshwater mussel crawls out of the sand, alive, and slowly closes its shells.

It smells of the river and of July 1996.

The rock rings. The backwater answers. It answers on my note, the very same, lower and quieter, and the ringing doesn't stop; it hangs over the water, and the floodlights on the far bank start to flicker, and the drones land on the rocks on their own, one after another.

"It has no bottom because time is down there," I tell the chairman. "It's settled here in a layer, like in rock, only not a million years per centimeter but the other way around. Down there it's noon. 1996. Don't go in there until morning."

He doesn't believe me, but he writes it down. At midnight the commission leaves for the hotel on Sovetsky, leaving a floodlight by the backwater and two guards up top, by the parapet.

## После A
I climb up to the parapet and tell the guards the expert asks not to be disturbed until morning. They're glad; they're cold. One of them, very young, asks whether it's true it's noon down there. I say it's true. He nods and says his grandmother used to say the same about some river, and goes off to the car to warm up.

I go back down to the water and switch off the floodlight. What's left is the comet, the one our astronomy teacher promised us in eleventh grade, low over Krasnaya Gorka.

## После B
"Nika," I say into the phone. "Will you come down?"

"I thought you'd never ask," she says. "Forty years and twelve more. You're an idiot, Samokhin."

She comes down the rocks with her thermos, carefully, leaning on my arm, and sits on the flat rock by the snag. I show her the sand in my palm. She touches it with a finger and is silent for a long time.

"Warm," she says. "Like back then. I remember what the sand was like. I stood on it and counted."

## После C
"Sardaana," I say. "Stay. Measure it."

She gets up from her chair, slowly, like an old woman, and comes over. I pour warm sand into her palm. She looks at it, then at me, then takes out the tape measure, her mother's, with little flowers, and measures from the water's edge to the snag. Six meters twenty.

"You're going in there, aren't you," she says. "I know you. You're standing and listening."

"I'm going."

"Then go," she says. "I'll sit here. For thirty years I've been afraid for people who can run out. Let's see how it goes with you."
