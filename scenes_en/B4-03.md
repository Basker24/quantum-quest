::if F_Т09 != ДОЛГ_СВАРНОМУ
Vitka comes home from the army on the twentieth of June, in uniform with an aiguillette he braided himself out of parachute cord. His mother bawls by the building entrance, the neighbor women bawl on the bench, Vitka hugs everyone and smiles. He smiles with his mouth. His eyes stay as if he were still looking at something beyond the fence of his unit, far away, and hasn't come back from there yet.

In the evening we sit on the hood of his stepfather's Lada behind the garages on Shakhtyorov. Sibirskaya Korona beer, sunflower seeds, mosquitoes. I tell him about the scrap yard, about Shurup, about the scales. He listens and nods, and then asks, "So does the Welder still pay?"
::end

::if F_Т09 == ДОЛГ_СВАРНОМУ
Vitka and I worked off our debt to the Welder by May. The Welder shook our hands with his soft warm palm and said, "You're free, boys. If you need money, you know where I sit." We know: under the umbrella.

We stay free for a week. On the eighth day Vitka says freedom is when you've got money, and we don't, and we're sitting on the hood of his stepfather's Lada behind the garages on Shakhtyorov again. Sibirskaya Korona beer, sunflower seeds, mosquitoes.
::end

::if F_В402 == СТОРОЖ
At home nobody has spoken to me for five months except Sonka. Since Kuzmich, Dad looks through me like through a bus window. Mom sets a plate in front of me and leaves the kitchen. Sonka brings her drawings to my room and asks why Dad is upset with me. I say it's because of a chicken. She laughs.
::end

::if F_В402 != СТОРОЖ
Nobody at home knows about the warehouse by the market. I told Mom I'd been unloading railcars at night at the marshalling yard, and she rubbed my frostbitten fingers with goose fat she's kept in the fridge since '98.
::end

The Welder drives up to the garages himself, in a black Land Cruiser, and that's a bad sign. He only drives somewhere himself when he really needs something. He sits down on the hood next to us; the hood sags. On a Yava pack he's drawn a map: a stream, a forest, an X.

"Outside Kiselyovsk," says the Welder. "The seam comes up to the surface, twenty meters down, and the coal, boys, is like sunflower seeds. A bootleg pit. The state doesn't know, and if it does, it's been paid. Two below, one on the winch, and the Bychok to haul it. Cash the same evening. Three thousand a shift each."

Dad doesn't make three thousand a shift at the Glubokaya even in his best month.

"And the props?" asks Vitka.

"There are props," says the Welder. "Aspen. Forest is right there."

Dad knows about bootleg pits. He talks about them in the kitchen whenever Kuzbass runs another story about somebody getting buried near Prokopyevsk: "That's not a mine. That's a grave with a shovel."

> The Welder didn't lie: the coal there really was like sunflower seeds. He just didn't say what an aspen prop is worth when the seam comes down on top of it.

## После A
"Down below," I say. "The two of us."

Vitka nods and clinks his bottle against mine. The Welder slaps us on the knees, first Vitka, then me, and drives off, and we sit there until two in the morning counting money we don't have yet. Three thousand a shift, twenty shifts a month, in a year that's a Lada Nine. Vitka wants a Nine. I want Mom to stop sitting over the bills.

In the morning we ride out to Kiselyovsk in the Bychok with Shurup. The pit is in a birch grove by a stream, covered with slate sheets and branches. A winch, a bucket, a rope ladder with rungs made of rebar. Down below it smells of damp and root cellar. Vitka climbs down first and shouts from the bottom, "Andryukh! It's like being in a tank down here!"

## После B
"I'll take the Bychok," I say. "I've got a license."

I've had the license six months, and Shurup cracks up. The Welder doesn't. He looks at me the way he looked at the cart of saucepans on the scales, and nods.

All summer I haul coal in sacks from the pit in the birch grove to the coal yard by the railway. Fifty-kilo sacks, the Bychok knee-deep in mud, Shurup asleep in the cab while I load. Vitka is down in the pit with one of the Kiselyovsk guys. Once a week he climbs up gray as a sack and says, "Andryukh, you've sure got it cushy." He says it without malice, but without laughing either.

## После C
Dad is sitting in the garage going over a carburetor from a Ural motorcycle. I tell him everything: the Welder, the bootleg pit, three thousand. He listens and doesn't raise his head.

"Aspen?" he asks at last.

"Aspen."

Dad puts the carburetor on the workbench and wipes his hands with a rag, for a long time, every finger.

"Aspen cracks when it's already too late," he says. "Larch cracks sooner. Under a crack, put larch. And listen. The roof, first it shoots, then it rustles, then it comes down. If it shoots, leave right away. Don't wait for it to rustle."

He says nothing more. In the morning I find his old hard hat in my backpack, with the chipped brim, from the Glubokaya, and under it his knife.
