Sardaana left in the autumn of '31, two months after the mound. She didn't slam the door. She packed her things into two backpacks, her things and her mother's tape measure, sat down in the kitchen in Yakutsk and said:

::if F_В309 == ЗВОН || F_В309 == ПОПЕРЁК
"You know ahead of time, Andrei. You stand there and you know. For sixteen years I've lived with a man who knows when something's going to collapse and is never afraid. I can't do it. Next to you I've started to be afraid for myself, do you understand? Not for you."
::end

::if F_В309 == ЗАМЕР || F_В309 == УВЁЛ
"You don't die, Andrei. You go where people die, and you come back out. Every time. For sixteen years I've been waiting for the time you don't come out, and I'm tired of waiting. I'm forty-three. I want to be afraid for someone who can run out."
::end

Three years later she married a vet from Nyurba. She has two boys. Once a year, on my birthday, she sends me a photo of some mammoth tooth with the caption: "Here. This one lay around a long time too." I answer: "Beautiful." That's all we write to each other.

Dad died in '29, in spring, of his lungs. I was on Taimyr; the radiogram arrived nine days after the funeral. In the autumn Sonka sent me a parcel in Yakutsk: the shoebox from Mom's boots, tied with a ribbon. In it were all my letters, from twenty-two years, every one. The envelopes had been opened neatly, with a knife. In the margins, in pencil, in Dad's hand, here and there "alive" was underlined, here and there "fine," and on one, from '08, from the golets, he'd written in the margin: "Storm. Felt it."

This is my twelfth year at the station. An island in the Laptev Sea, seventy-six degrees north, permafrost monitoring: boreholes, thermometers, gas sensors. Three of us in summer, and me alone in winter. The nearest person is two hundred kilometers away. In winter it's so quiet here you can hear the ice cracking by the shore and the blood knocking in my head.

In the silence I learned to listen long ago. The hammer from the golets lies on my desk, on a flat stone, as a paperweight. Every night at 04:12 I wake up, I don't know why, go to the desk and tap the stone with the hammer. It rings, and afterward the ringing in my head doesn't end, and there are voices in it, mine; I recognize them the way you recognize your own handwriting. The one who stayed on the golets under the storm.

::if seen_life(✝В3-05.A)
The one who sat down on the creek ice and took off his mitten because he was hot.
::end

::if seen_life(✝В3-08.A)
The one who lay down on Vitka in the burning cockpit.
::end

There are more and more of them. I don't know who they are or why I hear them. Nobody ever explained it to me. I decided long ago myself that it's all me, only many times over, and that thought makes it easier.

A month ago the radio talked about a clinic in Novosibirsk. They do a "second chance" there: they inject something into your blood, and afterward a person doesn't die when he's supposed to. It costs as much as an airplane. I laughed and switched the radio off.

Tonight I tap the stone, and there are strangers in the ringing. Not many, five or six, and their voices aren't mine, not at all, but they sound in my pitch, on my note, as if someone had taken my hammer and were tapping someone else's stone on the other side of the earth. One speaks English. One is crying. One is laughing, loudly, like a drunk, and repeating, "I didn't die. I didn't die. I didn't die again."

The back of my head is colder than it has ever been in my life.

## После A
The station's watch log has a column for "Phenomena." Usually I write "aurora, faint" or "ice ridging off the cape." Now I write: "04:12. Six strangers. One in English, about a yacht. One crying. The one who laughs isn't laughing today."

By spring there are twenty strangers. I tell them apart by their voices and give them nicknames, the way I named horses in Mundybash: Yachtsman, Crybaby, Drunk. One day the Drunk stops coming. A week later he comes again and again shouts that he didn't die. I don't know who they are. I only know that they're out there somewhere, and that something inside them sounds the way it does in me, and before, it didn't.

In summer my relief, a young guy from Tiksi, reads the log and looks at me for a long time. Then he says, "Andrei Nikolayevich, you could use a vacation." I say I took a vacation in '29, went to Dad's grave, and that was enough for me. He doesn't read the log again.

## После B
The flight comes in April. I hand the station over to my relief and fly to Tiksi, then to Yakutsk, then to Novosibirsk. In Novosibirsk I walk the streets for three days, just walk, in the crowd, along Krasny Avenue, and my head is noisy with cars and people, and in that noise the voices are almost inaudible.

Outside one clinic there's a line of expensive cars. A lyre is painted on the glass door, with the words "Second Chance." A gray-haired man in a good coat comes out the door and smiles the way people smile who've just been told they won't die. The back of my head goes cold. I cross to the other side of the street.

I put the hammer away in my backpack, at the very bottom, and don't take it out until summer.

## После C
I write to Vitka for three nights. About the mountain, the storm, what happened on the golets, the cockpit. About what I hear. About the strangers. I write him what I've never told anyone, not Sardaana, not Dad, and when I'm done I have twelve pages from the watch log. I tear them out and give them to the pilots when the supply flight comes in March.

::end

I read the letter on a rock by the shore where the ice cracks. Then I put it in the shoebox, with Dad's.
