The sea didn't come to Bangkok all at once. First a centimeter a year, then three, and by '45 half of Samut Prakan stands behind dikes, concrete ones, twice a man's height. The dikes were built by the Dutch, and the Dutch now live behind them, in villas along the canals, because they like water. This is my twelfth year in the marine rescue service, first as a volunteer, then for pay. So water, for me, is a job.

::if F_МАЛИ
Mali is twenty-six, a nurse at a hospital on Sukhumvit, and about my work she says, "Papa's off swimming again." Tonight she's on shift, and every half hour she texts me one word in Russian: "alive?"
::end

At two in the morning, in a spring tide, when the moon and the sun pull the water the same way, the dike at Bang Pu breaks. The water pours into the breach as into a mine shaft, roaring, and twenty minutes later the neighborhood is chest-deep. Our boat is orange, with a motor and a searchlight. The searchlight picks out of the darkness now a car roof, now a palm tree, now a dog on a fence.

To the right, on the canal, is a villa. Its roof is lit up like an aquarium, and on it stands an old man in a white bathrobe, holding a glass. That's Henk, a Dutchman, eighty, a retired engineer who once designed these very dikes. I shout through the megaphone for him to come down to the boat.

"No need!" shouts Henk, and raises his glass. "I have second chance! Novosibirsk! I don't die, Russian!"

I've heard of the "second chance." They do it at a clinic in Novosibirsk, for people with a great deal of money. They inject something into the blood, and afterward a person doesn't die when he's supposed to. I ran around Novosibirsk as a courier, delivering envelopes. Now they make immortality there for Dutchmen.

To the left, across the street, is a two-story school. On the roof are twenty children in orange vests and two teachers, shining flashlights. The water has reached their second floor, and it's still coming.

::if ТЕЛ >= 45
It's about thirty meters to the villa, across the current. I swim a kilometer along the shore every morning, and at fifty-six my body still obeys me. I can see the current by the debris: it's pulling toward the breach, but by the villa wall it's quieter.
::end

::if ДАР >= 60
The whispering in my head is loud tonight, as if all the neighbors behind the wall had gathered at once. A chill pulls at the back of my head and shows me the quiet water along the wall, where you can go, and the whirlpool by the breach, where you can't.
::end

> Henk lived to ninety-seven. He died in his own bed, like any ordinary Dutchman, of the kind of thing an injection can't buy you out of.

## После A
I jump and swim, keeping close to the wall, where it's quieter. The current pulls toward the breach but can't quite reach me. I climb onto the villa's roof by the drainpipe, and Henk looks at me with interest, like at a fish in an aquarium.

"You are crazy," he says. "I told you."

I don't argue. I put a vest on him right over his bathrobe, and he lets himself be dressed like a child. The guys bring the boat up, we lower him on a rope, and the whole way down he holds his glass and doesn't spill a drop. An hour later the school is taken off its roof by another boat, from Samut Sakhon. All twenty children. On the shore, on dry ground, Henk says "thank you" to me, then, after thinking, adds, "No need, but thank you."

In the morning, when the water starts to go down, we pass the villa. Henk is sitting on the roof in a deck chair, in his wet bathrobe, waving his glass at us. Alive and pleased. "Told you!" he shouts. Seven people in the neighborhood died that night. Henk's bathrobe even dried.
