The sink in the corner is enamel, with a chipped edge. I turn the tap, and the water comes warm, rusty, then colder. I lean down, put my mouth under it and drink.

The water gets colder. It gets colder than water from a pipe ever is in Kemerovo in July, and it smells of river slime. I want to straighten up, and my head won't rise. There are no hands on the back of my head; the water itself is holding my head, heavy, from all sides. My ears hum the way they hum when you dive to the very bottom of the backwater.

I swallow again and realize I'm not swallowing with my mouth. The water is coming into me by itself, through my nose, into my throat, my chest, and my chest gets tight and heavy, as if wet sand had been poured into it. My feet slip on the tiles. The sink is right here under my palms, solid, and I'm drowning in it, the way that little one drowned at the snag, only nobody is pulling me up.

The last thing is the smell of Mom. Her hair, coal dust, and she says to me from somewhere above, "Andryusha, how could you."

## Пробуждение
…and I'm lying on the bed again, soaked in sweat, and in the dark the little one is whimpering, "Thirsty."

I'm so thirsty my tongue sticks to the roof of my mouth. I look at the sink in the corner. The tap is dripping, rarely, one drop every few seconds, and every time I flinch.

I drowned in a sink. I remember sand on my teeth from our Tom, sweetish and fine, and I remember that the water wouldn't let go of my head, though no one was holding it. The little one asked for a drink, and I gave him a drink of myself.
