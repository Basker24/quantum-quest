Dad is dying of what miners die of when they're lucky enough not to die in the mine. The doctors call it anthracosis. Dad calls it "the dust" and jokes that in thirty years he's breathed himself a second coal seam.

In the regional pulmonology ward he lies by a window that looks out on a spoil heap, and he stares at it all day long. He's not allowed to smoke. Sometimes he takes a cigarette from the nightstand, doesn't light it, and just holds it between his fingers, the way he did that night in the kitchen, after the explosion.

I know nothing can be done, and I know why. You can rewind away from a collapse, from an outburst too; there death has a minute, a fork. Dust has no such minute. It built up over thirty years, breath by breath, and not one of those breaths can be played again. Granddad would probably have had some scientific name for it. I call it the Wall.

On Wednesday Mom brings the envelope from the china cabinet. That one, "For Kolya," with the wax seal, which lay behind the crystal for twenty-three years. Dad takes it in his hands, looks at it for a long time and holds it out to me.

"You read it, Andryukha. My eyes aren't what they were. And no voice left."

I break the wax. It's old and crumbles; red crumbs fall onto Dad's blanket, and he flicks them off with a finger, one at a time. Inside is a single sheet, Granddad's handwriting, blue ink, dated at the bottom, January '86.

"Kolya. If you're reading this, it means I didn't come back. You're lucky, son, and it isn't luck."

Dad knocks twice on the nightstand with his knuckles, very quietly. I read on.

"I passed it on to you without wanting to, and forgive me for that. Don't go below two hundred."

At the words "below two hundred" Dad closes his eyes. There's one line left, and I read it to myself, because my lips won't obey me: "And if you have a son, keep him away from the water."

## После A
I read the letter to the end, read it again, slowly, and then tell him things about school, about Seventh Grade B, about Shmelyov Junior. Dad doesn't open his eyes, but in some places he smiles, and that means he can hear.

He dies four days later, at night, at 04:12. They call me, but I'm not asleep anyway.

## После B
I tell him everything. He listens with his eyes closed. I'm already thinking he's fallen asleep when he says hoarsely through the mask:

"Three times. In '05. I told you."

"You did, Dad."

"Well then." He breathes for a long while. "So I wasn't a fool."

He dies four days later, at night, at 04:12.

## После C
I don't say anything. I hold his hand, dry and light as newspaper. In the evening a nurse comes in, checks the drip and goes out without a word. We sit like that until it gets dark outside and the spoil heap disappears.

He dies four days later, at night, at 04:12.

## После D
I take out the watch and put it in his palm. He brings it up to his eyes, squints at the little star, then turns it over and reads the engraving with his lips, without a sound.

"Dad's," he says. "He never wore it to work; in the lab you had to take metal off. Where'd you get it?"

"Long story, Dad."

"Then don't tell it."

He holds the watch in his fist until evening. He dies four days later, at night, at 04:12, and afterward the nurse gives the watch back to me.
