In '47 they advertise the "second chance" at bus stops. On Sovetsky there's a screen: a gray-haired smiling man in a good coat and the words "Orpheus. Second Chance. For those who have something to lose." The procedure costs as much as a three-room apartment in the center. There are clinics in Moscow, Dubai, and, recently, Novosibirsk.

What they're selling, the TV explains vaguely: "controlled cardiac arrest under cold," "awakening of the neural reserve." What that means, I know better than anyone in this city. A person with a "second chance" can go back one minute, once, one step, like I did at nineteen, in the elevator.

That same week, for the first time, strangers' voices sound in the Choir. Their voices are strangers', but the pitch is mine, the way a stranger on a bus whistles a song you thought was yours. There are many of them, all far away, in Moscow, in Dubai, in Novosibirsk.

On Friday a polite young man from Siberian Resonance comes to the school. That's the Russian arm of Orpheus now, he explains, as if I didn't know. Shadrin is a hundred years old, he says; Shadrin remembers me and sends his regards. The foundation needs a consultant, and the foundation is prepared to pay very well.

## После A
I write down everything I know and send it to three newsrooms. Two don't answer. The third, in Novosibirsk, runs the piece on its website, and six hours later it disappears along with the website.

A week later I'm called in to the principal's office and politely invited to "take a rest until the end of the term." I go on sick leave, and for the first time in thirty years I have nothing to do in the mornings.

## После B
I sign the contract at the Orpheus office in Novosibirsk, on the forty-second floor. They issue me a pass and look at me like a museum exhibit that has suddenly started talking. The work is simple: they ask me what the "patients" hear, and I explain.

The money arrives on my card every month, steady and generous. At night there are more and more strangers' voices in the Choir.
