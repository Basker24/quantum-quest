"Well, Andryukha, you're something," he said then. "There's, you know, water there. You watch yourself."

We didn't talk about anything else. But last autumn a parcel came to Koh Tao, after three months on the way, in a Rollton noodle box sealed with blue electrical tape. In it was a sailor's striped shirt, new, with the tag still on, and a canvas storm jacket, old, with a patch on the elbow. The note in the box was in Dad's handwriting, on the back of an electricity bill: "Granddad's, I think. Your gran kept it in the trunk. Wear it. Dad." Nobody wears a canvas storm jacket in thirty-degree heat. I hung it in the wardrobe, and it hangs there, smelling of our balcony.

It's April 2020 now, and the world is closed. The island is empty, the boats are tied up, the ferries aren't running, there are no planes. Dad caught covid at the start of the month, and the luck that brought him out from underground in '05 ran out on the ninth day, on a ventilator at the Regional hospital. They're burying him today, in Kemerovo, at Kedrovka, at nine in the morning. It's nine here too; we're in the same time zone as Kemerovo. Sonka is holding her phone and showing me the funeral by video call.

::if F_ПАРТНЁР == ПЛОЙ
Ploy sits beside me on the veranda steps with Mali in her arms. Mali is a year and three months old; she's asleep, and in her sleep she holds Ploy by the ear. Mali knows one word of Russian, "baba," and says it when she sees my mom on the phone.

On the screen there's snow, April snow, wet. A few people in masks. Mom in a black headscarf, small; I don't recognize her right away. Sonka brings the phone up to herself, and I see her eyes over the mask, wet and angry.

"You didn't even come," she says.

I say the borders are closed. She says she knows. We both know there were twelve years before the borders.

## После A
I watch to the end. They lower the coffin on straps, men in masks throw earth with shovels, and the earth knocks on the lid so loudly the phone crackles. Mom throws a handful and stands there. Sonka holds the phone steady, never once lowers it, though her hands must be frozen.

Then the screen goes dark. Sonka writes: "That's it." I sit on the veranda until noon, and it smells of the sea and someone else's flowers. Then I go to the wardrobe and stand in front of the storm jacket for a long time, and I still don't put it on.

## После B
I switch off the screen in the middle of Sonka's "you." I take my mask and fins and go to the sea, at nine in the morning, alone, along the empty beach. I dive without a tank, on a breath-hold, to fifteen meters, to twenty.

Underwater you can't hear anything but the whispering. Today the whispering is louder than usual, many voices at once, and I hang at twenty meters until my chest starts to burn. The voices seem to be going over something, the way old women at a cemetery go over names. When I surface, the sun is already up, and there are four missed calls from Sonka on my phone. I don't call back.

## После C
"Sonya," I say. "Give the phone to Mom."

Sonka is silent, then walks through the snow with the phone. On the screen, Mom's headscarf, then Mom's face, very close, covered in wrinkles I never knew.

"Andryusha?" says Mom. "Where are you?"

"By the sea, Mom."

Mom looks into the screen for a long time, as into a window. "Is it warm there?" she asks. I say it's warm. "Well, that's good," says Mom. "Just stay away from the water." Sonka takes the phone from her, and I see her turn away so Mom won't see her face.
