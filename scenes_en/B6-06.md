The hermitage is three log huts, a chapel and a bathhouse on the high bank of the Mras-Su, seventy kilometers from the last village. In summer you get here by boat, in winter over the ice. No electricity, no signal; there's a stove, a woodpile and Father Serafim.

He's seventy-five. He's small, dry, with a beard to his waist, and he walks faster than I do. When I arrived, he heard me out on the porch without interrupting, for about forty minutes. Then he said, "Demons don't talk like that. Demons lie more smoothly. Yours get muddled and argue. So they're people." And let me stay.

Serafim has a tuning fork. A two-pronged steel fork, dark from hands, with a little cross scratched on the stem. Before matins he strikes it on his knee, presses the stem to his ear and then draws out the first note, thinly, an old man's voice. In his youth Serafim sang bass in the miners' choir at the Palace of Culture in Rudnichny, before he became a monk.

That first winter he let me hold the tuning fork. I struck it, pressed the stem to my temple, and the Choir in my head lined up on a single note, like a class at assembly. All the voices at once, and each one separately, clearly, as if they were standing behind me.

"Our choirmaster used to do that," said Serafim. "Tikhon Savelyich. He used to say everyone has a tuning fork in his head, only in most people it doesn't ring. Strike it, and it'll sound. Samokhin, he was. No relation of yours, by any chance?"

I said he was my grandfather. Serafim looked at me for a long time, then crossed himself and never asked about it again.

Now it's the second week of Great Lent. For nine days I've eaten nothing, only water. The rule doesn't require it; Serafim scolds me. Only with hunger the Choir becomes quieter and clearer, and on the ninth day I can tell the voices apart the way I couldn't in two years in Altai. The one under water has said a whole sentence to me for the first time. Tonight I want to stand in the chapel until 04:12. It's thirty below in there.

Serafim stands in the chapel doorway with a heel of rye bread wrapped in a clean cloth.

"Eat," he says. "God's no fool; you won't trick him by going hungry."

## После A
I don't take the bread. Serafim puts it on the windowsill, next to the tuning fork, and leaves, slamming the door.

I spend the night on my knees on the boards. By midnight my knees stop hurting; by three they start to burn. At 04:12, when I can no longer feel my legs or hands, the whole Choir suddenly goes silent, all at once, and in the silence the one under water says to me clearly, slowly, as if dictating, "Down there someone held my hand too. Only he didn't make it in time." I don't know what it means. I write it with my finger in the frost on the windowpane.

In the morning Serafim silently sets a bowl of oatmeal in front of me. On the sixth spoonful I cry, and he pretends not to see.

## После B
I take the heel of bread in both hands. I eat it in front of him, crumb by crumb, and my mouth fills with saliva until my jaw hurts. Serafim stands in the doorway watching me, the way Mom watches Sonka when she has a fever.

"There now," he says. "Look at him, a saint."

I sleep that night in the hut, by the stove, twelve hours, no dreams. In the morning the Choir is loud and murky, like water in a spring flood. I'm angry at myself until lunch, and after lunch I chop wood and stop being angry.

## После C
I break the heel. Half I put in Serafim's palm, and hold his fingers until he closes them himself.

"You haven't eaten either, Father."

He looks at the bread, then at me, and suddenly laughs, quietly, into his beard. We eat on the porch, in the frost, under the stars. At night he prays, and I sit beside him and listen, and for the first time the Choir sounds calm in me, all the voices together.

In the morning he puts the tuning fork in my hand.

"Take it. You need it more. I've done my singing."

## После D
"I'll pray in the hut, Father. By the stove."

Serafim raises his eyebrows in surprise, but nods. I was in the chapel, all right, just not this time. My knees remember the boards they froze to, remember tearing free of them with the skin. I remember how Serafim found me there in the morning and for a long time couldn't close my eyes.

I spend the night by the stove, feeding it logs and listening. The bread lies beside me on a stool; I never take it. Toward morning Serafim sits down next to me, puts a hand on my shoulder and is silent until matins.
