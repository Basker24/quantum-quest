On Miners' Day Dad comes to see me alone. Mom is sitting in the car by the gate. She came last week and cried through the whole hour, and Anna Markovna said that was enough for now. Sonka sent a drawing: me with a fishing rod and a fish bigger than me. The fish is smiling.

Dad is in his work jacket, though it's his day off and a holiday. He's brought cucumbers from the dacha in a grocery bag and a copy of Kuzbass. He sits down on the bench, puts the bag between us and looks at the poplars for a long time, the way he looks at the sky when rain is coming.

Since that night I don't answer any of them anymore. When they start, I go to Semyonych and ask him to tell me about the tram. Semyonych was discharged last week. Now I retell his tram to myself, in a whisper, and it almost helps.

"Your mother says you talk to them," says Dad without turning. "To the voices."

"Not anymore."

He nods. Then he looks around at the gate, at the car, at the orderly by the steps, and leans so close to me that I smell Prima cigarettes and bathhouse.

"Remember what I told you in the kitchen? What I felt down there. Well, I had it too. At the longwall. Three times. Not voices, a cold right here." He touches the back of his head. "I never told anyone. Not your mother, not the guys. I thought if I said it, they'd put me in here with you, in the next bed."

He straightens up and unfolds Kuzbass as if he's reading. He holds it tight, with both hands, and the paper trembles anyway.

Before I'm discharged, Anna Markovna calls me into her office. Her folder already holds a whole stack of three-column sheets. She writes out a prescription, in big letters, so the pharmacy can read it.

## После A
I take the pills every evening, in front of Mom, in the kitchen, washing them down with fruit compote. Mom watches me swallow, and only then sits down to watch her series.

It gets quiet within a week. The voices don't go anywhere; they're like the neighbors' radio through the wall: you can tell it's playing, but not what. I sleep ten hours a night, eat for two and get fat, and Sonka calls me "Kolobok," after the round bun in the fairy tale. In the evenings Dad smokes on the balcony and sometimes calls me out to smoke with him, though I don't smoke. We stand there in silence. It's good being silent with him.

## После B
I take the prescription to the pharmacy on Lenin Avenue and buy everything on it. At home I pour the pills into the toilet and flush twice so they won't float back up.

Three days later Mom finds the empty blister packs in the trash. She says nothing, just sits down on a stool holding them. Then she starts to cry, quietly, without a sound, and cries for so long that I go out into the street.

At night I don't sleep. I sit in the kitchen and listen without answering. It's harder than I thought. In a month I learn to recognize the one under water by a single breath. In two I'm all gray, my hands shake, and people move away from me on the bus. Anna Markovna, whom I never went back to, leaves Mom her home phone number.

## После C
"Dad. How did you live with it?"

Dad doesn't answer for a long time. Then he folds the newspaper in four, then again, until it's thick as a brick.

"In silence," he says. "Cold at the back of your head, and you turn around. The guys think it's luck. In '89 we went to the junction, and halfway there the back of my head felt like an ice hole. I turned the crew around. Twenty minutes later the roof came down there, forty meters of it. After that they called me Lucky. And I sat in the kitchen all night thinking I'd lost my mind."

He looks at me for the first time in the whole conversation.

"You haven't lost it," he says. "If you have, then so have I. And I've been at the mine twenty years and never yet gotten a single man killed."

Before he leaves, he leaves me the cucumbers and Kuzbass. On the last page, where the crossword is, he's written in pencil the phone number of his crew boss, "just in case." I never did figure out in case of what.
