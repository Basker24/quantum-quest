I stand on the bank watching the boy sink toward the snag in the sunlit water. The girl's voice above counts to two hundred and fifty-two, falters and goes silent. No one dives. The fishermen in the fog stand motionless, and then there are fewer of them, and fewer still, until I'm the only one left by the water.

At four-sixteen the water in the backwater turns black again.

I climb up into the city at dawn. The city is almost the same. Only the Tom behind me is full, wide and gray, as if it had never gone anywhere, and at the bus stop on Sovetsky, instead of an advertising screen, there's a paper bus timetable that no one reads.

In the cemetery below Krasnaya Gorka I find a small headstone with an enamel photograph. A boy with his panama hat askew grins from ear to ear, two front teeth missing. "Samokhin, Andrei Nikolayevich, 1989–1996."

By the headstone a woman of about sixty is changing the flowers. She raises her eyes to me, gray, Mom's.

"Who are you here for?" she asks.

"Your brother."

She isn't surprised.

"He drowned before I was born," she says. "Mom had me afterward. Just in case, she used to say. So there'd be someone to live."

I look at Sonka, whom in this world I never had. She lives for two, and judging by the flowers, she's managing.

No one here thinks they might not die. They fear death the old way: in winter they put on a hat, and they don't go out on the March ice.

The Choir is silent, and for the first time since I was seven my head is quiet.

The sun rises over the Tom. I sit down on the bench by the little headstone, next to a sister who doesn't know me, and watch the water grow light.
