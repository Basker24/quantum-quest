Dad has been gone since last summer. In December of '24 he slipped at the bus stop by Krasnaya Gorka, on flat ground, broke his hip, spent six months in bed at home, and in June pneumonia took him. I wasn't with him at the bus stop or that night. His death had no minute I could go back to, and there was nothing for me to die for. Now Mom calls me every evening at nine and asks if I've eaten.

At the end of March we're at the dacha in Yagunovsky, shoveling the last snow off the roof. The snow is heavy and wet, the shovel sticks in it.

At the end of the street there's a pond, round, an old flooded pit that all winter is the boys' skating rink. Now the ice on it is gray and spongy, and there's already water along the edges. Kolka is ten, and you can't drag him away from that pond. An hour ago I told him not to go on the ice. He said, "I know, I know."

Now I hear the boys yelling over the fence. Kolka runs out to the middle after a puck, and the ice under him sags with a soft wet sound. Kolka is gone. Then his head comes up over the water, no hat, he grabs the edge, and the edge breaks off under his hands.

It's thirty meters to the hole.

By the fence lies a long board from an old greenhouse. Kolka is holding on to the edge with one hand and slapping the water with the other, and every time he slaps, another hand's width of the edge breaks off.

## После A
I run across the ice, and the ice cracks under my feet, but I know what to do with it. Don't stop, don't jump, and just before the hole drop flat and slide on your belly. I go down at the edge, grab Kolka by the hood and pull. He's heavy with water, like a sack of cement, and he clings to me so hard he scratches my neck.

A minute later we're both lying on the bank, soaked through. Kolka is shaking, and the first thing he says is, "Dad, the puck's still out there." They fire up the banya right there in the middle of the day, and I check the damper three times.

## После C
Under the middle of the pond there's a spring, and the ice there is as thin as newspaper. Now I know that with my whole body. So I don't run across the pond; I run along the bank to the reeds, where the ice is thicker and stitched through with stalks, and from there, the last three meters, I slide on my belly to the hole with the board.

I pull Kolka out by the hand on the first try. He doesn't even have time to swallow much. The boys on the far bank look at me as if I knew in advance, and they're right about that.

## После D
I remember the Tom at the end of March, a hole in the ice, a backpack on the edge, and the current taking me by the legs, calmly, like it owned the place. In this life I've never drowned in the Tom as a grown man. Whose memory that is, I'm not going to sort out. I remember the main thing: ice gets eaten from below by water that moves. And in this pond the water only moves in the middle, where the spring comes up in summer.

So I run in an arc along the bank, where the ice is gray but thick, and only by the hole itself do I drop on my belly and slide toward it with the shovel, handle first. Kolka grabs the handle on the second try, and I yank him out onto the ice like a fish.

On the bank I wrap him in my jacket and rub his ears, and he fights me off. Only in the banya, once he's warmed up and started arguing again, does he ask why I ran the long way around. "Because I've already run straight," I say, and of course he doesn't understand a thing.
