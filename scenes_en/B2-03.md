After the elevator I sleep with the light on for a month. Den thinks I've got a broken heart and slips me Splean CDs. I don't tell anyone anything. I can't exactly say I died in an elevator, only not all the way.

For Old New Year, the one by the old calendar on the night of the thirteenth of January, Lyokha from our year invites everybody to his place in Stepanovka. His parents have gone to Kolpashevo; for eight people there are two bottles of vodka and a pack of dumplings that nobody ever gets around to boiling. His dad's felt boots with rubber galoshes stand in the front hall, and all evening Lyokha kicks them into the corner to get them out of the way. The vodka runs out at two in the morning; the trolleybuses stopped at eleven.

Outside it's forty-two below. The air rings, the streetlights stand in rust-colored fog, and the snow doesn't creak underfoot, it shrieks. It's six kilometers to the dorm on Lenin Avenue if you go through Lagerny Garden, the park along the steep bank above the Tom. For a taxi I have exactly two hundred rubles, and it's nine days till my stipend.

::if F_В201 == ЖЕЛЕЗО
I'm wearing white Adidas with three stripes, bought in the fall with freight-car money. I threw out my old winter boots back then. I like myself in the new sneakers, and so far I haven't been cold in them.
::end

The cold starts at my ankles, like that time in the elevator, and creeps up. I stamp my feet outside the entrance and catch myself looking down to see if anything white is spreading there.

> Forty-two below happens in Tomsk once in ten years. I got it the night the vodka ran out at two.

::if ДАР >= 20
The back of my neck is colder than my cheeks in the frost. And I already don't want to look at the bench by the monument in Lagerny Garden, though you can't even see it from here.
::end

Lena from third year comes out with me, the last of us. She lives right here, in the next entrance, but she doesn't go home; she stands picking at the frost on the railing with her mitten.

"Freezing," she says to the railing. "I wouldn't go anywhere in this."

And she keeps picking, though the railing there is down to bare iron.

## После A
I walk fast and don't stop once. I go past the bench by the monument without looking at it, though my legs are begging to sit. I get to the dorm at half past four, with white cheeks and, it seems to me, no toes. The toes come back later, and that hurts more than the frost.

## После B
The driver takes all two hundred rubles and still grumbles that in frost like this nobody drives for less than three hundred. For nine days till my stipend I eat instant noodles and borrow stewed meat from Tagir. Tagir gives it and says nothing, and that's more shameful.

## После C
"Can I wait it out at your place till morning?" I ask Lena. "I won't make it to Lenin Avenue."

Lena laughs and says, "About time." We drink tea in her kitchen until six in the morning, and for the first time since December I fall asleep with the light off.

## После D
I listen to the chill at the back of my neck and don't go through Lagerny Garden; I take the long way along the avenue, where windows are lit and the occasional car goes by. It comes out two kilometers longer. I never do look at the bench by the monument, not that night, not later.

## После E
I walk through Lagerny Garden counting my steps. Over forty below, the streetlights standing in rust-colored fog, the bench by the monument white with frost and empty. After a kilometer my feet stop feeling cold, and I know that's bad. Somewhere in another life I got warm just like this among boxes of chicks in cowboy hats, and I sat down. Here I don't sit down. I rub my ears with my mitten until they start to hurt again, and walk on.

By the bench I want to sit so badly that I stop and look at it like a dog that might bite. Then I walk past. I get to the dorm on Lenin Avenue a little after four, with white cheeks and fingers that won't bend until morning. Auntie Galya at the front desk pours me tea from her own thermos and scolds me. And the streetlights in Lagerny Garden, when I looked back at them from the edge of the bluff, weren't standing where they'd stood: they were one span further to the left.

## После F
Lyokha's dad's felt boots are two sizes too big for me, and the galoshes slap against my heels. Lyokha watches me put them on and says, "What are you, Samokha, an old man?" I carry my sneakers in a bag from the Yarche supermarket.

In the park I walk along the very edge, but the felt boots grip the ice like paws. At the turn by the bench I stop and look down for a long time at the white riverbed with steam rising off it. Then I walk on, holding on to the bushes, though I don't need to.

I get to the dorm at half past four. I can't feel my feet, but they're mine. I return the felt boots to Lyokha a week later, washed, with a bottle of Sibirskaya Korona beer in each one.
