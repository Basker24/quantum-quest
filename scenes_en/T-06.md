In July Nika comes again, for the first time since ninety-six. At first I don't recognize her. She has braces, there are Strugatsky novels in her backpack, and she calls Vitka "Viktor," which makes Vitka so mad his ears turn red. Vitka is fourteen. He smokes Yava cigarettes, shows off, and spits through his teeth like his stepdad, Uncle Tolya the cab driver.

::if F_Т01 == БУБЛИК
Bublik is mine now. Back in ninety-six Nika went home without him; she said he'd "made his choice." He's grown into a big floppy-eared ginger dog, and when he sees Nika he howls like a lunatic and rolls on his back. Nika pretends she doesn't care and scratches his belly for ten minutes.
::end

On the third day the three of us go to Black Backwater. Nobody suggested it; our feet just went that way. The backwater is just as black, and the snag still sticks up out of the water. I'm thirteen, and I'm trying as hard as I can to look like I don't care.

::if F_Т03 == ВОДОБОЯЗНЬ
For six years I haven't gone into water deeper than my knees. When I'm in the bath, the door stays open. Vitka knows and keeps his mouth shut, and for that I forgive him a lot.
::end

"So, Vodyanoy," says Vitka. "Rematch?"

Nika doesn't say anything. She's looking at the snag, and I'm looking at her and thinking she probably still remembers how high she counted.

Upriver, around the bend, the old buoy keeper's hut sticks up from the bank. Nobody has set out buoys on the Tom for years, but the buoy keeper lives there anyway. People say he's about a hundred and sees everything that happens on the water.

## После A
Nika and I swim to the snag side by side, her a little ahead. It's so cold my jaw aches. About five meters from the snag the back of my neck goes cold, and a second later my leg cramps, the whole leg, from heel to hip, like somebody's tied it in a knot. I go under with my eyes open and see white branches in the black water.

My hand finds a branch by itself. I climb up onto the snag, coughing, and hold on with both hands, and my leg sticks out stiff as a plank. Nika is treading water next to me, white in the face, and I hear her whispering, "One. Two. Three." She's counting without noticing.

"It's gone," I say at eleven. She stops counting. Five minutes later the leg lets go, and the water turns back into plain water.

We race back. Nika wins by half a length and laughs with all her braces showing, and on the shore Vitka yells that we're both nuts.

::if F_Т03 == ВОДОБОЯЗНЬ
For six years I haven't gone into water deeper than my knees. That evening, for the first time in six years, I take a bath with the door shut.
::end

## После B
We sit on a warm rock away from Vitka, and I tell her about the dark under the water. About the voices, about how crowded it was, about the counting. I talk and wait for her to laugh.

Nika doesn't laugh.

"I believe you," she says. "I'm the one who was counting."

I don't tell anybody else about it after that. Neither does she.

## После C
Vitka and I crack up over her braces and the way she says "Viktor." Nika doesn't say anything. Then she packs up her backpack and walks off alone, without looking back.

Three days later she goes home to Novosibirsk early. Vitka says, "Who cares." And I feel lousy all evening and can't figure out why.

## После D
The buoy keeper is sitting by his hut, mending a net. He looks about a hundred, and his hands are blue with tattoos. He looks me over for a long time and says:

"Every July a fellow from the city comes out here, in a suit. Fifteen years now. Stands by the backwater till first light. Doesn't fish, just looks. Lost a boy here, people say, back in eighty-eight. Never found him."

He spits in the water and doesn't say another word to me. All the way back I think about that boy from Shakhtyorskaya Street the old woman downstairs told us about.
