At the end of June, up north, near Chiang Rai, nine boys from a village football team and their coach went into the Tham Pha Noi cave. They went after practice, for an hour, and the first monsoon downpour came off the mountains, and the water closed the passage behind them. They were found on the tenth day, alive, on a rock shelf three kilometers from the entrance, beyond flooded corridors. Now they have to be brought out, one at a time, underwater.

The whole country is talking about it, and in every noodle shop on Koh Tao the TV shows the same thing: the cave entrance, soldiers, pumps. They need divers to carry tanks to the staging stations. I call the number on the screen, say in Thai that I'm an instructor, and a day later I'm standing at the entrance, knee-deep in rust-colored slurry, with my tanks.

When the screen shows a narrow crawlway, my chest goes tight, right there in the noodle shop, on a plastic stool. I go out onto the street and stand there until it lets go.
::end

::if F_В505 == ДЕРЖАЛ
Ploy sees me off at the ferry and says, "Bring them out." As if I'll be the one carrying them. Others will carry them, the best cave divers in the world, English and Australian. My job is tanks.

The tanks go to the third chamber, through the squeeze. Back in Rudnichny we had a word for the hole in the fence we crawled through for bird cherries and scraped our backs on: the skin-ripper. Here the skin-ripper is a gap under water, about ten meters long, between the ceiling and the rock, in silt so thick you can't see your own hand. A rope stretched along the bottom leads there. You hold on to it and crawl, pushing ahead of you the tank you're carrying.

::if F_В507 == СОМЧАЙ
The tanks are my own, from Chalok, from Somchai's. After the morning run to the second station my temples still ache, and spots flicker in front of my eyes. Somchai would say it's the sun. There's been no sun here for three weeks.
::end

My shoulders just fit through the gap; I tried this morning at the dry end, in the shallows. The main thing is not to jerk when the ceiling settles on your back. It settles, and you have to keep crawling.

::if ДАР >= 50
In the silt at the mouth of the squeeze there's a pull at the back of my head, a chill, toward the gap and a little to the left. I've felt that pull underwater many times. Someone who's been here before is showing me where to go.

The shift leader, a Thai marine with a face gone white from lack of sleep, points at me and at the squeeze. Three tanks. Or stay by the pumps, he says; they need people at the pumps too.

> They brought all the boys out, all nine and the coach. With me or without me, it made no difference. I couldn't change that story; I could only not die in it.

## После A
I dive into the gap, holding the rope, and the ceiling settles onto my back, as promised. I don't jerk. I crawl, pushing the tank ahead of me, centimeter by centimeter, in rust-colored silt where the flashlight only lights itself. After about five meters the ceiling lets go, and I swim out into the black water of the third chamber.

A gray-bearded Englishman takes the tanks from me. He gives me a thumbs-up and swims on, to where the boys are sitting on the rock shelf. I can't see them, only hear them. One of them is laughing.

I crawl back just as slowly. At the entrance they sit me on a crate and give me sweet tea. My hands are white and wrinkled, like Mom's after the laundry.

## После B
I stay by the pumps. The pumps drone, the hoses twitch as if alive, and I haul tanks from the truck to the water over slippery rocks, twelve hours a shift. A tank weighs fifteen kilos. By night I can't feel my shoulders.

The ones going into the squeeze pass me on their way into the water. I hand them tanks and don't look them in the eye. On the eighth day they're all brought out, one at a time, on stretchers, wrapped in foil. The crowd at the entrance applauds. I stand by the pump and applaud too, and don't know what to do with my hands when the applause stops.

## После C
I give my tanks to the marine and say there's something wrong with them. He sniffs the valve and winces. "Bad," he says, and gets me three military ones, gray. The air in them is nothing, tasteless, and my headache goes away on the second breath.

In the gap the ceiling settles on my back, and I crawl. In the middle of the gap I wait for it to turn sweet and colorful and sleepy, and nothing happens. I swim out into the third chamber. The gray-bearded Englishman takes my tanks and swims off to the shelf where the boys are sitting.

::if F_В507 == СОМЧАЙ
On the way home I call Somchai and tell him about his generator. Somchai is silent on the line for a long time, and then says, "Okay, okay," and a month later moves the air intake up onto the roof.
::end

## После D
At the mouth of the gap I take the tank off my back and push it ahead of me, arms outstretched, together with the three others. That way I'm narrower. The ceiling settles on my back, and my shoulders have somewhere to go.

::if F_В507 == СОМЧАЙ
My tanks are military ones, gray. My own, Somchai's, I gave to the marine that morning, and he sniffed the valve and said, "Bad."
::end

I crawl along the rope, centimeter by centimeter. Where last time the valve caught on a rock and held, I tilt the tank sideways, and it goes through. I swim out into the third chamber, hand the tanks to the gray-bearded Englishman and hang in the black water for a long time, holding the rope, until I start breathing like a human being.
