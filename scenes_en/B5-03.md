After Phangan I can't look at the sea at night for a year. In the daytime I can, but at night the skin on my chest starts to burn, where there's nothing on it. I don't try explaining this to anyone. I tell Alla Borisovna I'm quitting, and she says, "You idiot, you've got the face for it." And with all the money I saved on crocodiles I go to Koh Tao and sign up for a diving course.

Why, I don't understand myself. The island is small, twenty-one square kilometers, with forty dive centers and one ATM. If at night I lie in that water and die, then in the daytime I'll work in it. Mom would say I'm doing it out of spite. Maybe I am.

I passed the course in October, the second one in December, and now I'm a trainee at the Blue Crab dive center on Sairee beach. Trainee means I haul tanks, rinse gear in fresh water, take students to the instructors, and dive for free. My instructor is Ploy. She's twenty-four, Thai, from Nonthaburi, a suburb of Bangkok. So beautiful that students lose count during the briefing. Thin, with bird bones, and a meter seventy tall, taller than half the guys here. Her hair is black, straight, below her shoulder blades; before a dive she twists it into a knot and spears it with a pencil. She looks at farangs, which is what they call white people here, the way my mom looks at Sonka's boyfriends. She knows three words of Russian: "normalno," "davai" and "blin." Her "blin" comes out better than mine.

Underwater she's different. Underwater she laughs with her eyes, shows me a turtle, a parrotfish with her hands, and once, at twenty meters, just takes my hand and holds it until I stop breathing like a steam engine. That night, for the first time since Phangan, I sleep peacefully.

Tonight in the bar by the center they're drinking to a new trainee, a Swiss. They drink by local tradition, through a snorkel, and the Swiss is under the table. I've had mine, and it's time for me to go home, to Chalok, over the hill, on a rented Honda. Three kilometers. The road there is concrete, steep as the sledding slope in our yard in winter, and covered in sand.

## После A
I go down in first gear, slowly, like an old lady on a sled, pressed so close to the left shoulder that my flip-flop scrapes the concrete. At the bend headlights hit me from below, and a pickup carrying ice for the bars shoots past me right down the middle of the road. It honks for a long time, at the whole island.

I stop at the shoulder and sit on the scooter until my knees stop shaking. The pickup wouldn't have seen me if I'd been riding the usual way. It doesn't see me even now; to it I'm just a shadow on the shoulder.

In the morning Ploy looks at me across the counter and says, "Lucky farang." Nothing more.

## После B
I catch the helmet with one hand, and it's the best thing I've done on this island. Ploy gets on her scooter and waves to me: "Follow." She rides ahead, slowly, and at every bend raises her left hand like a cyclist. At that bend she stops completely, puts a foot on the ground and waits.

Headlights hit us from below. A pickup with ice for the bars shoots past us right down the middle of the road and honks for a long time.

"Every night," says Ploy, when it's gone. "Same car. Same corner."

> Back then Koh Tao lost a scooter rider a week. The island is small, and everyone knew which bend.

At my gate in Chalok she takes back the helmet, puts it on herself and says, "Seven o'clock. Don't late." And rides back over the hill, alone.

## После C
I leave the scooter at the bar and walk over the hill. The moon is out, the frogs are bellowing, hermit crabs the size of a fingernail crawl across the concrete. On the way down I step on the edge of a drain in the dark, my foot goes down, and pain shoots through my ankle so that I sit down right in the road.

Just then headlights hit me from below. A pickup with ice for the bars shoots past me right down the middle of the road and honks at me, sitting there, long and angry.

I hobble home for an hour. In the morning Ploy sees me going down the stairs to the boat sideways, laughs out loud, for the first time in front of me, and silently tapes up my foot. My foot doesn't feel any better, but I do.

## После D
At the top of the hill I switch off the scooter and sit down on the shoulder. My knees won't hold me. Down below, at the bend, there's nothing yet, only darkness and frogs.

A minute later headlights hit from below. The pickup with ice for the bars races up past me, right down the middle of the road, and honks just because, just in case. I watch it go. I remember its grille going into my chest, and I remember the sound the handlebars made breaking.

I go down on foot, wheeling the scooter beside me. In the morning Ploy asks why I have that look on my face. I say I didn't sleep well.
