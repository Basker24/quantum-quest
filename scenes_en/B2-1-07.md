For eight years I've lived in Tomsk under another name. It took six months and all the money I had to get a passport made out to Andrei Tikhonovich Savelyev. Granddad's name was Tikhon Savelyevich, and I couldn't think of anything better. I rent a room with a stove in a wooden house on Tatarskaya Street and fix watches and radios in a tin booth by the Central Market. The Choir helps: if something in a radio is about to burn out, I hear it before the owner does.

Since last winter there have been strangers' voices in the Choir. Strangers' voices, but my pitch. It's like somebody in the next stairwell playing the same tape you have, and you hear it through the wall, half a beat behind. There aren't many of them yet, and they're all far off to the west.

For all eight years I deliberately didn't look for Lunyov, so as not to lead Site Nine to him. In August a frail old lady in a beret brings an old Okean radio to my booth to be fixed. She doesn't recognize me, but I recognize her at once. Zoya Mikhailovna, our senior lab technician, the one who cried by the elevator.

"Do you remember Professor Lunyov?" she asks while I open up the radio. "He's in the care home at Kashtak now. Doesn't recognize anyone. I go to see him on Thursdays; there's nobody else."

On Thursday I go instead of her.

Lunyov lies by the window, with pines outside. He's become small, like a teenager, his glasses hanging on a cord. When he sees me, he tries to sit up.

"Tikhon Savelyevich," he says. "You've gotten old."

"I have, Vadim."

"And Mitya's down at the river." Lunyov speaks confidentially, in a low voice, so the orderly won't hear. "Measuring the water. I'll call him in a minute; he'll show you where it flows. It's flowing the wrong way, Tikhon Savelyevich. It's flowing underground."

Then he fumbles under his mattress for a long time and pulls out a stack of oilcloth-covered notebooks held with a pharmacy rubber band. The rubber band is old and snaps in his fingers.

"These are for you. I've counted it all out. Give them to Andrei, your grandson. He'll come."

I take the notebooks. They're warm from lying under him.

"I've been waiting for him," says Lunyov, and closes his eyes.

> He was eighty-five. At the funeral there were three of us: Zoya Mikhailovna, the orderly, and me, Savelyev.

## После A
I sit down on the chair by his bed and open the top notebook. The handwriting is small and even, like in a lab log. On the first page, a year, '88, and one word: "Mitya."

After that, a pencil drawing. The backwater, the snag like a hand, our bank. From the snag a dotted line runs down, under the riverbed, and Lunyov has labeled it neatly, as on a blueprint: "old adit → level −640 → T. S.'s chamber." Beside it, pressed harder: "The current. It carried him there. He's there, with him."

They never found Mitya's body because the river drew him underground, to my grandfather's chamber. I sit over that drawing until the orderly brings Lunyov his supper. I open the last notebook too, but it's nothing but columns of numbers, and I can't make sense of them.

Lunyov dies three weeks later, in his sleep, after lunch.

## После B
I put the notebooks in a bag without opening them. I sit down on the chair by the bed. Lunyov finds my hand on the blanket and holds my finger, the way little kids hold grown-ups on a bus.

Then he starts humming something, quietly, without words. I don't recognize the tune at first. They used to play it from the loudspeakers by the House of Culture in Rudnichny on Miners' Day, and Dad would sing along without knowing the words. Lunyov falls asleep in the middle of a verse, and I sit there until it gets dark.

Lunyov dies three weeks later, in his sleep, after lunch. I put the notebooks in a shoebox and set it on top of the wardrobe.

"Vadim Petrovich," I say. "What happened in '96?"

He opens his eyes. For a minute he looks at me, and I see him recognize me, truly, for the first time that day.

"Andrei," he says. "City Hospital No. 2. Two test tubes. I didn't take them for tests. In '98, during the default, I sold them. To a foreign foundation; Orpheus, it was called. Along with a copy of your grandfather's archive. With that money I hired divers every summer." He rubs his eyes, like he used to, with both hands. "I sold you, Andrei. You at seven. And I don't even know what they made of it."

Then something in his face switches off. He looks at me politely and asks whether Tikhon Savelyevich would like some tea.
