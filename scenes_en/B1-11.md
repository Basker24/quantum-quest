The coal decline really got going in the thirties, the way snow comes, a little at a time and then knee-deep all at once. Nobody needed coal anymore. The Glubokaya was the first to close, back in '26, then the others one after another. The Severnaya is still holding on; they left it for last. The mining villages around Kemerovo are emptying out like villages after a war: windows boarded up, a warehouse in the House of Culture, nobody at the bus stops. I'm a decommissioner now. For twelve years I've been going down into mines my father opened and closing them for good, sealing the shafts with concrete and letting water into the workings. It pays well, because nobody wants to do it.

In August of '44 the ground subsides in Rudnichny, behind the school. At night, without a sound, a pit three meters deep, and two garages and a poplar go down into it. The city calls us in, because under Rudnichny there are old workings from the thirties that nobody remembers anything about. I dig out the plans in the archive. On a 1939 plan an adit runs from Krasnaya Gorka under the bed of the Tom, long as a gut, to the old incline under Rudnichny. From the incline a passage goes down, and on the plan it's been cut off with scissors. Cut neatly, along a ruler.

We go down the incline, three of us, me and two young guys, Gosha and Rustam. Past the cut, the passage goes down a long way, with turns, and ends in a brick wall. In the wall is an iron door, an airtight one, with white stenciled letters: "LEVEL −640. AUTHORIZED ENTRY ONLY." Somebody bricked the wall up in a hurry once; the bricks are crooked. Rustam spends half a day breaking through them with a hammer drill.

Behind the door is a corridor, and it's flooded. The water is black, like the tea Dad drank after a shift, and it stands level with the top of the door frame. According to the plan, it's forty meters to the next door. Forty meters underwater, in the dark, along a passage nobody has seen since '86.

## После A
::if F_Т03 == ВОДОБОЯЗНЬ
My throat is clenched the whole way, and I swim more by the voices than by my arms. They lead me along the corridor the way you lead a blind man by the elbow, and at every turn one of them knows the way.

The water is ice-cold. I swim along the wall, holding a cable on hooks with one hand; the flashlight reaches half a meter and stops in the murk. At twenty meters there's an air pocket under the ceiling; I come up into it, hit my helmet on the concrete, and breathe. At forty I feel steps. I climb out of the water into a chamber, and the light on my helmet lights it all at once.

The room is about six meters by eight, dry, the walls covered in frost. A cot upholstered in leatherette, above it a ring of copper coils. A control panel with toggle switches and dials, all Soviet, all gray. On the wall, in big chalk numbers: "41." On the table, on an oilcloth, lies a thick school notebook in cellophane, held with a rubber band. On the cover, in ink: "Research topic TUNING FORK. Observation log. Facility Level 640."

I sit down on the cot and read. The handwriting is the same as on the letter "For Kolya." A ruled table: number, date, time, place, method, what was heard. "No. 1. 12.04.61. 04:12. The Ob Sea, ice hole. Water, 4 min. They sang." About forty rows. Almost all of them 04:12.

::if F_Т05 == ТЕТРАДИ
I've seen this table before, at eleven, in a box from a pair of Yugoslav boots. Back then I copied it onto the last page of my school notebook and understood nothing.

Between the tables Granddad writes in words, and I hear his voice, though I never heard it. "Everyone has a tuning fork in their head. In most people it stays silent until they die. Mine was struck in the ice hole, and since then I hear the deaths of the men I might have been. I call the voices the Choir; that's what a choirmaster is supposed to do. Kolya, I'm afraid, got it from me. On duty on the line up top: V. P. Lunyov, grad student, sharp, only smokes too much."

The last entry: "28.01.86. 04:12. The fortieth. Six men in the gallery; I can't get a single one out. They can't hear, but I hear them all. Vadim. Synchronize watches. Forty-first. Going."

By the far wall, where water from the old adit once came into the chamber and left silt, lies a child's sandal. Brown, with a one-hole buckle. I pick it up. On the insole, in ballpoint, in a mother's handwriting, the way you label shoes for summer camp: "Lunyov D." Next to it in the silt lies something light and white, and I don't look there. The old woman on the ground floor said that back in the eighties a boy from Shakhtyorskaya Street drowned near the backwater and they never found him. I found him, fifty-six years later, under the Tom, in Granddad's chamber. Dmitry. Mitka, probably. The son of that grad student who smokes too much.

The Choir stands shoulder to shoulder in this room, packed tight, like in the cage. The boy isn't among them. He's somewhere apart, still looking for which way is up.
::end

I wrap the sandal in the cellophane from the notebook and carry it up top, through forty meters of water, pressed to my chest.
